# Week 2 Making Data
This week asks **how making data also changes what can be known, who can act, and what kinds of people can exist**. Its progression is from making representations of the world, to making representations of people, to making people through those representations. The link across the readings and lectures is that a category or measurement acquires force through the institutions and practices that use it.
Following the [[Course Review Guide]], the assigned authors are **Bowker and Star** and **Ian Hacking**. [[L3 Making Data]], [[L4 Making Personal Data]], and [[L5 Making People Out of Data]] develop their concerns through air quality, administrative records, genetic data, and self-quantification.

---

## Data-making joins knowledge to power
Data begins with decisions about a phenomenon: what it is, what should be observed, which differences matter, and how observations should be recorded. [[Representation (HCE)|Representation]] means making something stand for something else. It requires [[Selection]], because a record cannot include everything, and [[Abstraction]], because a usable representation leaves much of its originating context behind. Instruments, standards, and institutions provide [[Mediation]] rather than direct, unfiltered access to reality.
A [[Perspective]] is therefore unavoidable. The relevant question is whether the representation supports the purpose for which it is being used and what its exclusions mean for affected people. More data does not abolish the need to select or interpret.
### AQI: a useful representation with a particular field of vision
In [[L3 Making Data]], [[AQI]] makes complex pollution measurements communicable through a standardized index and health categories. Producing that index requires decisions about pollutants, sensor placement, standards, calculations, and reporting scales, plus the labor of maintaining instruments and records. Schools and other institutions then use the index to guide action.
Regional air-quality reporting and the experiences of refinery fence-line communities do not necessarily concern the same pollutants, places, or timescales. A regional index may not capture a local flare or an exposure that differs block by block. Community complaints, photographs, and demands for local monitoring can make a different phenomenon visible and actionable.
This is the relationship between **counting and counting politically**: a concern represented in authoritative data is easier to put before an institution. A [[View from Above Below|view from above]] supports comparison and coordinated action; a view from below may reveal what that overview misses. Neither means all official data is false or all local observations are automatically sufficient. The analytical task is to explain what each can see and whose concerns receive institutional recognition.

## Classifications become infrastructure and reshape biographies
Before anything can be counted, someone must decide what counts as an instance of it. [[Classification (HCE)|Classification]] organizes observations into categories, but those categories can also organize social opportunities. [[Race Classification and Reclassification under Apartheid|Bowker and Star's apartheid study]] makes this connection unusually visible because assigned race determined access to residence, education, movement, and relationships.
### The tension between rigid boxes and lived social membership
The apartheid state attempted to enforce mutually exclusive legal categories. Bowker and Star contrast this **Aristotelian** model of firm boundaries with **prototypical** judgments based on resemblance, appearance, and social acceptance. Actual classification drew on unstable combinations of descent, physical appearance, community reputation, and everyday judgment. The legal system demanded categorical certainty while its practical operation continually revealed ambiguity.
Administrative documents, passbooks, officials, police, and appeal procedures made the categories consequential. The system's violence did not depend on a category being scientifically valid: institutional power could enforce it anyway. A more accurate measurement of race would not solve a system whose racial divisions were themselves instruments of domination.
### Invisibility, breakdown, and torque
Classifications become invisible when ordinary routines presuppose them. Forms, schools, housing rules, and identity documents make an earlier political decision seem like an obvious fact. To [[Naturalize and Denaturalize|denaturalize]] a category is to recover the decisions, work, and institutions that sustain its apparent naturalness.
The artificiality becomes especially visible at boundaries and moments of reclassification. Sandra Laing's exclusion from a white school exposed the conflict between ancestry, appearance, and institutional recognition. Vic Wilkinson's repeated reclassifications showed how changes in relationships and location could alter an allegedly fixed identity.
[[Torque]] names the twisting of a person's biography as it encounters a classification system that does not fit. The harm is not simply an incorrect database entry: families, schooling, work, and possibilities for living together can be reorganized around the official label. What looks like a technical exception from above can be a life-changing disruption from below.

## Personal data travels beyond the person and the original purpose
[[L4 Making Personal Data]] extends the classification problem beyond overtly coercive regimes. A [[Database]] can make records durable, retrievable, and usable for institutional action. A grade, identifier, or eligibility score can represent a person when that person is absent, sometimes becoming the required route to recognition or resources.
The university punch-card case shows students contesting this relationship. The Free Speech Movement's appropriation of the card's handling instructions challenged an institution that appeared to process people as standardized records. The issue was how administration related to students, not merely the physical storage medium.
### Abstraction, circulation, and vulnerability
[[Circulation]] occurs when records move between settings or audiences; durability allows them to travel across time as well. A record can outlast the situation it described and acquire a new meaning when a different institution uses it. Abstraction makes this movement easier, but it can hide the context necessary to interpret the record responsibly.
The guide's Coordinated Entry System and VI-SPDAT example illustrates how institutional recognition can depend on being represented as sufficiently vulnerable. People disclose personal circumstances to enter a system that scores and ranks need. The central question is what that representation enables institutions to do, what it leaves out, and how applicants' access depends on fitting its categories. This is a lecture case here, rather than a separate treatment of another author's argument.
[[Vulnerability (HCE)|Vulnerability]] therefore includes exposure to exclusion, unwanted intervention, or misrecognition through data. [[Representational Harm]] concerns how a representation depicts and positions people; an inaccurate or degrading depiction can matter even without a conventional privacy breach. Conversely, an accurate record can still become harmful in an inappropriate context.
### Why personal data is relational
[[Relationality]] complicates the idea that personal data belongs to one isolated individual. Direct-to-consumer DNA testing can reveal information about relatives who never submitted a sample. Statistical grouping can also make strangers consequential to one another: behavior attributed to a category may guide predictions about everyone placed in it.
Consent by the contributor and removal of a person's name therefore do not exhaust the ethical questions. Data can affect people through kinship, group membership, institutional association, or inference, including people who did not directly participate in its creation.

## Classifications can make new ways of being a person
[[Ian Hacking - Making Up People|Hacking]] deepens the week's argument by asking what happens when people respond to knowledge about the category into which they have been placed. His account joins five elements: **classifications, classified people, institutions, expert knowledge, and experts**. A label changes lives through this wider arrangement, not through words alone.
### The looping effect
The [[Looping Effect]] occurs when a classification changes how people are treated or understand themselves, their responses change the classified population, and those changes feed back into expert knowledge or the classification itself. People are moving targets because they can adopt, resist, reinterpret, or collectively reclaim classifications.
Hacking's multiple-personality example distinguishes asking whether a condition existed earlier from asking whether it was an available **way to be a person**. Diagnostic categories, clinical practices, and public narratives opened new possibilities for interpreting experiences and seeking treatment. This does not require concluding that suffering was fabricated. His autism example likewise examines how changing classifications and institutions made new forms of identity and collective self-understanding possible.
Bowker and Star foreground what happens when administrative categories collide with biographies; Hacking foregrounds the interaction through which categories and people change one another. **Torque and looping are related but not interchangeable**. A painful mismatch is not automatically a feedback loop, and a feedback loop need not consist only of harm.
### Self-quantification as a technique of self-making
[[L5 Making People Out of Data]] connects this argument to [[Self-Quantification]] and [[Self-Making]]. A sleep score, step target, or personality label can become a standard against which someone evaluates themselves and changes behavior. The measure moves from describing a person to helping organize that person's activities and priorities.
[[Performativity (HCE)|Performativity]] names this capacity of representations, classifications, and practices to help produce what they describe. It is broader than deliberate playacting and does not mean the represented phenomenon is unreal. In quantified self-tracking, an apparently objective number can make some forms of improvement easy to recognize while crowding out less measurable understandings of a good life.
The lecture's [[Cyborgs|cyborg]] figure captures the relational character of this self: people are formed partly through technologies, records, norms, and institutions beyond their bodies. Data is not always a detachable copy of an otherwise complete person. It can help constitute who that person is able to become.

Trace **making a representation → making it authoritative → circulating or applying it → changing people's circumstances or self-understanding**. Identify the institutions that connect each step.

1. Use AQI to distinguish necessary selection from a representation that is inadequate for a particular community or purpose.
2. Explain when apartheid's classification infrastructure became visible and how torque connects administrative categories to biography.
3. Use genetic testing or institutional records to explain why personal-data ethics extends beyond individual privacy.
4. Explain Hacking's distinction between discovering a condition and making a way to be a person, then connect the looping effect to self-tracking without treating the concepts as identical.
