# Theodore Porter
Reading (L14): *Objectivity and Authority: How French Engineers Reduced Public Utility to Numbers*. Source: [[datac104/Reading Notes/Week 6/Theodore Porter's Objectivity and Authority - How French Engineers Reduced Public Utility to Numbers|reading note]].

## Argument
Porter argues that the spread of quantification in public decisions reflects the social problem of establishing trustworthy authority. Numerical procedures create standardized, impersonal forms of communication that can travel beyond relationships of personal trust. Their apparent mechanical fairness helps administrators defend decisions against accusations of favoritism or corruption. Quantification often develops where expert authority is exposed to challenge, making it a strategy of insecure elites facing outside scrutiny.

The French *Corps des Ponts et Chaussées* illustrates this process. State engineers translated declarations of public utility into calculations, presenting infrastructure decisions as applications of rules rather than exercises of discretion. Their mathematical language was publicly credible while restricting effective criticism to specialists. Yet numerical procedures did not eliminate political judgment: competing formulations embodied different ideas about markets and the public good. Secure elites could still rely on experience and instinct, so quantified objectivity should not be equated with the inevitable triumph of technocracy.

## Supporting Examples

- **Navier's canal calculation:** Navier compared construction, maintenance, and financing costs with transport savings to justify publicly useful canals even when toll revenues alone would not make them profitable. His formula defended state investment by calculating benefits that private investors might overlook, showing how numbers could support a particular vision of public responsibility.
- **Dupuit's marginal utility and differential pricing:** Dupuit argued that users derive different benefits from transport and that utility depends on the price charged. His approach moved calculation toward representing market demand and willingness to pay. The contrast with Navier demonstrates that quantification can serve different political and economic commitments while retaining an appearance of objectivity.
