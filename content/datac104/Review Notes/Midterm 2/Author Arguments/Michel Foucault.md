# Michel Foucault
Reading (L13): "Panopticism," *Discipline and Punish*. Source: [[datac104/Reading Notes/Week 6/Michel Foucault's Discipline and Punish (Panopticism)|reading note]].

## Argument
Foucault argues that **disciplinary power** works by organizing space, visibility, records, and comparison so that individuals become observable and governable. It does more than exclude or punish: it identifies, differentiates, trains, and modifies people. The Panopticon captures this mechanism because surveillance is visible but unverifiable. A person knows they can be watched while being unable to determine whether anyone is watching at a particular moment, encouraging them to internalize the constraint and regulate their own conduct.

Power can consequently function without continuous intervention by a particular ruler or guard. Its effectiveness resides in an arrangement of bodies, architecture, observation, and knowledge. Foucault treats panopticism as a generalizable political technology that can operate in schools, hospitals, workshops, and other institutions as well as prisons. Surveillance produces knowledge about individuals while giving institutions the means to classify them and increase their usefulness, making discipline part of ordinary social organization.

## Supporting Examples

- **Bentham's Panopticon:** Isolated cells surround a central tower whose occupants cannot be seen by inmates. Each inmate is exposed to observation while being separated from fellow inmates. Because the observer's presence is uncertain, even an empty tower can sustain self-discipline, illustrating how the arrangement makes power operate automatically.
- **The plague-stricken town:** Quarantine divides the town into supervised spaces, fixes inhabitants in their homes, requires appearances at windows, and generates continuous written reports. Foucault contrasts this detailed, individualized administration with the leper's exclusion, showing how surveillance and registration build a disciplined population.
