# Mona Sloane
Reading (L24): "The Business of AI," *Predicted: How AI Is Restructuring Social Life*. Source: [[datac104/Reading Notes/Week 9/Mona Sloane - The Business of AI|reading note]].

## Argument
Sloane argues that AI must be understood through the political economy of the **prediction paradigm**: turning predictions, models, and data into proprietary assets expected to generate future income. She questions narratives that equate speculative valuations with proven productivity. Predictions have value within particular social and institutional contexts, while the business built around them depends on extracted behavioral data, precarious data work, and material infrastructure consuming energy, water, and minerals. Treating data as a naturally available resource obscures the people and institutions that produce it.

Automation also reorganizes professional discretion and social status. Labels such as high-skill and low-skill reflect institutional prestige and power as well as practical expertise, shaping whose work is considered replaceable. Systems may strengthen the authority of privileged professionals while stripping autonomy from workers in lower-status or feminized occupations. Claims of machine objectivity consequently redistribute judgment rather than removing its social basis, with consequences for identity, dignity, and the interpersonal work of understanding others.

## Supporting Examples

- **Recruiters and semantic search:** Specialized recruiters exercise craft judgment by writing Boolean queries whose logic they can inspect. Opaque semantic search disrupts the connection between their instructions and the results, prompting workarounds to regain control. The example shows automation diminishing professional agency and producing extra labor rather than straightforwardly improving efficiency.
- **Dermatologists and AI-assisted diagnosis:** Dermatologists combine AI risk scores with clinical experience and patient context, sometimes using the score to persuade a patient to accept a biopsy. Here machine authority can augment professional discretion. Sloane contrasts this with recruitment to show how status and gendered occupational hierarchies influence whether AI supports or displaces human judgment.
