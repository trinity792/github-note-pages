# David Noble
Reading (L21): *Social Choice in Machine Design: The Case of Automatically Controlled Machine Tools*. Source: [[datac104/Reading Notes/Week 8/David F. Noble - Social Choice in Machine Design The Case of Automatically Controlled Machine Tools|reading note]].

## Argument
Noble argues that machine design embodies **social choices** shaped by power, institutional sponsorship, and struggles over production. The postwar dominance of numerical control over record-playback in metalworking cannot be explained simply as the inevitable victory of the most efficient technology. Record-playback reproduced a skilled machinist's movements, preserving the worker as the source of production knowledge. Numerical control translated engineering specifications into coded instructions, promising to move planning and authority from machinists to management and office programmers.

This choice operated through both vertical relations between management and labor and horizontal relations between large firms and smaller shops. Managers sought predictable output, reduced worker autonomy, and less union leverage, while military funding and standards favored large defense manufacturers. Noble also distinguishes intended control from actual outcomes: early numerical-control equipment still depended on skilled machinists to handle breakdowns and protect production quality. Automation's social consequences therefore emerge through contested implementation as well as design.

---

## Supporting Examples

- **Management's response to record-playback:** The reading describes executives rejecting a technically successful system because it reproduced what machinists did rather than enforcing what management wanted them to do. This response reveals that the location of control mattered beyond repeatability or machining accuracy.
- **Air Force funding and APT standardization:** Military subsidies, procurement, and support for the complex Automatically Programmed Tools language helped make numerical control the dominant path. Its demanding hardware and programming requirements suited large aircraft contractors while disadvantaging small shops. Institutional backing thus shaped which technology became standard and which firms could use it.
