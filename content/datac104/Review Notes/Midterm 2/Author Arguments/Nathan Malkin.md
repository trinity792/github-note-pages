# Nathan Malkin
Reading (L11): *Contextual Integrity, Explained: A More Usable Privacy Definition*. Source: [[datac104/Reading Notes/Week 5/Helen Nissenbaum’s Theory of Contextual Integrity|reading note on contextual integrity]].

## Argument
Malkin explains Helen Nissenbaum's **contextual integrity** framework: privacy consists in appropriate information flows within a social context. Secrecy, the public/private distinction, legal compliance, and consent checkboxes cannot by themselves explain why a data practice violates privacy. People willingly share medical information with doctors, for example, while expecting limits on its circulation. A flow must be specified through five parameters: data type, data subject, sender, recipient, and transmission principle, meaning the conditions governing transmission. Changing a parameter can violate the contextual norms that made sharing acceptable.

Established expectations are a starting point for ethical judgment, rather than an unquestionable standard. Novel flows should be evaluated through affected parties' interests, ethical and political values, and the purposes of the context itself, such as patient care or student learning. This also permits criticism of established norms that perpetuate inequality. For computing, the practical lesson is to investigate the complete context and the consequences of a flow, including sensitive inferences made from apparently ordinary data.

## Supporting Examples

- **Smart-speaker recordings:** Users may expect voice commands to be processed by a service to answer requests, yet object when human contractors listen to recordings or when the company uses them for advertising. The data may be similar, but the recipient, actor's role, or conditions of transmission have changed, explaining why the new flow can breach privacy expectations.
- **Target's pregnancy inference:** The reading note describes a retailer inferring pregnancy from shopping habits. Permission to process purchases does not automatically make the resulting health inference appropriate. Contextual integrity requires evaluating the inferred information and its circulation on their own terms.
