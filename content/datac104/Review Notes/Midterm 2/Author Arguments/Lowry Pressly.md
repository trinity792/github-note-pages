# Lowry Pressly
Reading (L12): Introduction to *The Right to Oblivion: Privacy and the Good Life*. Source: [[datac104/Reading Notes/Week 5/The Right to Oblivion - Privacy and the Good Life|reading note]].

## Argument
Pressly challenges the **ideology of information**, the assumption that more information and self-knowledge necessarily improve human life. Defining privacy as control over existing information already concedes that life should be converted into data. Privacy settings may restrict other users' access while leaving the platform's initial extraction intact. Privacy's deeper value therefore includes protecting against information being created in the first place, preserving regions of life that remain unrecorded and undetermined.

He calls this condition **oblivion**: an openness and unknowing that support imagination, trust, personal change, and ease of existence. It differs from secrecy, which protects a definite fact already known to someone, and from hiding, which keeps a person anxiously oriented toward a possible observer. Privacy lets people detach from that gaze and from their documented identities. Its value exceeds a transaction in which individuals exchange data for convenience; a good social life depends on limits to what is made knowable.

---

## Supporting Examples

- **Everyday deliberate obliviousness:** When someone witnesses an embarrassing moment and looks away or acts as though it never happened, privacy is supported even though both people know what occurred. The example shows that respecting privacy can require a social practice of leaving something alone, beyond controlling possession of a fact.
- **Borges's *Funes the Memorious*:** Pressly reads the story of a person who remembers everything as a warning about exhaustive knowledge. Without forgetting and generalization, thought and imagination become impaired. Oblivion is therefore a condition of meaningful mental life rather than merely a failure to retain information.
