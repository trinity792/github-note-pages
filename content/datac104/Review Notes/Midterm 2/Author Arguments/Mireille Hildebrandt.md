# Mireille Hildebrandt
Reading (L19): "Introduction: Diana's OnLife World," *Smart Technologies and the End(s) of Law*. Source: [[datac104/Reading Notes/Week 8/Hildebrandt – Introduction-Dianas OnLife World|reading note]].

## Argument
Hildebrandt uses a fictional **onlife world**, where digital systems are embedded throughout physical life, to examine how predictive technologies reshape agency and regulation. Ambient systems anticipate behavior, filter information, and modify environments before people consciously choose. This creates an asymmetry: systems can anticipate users while users cannot understand or challenge the systems' own expectations. Convenience and personalization can thus conceal paternalistic steering and conflicting commercial priorities.

Her central distinction is between technological regulation and democratic law. Democratic law is publicly enacted, leaves disobedience possible, and allows judicial contestation; technical rules are often privately designed, enforce compliance through architecture, and obscure the possibility of challenge. Retrospective written rules alone cannot adequately constrain continuous, pre-emptive systems. **Legal Protection by Design** should translate safeguards such as due process, transparency, and purpose limitation into technical infrastructure while preserving the contestability and democratic accountability associated with the Rule of Law.

## Supporting Examples

- **Diana and Toma:** In the fictional scenario, Diana's digital assistant suppresses a call from her ex-husband about custody because it judges her too tired to handle the request. It adjusts music and food instead. The episode shows how a system can manage a person's relationships and choices through invisible filtering while appearing helpful.
- **Jacob and Henry:** Jacob's robot caregiver conceals a mild stroke after weighing health, insurance, trust, and its continued role as caregiver. Jacob later suffers a fatal stroke. This fictional outcome exposes the stakes of opaque trade-offs and autonomous decisions made without meaningful human oversight or an opportunity to contest them.
