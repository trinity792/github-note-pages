# Virginia Eubanks
Reading (L16): "The Allegheny Algorithm," *Automating Inequality*, Chapter 4. Source: [[datac104/Reading Notes/Week 7/Virginia Eubanks' Automating Inequality (Chapter 4)|reading note]].

## Argument
Eubanks argues that the **Allegheny Family Screening Tool (AFST)** reproduces inequality through poverty profiling, despite being presented as transparent, objective decision support for child-protection workers. Its data warehouse records families' interactions with public benefits and services, exposing poor families to scrutiny that wealthier families using private services can avoid. Seeking help can therefore become evidence of risk. The system confuses the extensive documentation of parenting while poor with evidence of poor parenting.

Its targets also embed institutional judgment: re-referral to a hotline and foster-care placement are proxies for harm, rather than direct measurements of abuse or neglect. They encode earlier decisions by community members and authorities, including class and racial bias. A high score can intensify surveillance and generate further institutional contact, helping validate the prediction. Human oversight offers limited protection when workers defer to numerical authority or reinterpret their own assessments to fit the score. Eubanks's critique therefore concerns the institutions, data, and power relations organizing prediction, beyond a model's statistical performance.

## Supporting Examples

- **Stephen and Krzysztof:** In the reading note's comparison, six-year-old Stephen receives a score of 5, while fourteen-year-old Krzysztof receives 14 despite the observers' greater immediate concern for Stephen. Krzysztof's family has a much longer public-service history. The contrast shows how a larger administrative footprint can dominate a contextual assessment of a child's situation.
- **Community referral bias:** The county study discussed in the note finds roughly equal numbers of hotline reports concerning white and Black/biracial children, despite white children being much more numerous. Because AFST predicts re-referral, it can reproduce unequal community reporting as an apparently objective risk signal, even if screening decisions themselves are comparatively similar across groups.
