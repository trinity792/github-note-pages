# Baumol's Cost Disease
Lecture: L21. Sources: [[datac104/Lecture Notes/L21 Automation and Labor|L21: Baumol's cost disease]]; [[datac104/Reading Notes/Week 9/Mona Sloane - The Business of AI|Sloane reading note: professional discretion and connective labor]].

## Case and Significance
What?: L21 uses **Baumol's cost disease** to explain why labor-intensive services can become more expensive relative to manufactured goods. Automation can rapidly increase output per worker in sectors such as electronics, while activities involving care, teaching, or interpersonal attention often have less scope for comparable productivity gains. Service employers still compete for workers, so wages can rise even where output per worker changes little.

Implication: The case complicates the assumption that every activity can become cheaper simply by substituting technology for labor. In some services, the time and relationship between people are part of the valued activity. Reducing that time may alter quality rather than produce the same service more efficiently. Sloane's discussion of professional discretion and connective labor helps explain why assessing automation requires attention to what an institution is trying to accomplish, beyond a count of completed tasks.

---

## Key Details

- The lecture contrasts falling costs in high-productivity manufacturing with increasing relative costs in healthcare, higher education, and care work.
- The mechanism is uneven productivity combined with competition for labor. It does not require concluding that service workers are inefficient or that a higher price proves a technological failure.
