# Silicon Valley Company Sequence
Lecture: L22. Sources: [[datac104/Lecture Notes/L22 Making Silicon Valley I|L22: firms and the semiconductor industry]]; [[datac104/Reading Notes/Week 9/Tom Nicholas The Emergence of Silicon Valley|Nicholas reading note: mobility and regional institutions]].

## Case and Significance
The table's company sequence brings together **Hewlett-Packard, Shockley Semiconductor, Fairchild Semiconductor, and Intel**. Hewlett and Packard's work under Terman's influence exemplifies Stanford-linked electronics entrepreneurship. Shockley later brought semiconductor expertise from Bell Labs to the region. Eight employees left his firm to form Fairchild, and people leaving Fairchild subsequently founded Intel. Hewlett-Packard belongs to the broader regional story; it is not presented as the parent of the semiconductor spinoff chain.

The case illustrates cumulative networks of technical labor, finance, and markets. A firm's failure or internal conflict could release expertise into new enterprises while keeping people and capabilities within the region. Military customers supported difficult, expensive early integrated-circuit production, helping establish the conditions for later markets. The famous succession of founders and firms therefore rests on institutional resources and public demand as well as entrepreneurial initiative.

## Key Details

- Fairchild's formation after conflict at Shockley's company shows how mobile technical workers and financing could turn an organizational rupture into a new enterprise.
- Intel, founded by people who left Fairchild, extends the spinoff pattern. Nicholas's discussion of California's limits on noncompetes helps explain the legal setting supporting mobility.
