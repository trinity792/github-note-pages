# Havasupai Blood Samples
Lecture: L12. Sources: [[datac104/Lecture Notes/L12 Privacy in the World|L12: Havasupai blood samples]]; [[datac104/Reading Notes/Week 5/Helen Nissenbaum’s Theory of Contextual Integrity|Malkin reading note: contextual integrity]]; [[datac104/Review Notes/Week 5 – Privacy|Week 5 review: collective tribal sovereignty]].

## Case and Significance
In 1990, Havasupai tribal members supplied blood samples to Arizona State University researchers for research on diabetes. Researchers subsequently used the samples for other genetic studies, including mental illness and geographic origins. The lecture describes these additional purposes as outside the original consent and explains that the conflict involved the tribe's relationships, history, and collective identity as well as the information obtained from individual samples.

The case shows why **individual consent cannot exhaust collective privacy and sovereignty**. Genetic findings about one person can implicate a community, and control over biological materials raises questions of self-determination and institutional authority. Contextual integrity helps identify the shift from a diabetes-research relationship to other purposes, while the demand for the samples' return emphasizes that these were material objects embedded in social and historical relationships.

---

## Key Details

- The tribe excluded the researchers from tribal land and pursued litigation. The lecture records a settlement involving payment and the physical return of the blood samples.
- The Week 5 material connects origin research to conflict with the tribe's own account of its origins. The harm therefore extended beyond disclosure of an identifiable individual's medical information.
