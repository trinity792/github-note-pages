# Scientific Management
Lecture: L21. Sources: [[datac104/Lecture Notes/L21 Automation and Labor|L21: scientific management]]; [[datac104/Reading Notes/Week 8/David F. Noble - Social Choice in Machine Design The Case of Automatically Controlled Machine Tools|Noble reading note: pacing and control]]; [[datac104/Reading Notes/Week 9/Kate Crawford - The Atlas of AI (Chapter 2 Labor)|Crawford reading note: industrial discipline and AI]].

## Case and Significance
What?: breaks craft work into measured, standardized motions. Time studies and managerial procedures seek to identify and impose an approved method and pace. L21 emphasizes that this is a redistribution of knowledge and authority: expertise formerly held by workers is formalized in management's instructions.

Implication: The case connects early industrial discipline to later automation and algorithmic management. Workers' pacing can protect health, wages, and autonomy, while management may treat it as a barrier to output. Noble shows numerical control promising to extend managerial command, and Crawford traces digital tracking to older attempts to measure workers' bodies and time. Efficiency claims must therefore be assessed alongside control over production and the human costs of the required pace.

---

## Key Details

- The notes describe systematic soldiering or pacing as workers' regulation of cutting speeds and output. Taylorism targeted that discretion rather than merely supplying a better tool.
- Noble's account also limits the story of total control: unreliable machinery still required skilled machinists. Management's intention to deskill work did not automatically determine shop-floor reality.
