# U.S. Privacy Regulations (1970s)
Lecture: L11. Sources: [[datac104/Review Notes/Week 5 – Privacy|Week 5 review: historical development]]; [[datac104/Lecture Notes/L12 Privacy in the World|L12: the inherited privacy framework]].

## Case and Significance
The course presents 1970s U.S. privacy regulation as a response to fears about computerized administrative records and centralized government dossiers. The Privacy Act of 1974 addressed federal recordkeeping, while Fair Information Practice Principles (FIPPs), associated in the notes with the 1973 HEW report, framed privacy around notice, consent, participation, information security, and accountability. These developments made individual control over personal information a central model of privacy.

The case illustrates **co-production**: privacy's legal meaning developed with particular technologies, institutions, and political fears. L12 argues that this inherited framework can miss changed purposes, collective harms, dignity, and concentrations of power in contemporary data systems. A practice may satisfy notice and security procedures while violating the relationships or expectations that made the original disclosure acceptable.

---

## Key Details

- The notes distinguish the federal Privacy Act from the wider sectoral and self-regulatory approach to commercial information. The 1970s did not produce a single comprehensive framework covering all U.S. data practices.
- L12 groups the inherited framework into consent, security, and enforcement, then uses secondary data uses and machine-learning training to show why procedural permission alone can be inadequate.
