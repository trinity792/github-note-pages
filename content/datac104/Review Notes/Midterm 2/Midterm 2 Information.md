## Weekly Summaries
### Week 5
Week 5 argues that privacy is not natural but a historically contingent sociotechnical construct, co-produced with law and technology. Its two sources are Warren and Brandeis's 1890 "right to be let alone," a reaction to snapshot photography and yellow journalism that protected elite domestic life, and the 1970s panic over computerized government databases, which produced the Privacy Act (1974), the Fair Information Practice Principles (FIPPs), PII, and a sectoral, self-regulated US regime policed by the FTC. These tools are weak in the datafied world: de-identification fails (Sweeney's 87 percent, the Netflix Prize), differential privacy trades accuracy for protection (the 2020 Census), and notice-and-consent and terms of service cannot capture collective or relational harm. Lecture 12's cases show compliance coexisting with harm: Havasupai blood samples (tribal sovereignty), Crisis Text Line and Loris.ai (commercializing distress), and TSA body scanners (binary gender norms). Malkin's account of Nissenbaum's contextual integrity replaces procedure with appropriate information flows, judged by five parameters (data type, data subject, sender, recipient, transmission principle), though contested norms make it hard to legislate. Pressly goes further: privacy protects "oblivion" and guards against creating information at all, which points toward data minimization and sometimes not making data.
### Week 6
Week 6 examines how surveillance, quantification, and decision frameworks make social order. Lecture 13 traces surveillance from 1984 and Bentham's Panopticon to post-9/11 data analytics; Foucault's Panopticon works because surveillance is visible but unverifiable, so subjects internalize discipline, while datafication adds differentiating precision, state-corporate partnerships, and differential exposure (Snowden, Flock and NYPD surveillance, border towers). Lecture 14 follows Porter in treating quantification as a rhetorical "technology of distance" that builds trust among strangers. Mechanical objectivity is a "weapon of the weak," adopted by exposed elites like the French Ponts et Chaussées engineers to defend decisions against charges of favoritism, and metrics such as US News rankings are performative because institutions reshape themselves to score well. Lecture 15 historicizes "decision-making" as a postwar product of cybernetics, behaviorism, game theory, and rational choice, which now underlies choice architecture, nudges, libertarian paternalism, and algorithmic hypernudges. Power works as second-order decision-making by setting menus and defaults, as in the Facebook turnout experiment, so people keep the feeling of choice while their options are engineered.
### Week 7
Week 7 moves from "algorithmic bias" to a structural critique of automated decision-making (ADM). In COMPAS, ProPublica's error-rate parity and Northpointe's calibration parity are mathematically incompatible when base rates differ, so fairness is contextual and political, and ground truth and proxies (arrests standing in for crime) are institutional constructions. Lecture 17 shows harms beyond accuracy: predictions are performative, agency is foreclosed, and human-in-the-loop oversight erodes through automation bias. The Ofqual grading algorithm was unfair even if it matched historical school performance, and Eubanks shows the Allegheny Family Screening Tool profiling poverty by predicting re-referrals and placements rather than abuse, then creating self-fulfilling feedback loops. Lecture 18 historicizes fairness: actuarial fairness, which makes individuals pay for their own risk (the unisex insurance debates), was co-produced with the shift from the collectivizing welfare state to neoliberalism and the prediction paradigm. Fourcade and Healy extend this with classification situations, which are postdemographic, opaque, and ordinal, and with eigencapital (embodied, objectified, institutionalized, as in the credit score), which sorts people in a panoptic "ordinal society."
### Week 8
Week 8 shows that the future is imagined, and contested, in the present through narratives. Lecture 19 introduces futuring as more than prediction: narrative templates (1984, utopia and dystopia, The Matrix) and Afrofuturism shape what futures seem possible, and Hildebrandt's "Diana's onlife world" is scenario-based futuring that exposes how pre-emptive, ambient technology replaces law's democratic enactment and contestability, which she answers with legal protection by design. Lecture 20 treats AI as a future-tense myth built on three templates (mastery or extermination, fusion, companionship) and on the doomer and accelerationist poles, which both make AI the turning point of history. Existential risk, longtermism, and transhumanism are shaped by elite positionality and class politics, as in 2001 and the immortality fantasies of Silicon Valley figures like Marc Andreessen. Le Guin's carrier bag narrative offers an alternative to the hero-spear "killer story." Lecture 21 reframes automation as a sociotechnical process and social relation, with technology as capital, from the Luddites and the Machinery Question through Babbage's Difference Engine and Taylor's scientific management to Baumol's cost disease. Noble's Numerical Control beat record-playback because management and the Air Force wanted control over machinists, not because it was more efficient.
