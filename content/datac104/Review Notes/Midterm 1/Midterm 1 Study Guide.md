# Midterm 1 Study Guide
## Exam Format

The midterm will be held on Thursday, September 24 at 7 PM. Please arrive early so we can begin promptly. A bCourses announcement with room assignments will be released on Monday.

The midterm covers all materials from weeks 1 through 4 (from “Foundations” to “How Was the World Datafied?”). It consists of two short essay questions, each graded out of 10 points.

The questions are designed to test your understanding of the course’s key arguments and concepts. Some questions ask you to take arguments and concepts from one part of the course and apply them to case studies and examples from another. Other questions ask you to explain arguments or concepts in greater depth. Knowing facts is important, and so is understanding.

A well prepared student will come to the exam with strong knowledge of all lectures and readings. This means studying 1) their main arguments, 2) their key concepts and vocabulary, and 3) their case studies and examples. The student will also know the ideas in the HCE toolkit.

You will have 60 minutes to complete the exam. We recommend using 30 minutes per answer. DSP students will receive accommodations in accordance with their DSP letter. Please reach out to the course coordinator ([datac104@berkeley.edu](mailto:datac104@berkeley.edu)) if you have not been contacted about midterm accommodations.

The exam is in-person and closed book. You need to bring a pen or pencil, and backups. A blue book is not required.

## Sample Exam Question and Answer

The following question and answer are provided as models for your own answer. You should note how it explains concepts, presents evidence, makes arguments, and follows a clear organization. In practice, real answers will be less polished, and may be longer or shorter.

### Sample Question: 
Based on his argument in “Do Artifacts Have Politics,” how would Langdon Winner analyze the racial passbooks and race tests used by Apartheid South Africa?

### Sample Answer: 
In “Do Artifacts Have Politics,” Langdon Winner seeks to explain how technologies (“artifacts”) can have political qualities. For Winner, “politics” means the ability to shape society, not just in elections or political parties, but in power and “social order.” One example was the bridges in New York that were so low they stopped buses (and therefore the poor) from traveling to the beach. Another was factory automation that eliminated skilled workers and undermined the power of the labor union. Still another was nuclear power, which unlike solar power seems to depend on strong forms of political authority to prevent disastrous meltdowns. Some of these are political in their specific context, like bridges in New York or automation in a fight with the union, and others seem to inherently affect any society that has them, like nuclear power. But the overall point is that technology affects what is possible in society and what is required in society, how power and authority functions in society, and who has that power.

I believe Winner would see the passbooks and racial tests of Apartheid South Africa as deeply bound up with the white supremacy of that society. Apartheid South Africa was built around strict divisions between racial groups (white, indian/asian, colored, and black) and domination of whites over other groups. The passbooks and racial tests were a necessary part of this power structure because they determined what group a person belonged to and where they could legally live or work. The tests were very invasive and included things like appearance, background, or even how curly your hair was (the “pencil test”). People were also judged based on their friends and family, and some people changed associations in order to pass for another race. The passbooks were especially discriminatory because only black people were required to carry them, and if they lost even a few pages they could be put in jail. The passbooks contained everything in a person’s life: jobs, residence, marriage, and more, and basically kept track of people as if they were a threat to society.

While some people might say these racial technologies were only important in the South African context, so they are contextually political, I believe these technologies are inherently political, because any society that is so concerned with “scientifically” testing people’s race and keeping track of them is going to be a deeply racist society based on domination. First, the passbooks and tests required a strong hierarchy to enforce them. The passbooks were deeply resented, and the racial tests often contradicted people’s own lived experiences of their identity. Had the government been any less powerful, no one would have submitted to the evaluations and surveillance necessary to collect the data that was needed for them to “function.” Furthermore, like nuclear power, these tests and passbooks change the society they exist in, making it a society based around racial control. The desire to test and classify races is always going to be “strongly compatible” with separating those races or making hierarchies between them. Ultimately, the tests and passbooks were a necessary part of the infrastructure of apartheid, and would inherently push any society that used them toward apartheid-style thinking.

### Discussion: 
This is a strong answer and would receive a high grade. We do not expect you to achieve this exact style and polished language in just 30 minutes of (very rushed) outlining and writing. Nevertheless, it is a good model for your work. A few things stand out.

#### Organization: 
When answering a question, ask yourself what the question is really asking for, and use that knowledge to organize your answer. This question essentially asks for three things: 1. Langdon Winner’s argument, 2. the Apartheid South Africa case study, 3. Winner’s likely analysis of South Africa (aka the connection between them). Because the question logically requires three things, the student answered in three sections. This is not a hard and fast rule, but it’s a good starting point. (A less good answer might have defined Winner’s ideas in the middle of discussing Apartheid, or worse, forgotten to discuss his ideas at all.)

Clear explanation of course concepts and arguments: Your answer should always show understanding of the main arguments in lectures and readings. A-level answers will be able to describe arguments in detail, with clear definitions, and with awareness of important distinctions. The student understood that Winner’s goal was to explain how technology can be political. They also showed an understanding of his nuances, like his idea of “politics” as extending beyond parties and elections, and his distinctions, like between contextually political and inherently political. (A less good answer would have simply offered some of his examples without any explanation of them. Show your understanding.)

Clear explanation of examples and case studies: The student gave a very clear explanation of the tests and passbooks. This includes the broader South African context, which is relevant for this question. Similarly, they offered brief but useful examples of the ways technologies can be political (bridges, nuclear power). (A less good answer would have failed to explain or contextualize the passbooks, acting as if the reader already knows what they’re talking about. Show what you know.)

#### Argument: 
The student's argument is very clear and logical throughout. The question asked the student to analyze South Africa using Winner’s way of thinking, so their answer continually makes references to and applies Winner’s concepts: Winner talks about politics, so they talk about politics. Winner divides technology into “contextually political” and “inherently political,” so they structure their argument around that distinction. Winner talks about nuclear weapons, so they use that analogy (“like nuclear weapons”). Winner uses the standards of “requirement” and “strong compatibility,” so they use them too. Most fundamentally, their argument is persuasive, relevant, and shows understanding. (A less good answer would describe Winner’s ideas but then fail to really employ them. Alternatively, it would present its opinion without explanation or justifications. Show your thinking.)

## Study Tips

### For Lectures:

- Attend all lectures and take notes.
- After lecture, begin with the key takeaways listed at the end of the slide decks, and then review your notes with these takeaways in mind.
- Be cautious about looking up key terms on Wikipedia or with AI, as the definitions might not correspond to how the term was used in class. If you describe aspects of a case study or key term that were not discussed in the class at the expense of demonstrating your understanding of actual course ideas, you’ll be wasting time.
- Check your understanding against the [Course Review Document](https://www.google.com/url?q=https://docs.google.com/document/d/13XwoM-O9v1sVxWMUcu0yq1z2YLoL3Ra0ZZbS59TQR1o/edit?tab%3Dt.0%23heading%3Dh.g7ao1uo2am6c&sa=D&source=editors&ust=1789949285672109&usg=AOvVaw2H51rpihkxYXWeavFdKLM_)[.](https://www.google.com/url?q=https://docs.google.com/document/d/19QIxKeTiHw2oFnlCy2J2BsNlmSbAD2znJmlGYMlJpYY/edit?tab%3Dt.0%23heading%3Dh.lfdxv8orbtwl&sa=D&source=editors&ust=1789949285672303&usg=AOvVaw0XhGZ_GpU9e6YRbtHqItde) 

### For Readings:

- Read and take notes in your reader or in a separate document.
- Ask yourself, what were the most important arguments and concepts in the readings?
- Which elements of readings were emphasized in your discussion section?
- How did the readings relate to the lectures and lecture arguments?

Review the [HCE Toolkit](https://www.google.com/url?q=https://bcourses.berkeley.edu/courses/1557692/pages/hce-resources&sa=D&source=editors&ust=1789949285673389&usg=AOvVaw1hSyD3M334NnBd0FxbCISj): Are you able to analyze the course’s case studies using these tools?

Practice connecting different parts of the course:

- Draw concept maps that link one key concept or case study to other concepts in the class (learn more by attending Professor Edmundson’s exam review workshop)
- Apply an argument from the reading or an HCE tool to one of the case studies from lecture or the readings.

Write your own sample questions and practice answering them.

Always approach a question by asking yourself: What is the question asking for? What elements from reading, what concepts, what case studies, and so on. What would be a logical way to organize those materials in a clear order? And what is my overall argument or answer?

Once you know the basics of your answer, approach each paragraph and each element–a key concept, an HCE tool, a lecture argument, a case study, etc–with a desire to show understanding. Rather than assuming “the reader/grader will know what I mean,” actually explain yourself and your ideas. Explain concepts, define terms, make distinctions, provide examples, give context, and so on. This shows you have been paying attention.
