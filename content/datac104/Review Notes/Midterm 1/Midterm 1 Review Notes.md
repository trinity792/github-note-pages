## Week 1 - Foundations
### Do Artifacts Have Politics?
- Winner argues that physical artifacts, machines, and sociotechnical systems cannot be judged merely for their efficiency, productivity, or environmental side effects. Instead, they must be evaluated for the ways in which they actively embody, enforce, and maintain specific forms of power, authority, and systematic inequality.
- Two Modes of Political Artifacts:
	- Category 1 (Technical Arrangements as Forms of Order): Highly flexible technologies where a specific physical design or arrangement is chosen to settle a social or political issue in a community.
		- Examples: 
			- Cyrus McCormick II's Pneumatic Molding Machines -> Union busting
			- The UC Davis Mechanical Tomato Harvester -> loss of 32,000 farm workers
	- Category 2 (Inherently Political Technologies): Inflexible, intractable technologies that functionally require, or are strongly compatible with, specific social and political structures as their operating environment.
		- Engels argues that modern industrial systems/technologies requires a rigid hierarchy of one with authority exercising it on their subordinates. He argued that this hierarchy was merely a practical necessity independent of social organization
		- Plato uses a captain of a chip to justify non-democratic state rule. He argues that like with modern technologies, a single divisive authority is necessary.
		- Marx believed that industrial technology itself contained latent liberating conditions that would facilitate a proletariat revolution. Under capitalism industrial technology is exploitative: converting the worker into a "living appendage of the machine" and reproducing hierarchical divisions of labor. At a technical level Marx believed that the technical capacity would eventually render the hierarchical division of labor obsolete 
### The Datafied World
- the datafied world as a stack of data, analytics, algorithms, and AI or machine learning systems, emphasizing that the upper layers depend on ubiquitous data.
	- The main case concerned rental-pricing algorithms. These systems combine property, tenant, rental-history, and market data to recommend prices intended to maximize predicted landlord revenue.
- The lecture characterized social media as a datafied business model. Meta's ecosystem includes Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, Messenger, and Threads. Its platforms collect data about users and use that information to deliver targeted content and advertising. The instructor stated that advertising accounts for about 95 percent of Meta's revenue.
- The presence of datafied devices can change social relationships even when their technical capabilities are not actively demonstrated. The instructor used AI-enabled glasses and the course prohibition on unauthorized recording to illustrate this point. Because an instructor may be unable to determine whether someone is recording, the possibility of recording changes the relationship between instructor, students, and the potential wider audience.
- California subsequently enacted Assembly Bill 325, effective in January of the lecture year. According to the lecture, it did not ban pricing software itself but prohibited using data from outside an individual landlord's own data silo.
- The California approach distinguished between using an algorithm on a company's internal information and sharing information across otherwise separate companies. The shared-data arrangement was treated as the central source of potential collusion.
- Power is visible in the asymmetry between renters and landlords. Renters may have to surrender personal data to obtain housing, while landlords control access to pricing tools and use the resulting recommendations.
- A sociotechnical analysis treats the algorithm as only one component of a larger arrangement. The full system includes:
	- It includes historical property and market data.
	- It includes tenants and the information required from them.
	- It includes landlords and property managers.
	- It includes software companies and data scientists.
	- It includes property-management platforms and related business practices.
	- It includes market conditions, financial incentives, and investment.
	- It includes journalists, regulators, courts, legislatures, and local governments.
	- It includes the narratives and values through which the technology is interpreted.
- A technological-determinist narrative treats advances in data, algorithms, and computing as the main force driving social outcomes. As technology becomes stronger, resistance appears increasingly difficult, and people are left primarily with the task of mitigating its effects.
- An opposing deterministic narrative treats outcomes as entirely social and views technology as only the form through which preexisting social relationships are expressed.

# Key Terms from Slides
## Lecture 2: Making the Datafied World 2

### 1. Datafied World

- **Definition**: A world in which fundamental aspects of human life—including housing, transportation, healthcare, speech, location, and social interactions—are systematically converted into digital data, creating interconnected layers of ubiquitous data, analytics, algorithms, and autonomous agents that structure modern social order (`Lecture 2, slides 2–5`).
- **Case Context**: Illustrated by algorithmic rental pricing software (e.g., RealPage's YieldStar), where private housing markets are transformed into a datafied domain governed by automated price-setting tools (`Lecture 2, slides 3–5`).

### 2. Technological Determinism

- **Definition**: The reductive belief that technology develops independently according to its own internal logic and directly dictates social, political, and economic outcomes (`Lecture 2, slide 5`).
- **Differentiating Contexts**:
    - _Technological Determinism:_ Assumes technology is an autonomous cause that shapes passive human societies (`Lecture 2, slide 5`).
    - _Sociotechnical Framework (Winner / HCE):_ Rejects determinism, arguing that artifacts and technical systems are inherently political, built out of human choices, and intertwined with social power and institutional structures (`Winner p. 121–122`; `Lecture 2, slide 5`).

### 3. Everydayness

- **Definition**: The social process through which technological systems and data collection integrate seamlessly into the routine background of daily life (e.g., smart home devices, credit card transactions, location tracking) until they become naturalized and taken for granted as part of the physical and social landscape (`Lecture 2, slide 6`).

### 4. World-Making

- **Definition**: The capacity of technical systems, data, and classifications to actively construct new social orders, legal regimes, economic arrangements, and cultural meanings rather than merely describing an existing reality (`Lecture 2, slide 7`).
- **Differentiating Contexts**:
    - _World-Making:_ Focuses broadly on how data infrastructures build macro-level social order and institutional realities (`Lecture 2, slide 7`).
    - _Performativity (Hacking / Butler):_ Specifically focuses on how classifications and descriptions create new ways of being a human self or social identity (`Hacking p. 65–66`; `Lecture 5, slides 3–5`).
## Lecture 3: Making Data

### 5. Air Quality Index (AQI)

- **Definition**: A standardized quantitative indicator that aggregates measurements of multiple air pollutants (e.g., ozone, fine particulate matter PM2.5, hydrogen sulfide) into a single numeric scale to communicate health risks to the public (`Lecture 3, slides 2, 8`).
- **Case Context**: In the Bay Area Air Quality Management District (BAAQMD) case study, the AQI acts as an authoritative, selective representation that decides what "counts" as air pollution, shaping regulatory enforcement and public perception (`Lecture 3, slides 2–5`).

### 6. View from Above / View from Below

- **Definitions & Differentiation**:
    - **View from Above**: The synoptic, standardized, and aggregated perspective of the state, corporate executives, or centralized administrative bodies that abstracts away local messiness to manage populations and resources from a distance (`Lecture 3, slide 8`; `Scott p. 11`).
    - **View from Below**: The grounded, localized, experiential, and contextual perspective of affected communities (e.g., residents living near oil refineries or Cumbrian sheep farmers) whose lived realities often contest or reveal the blind spots of centralized administrative representations (`Lecture 3, slide 8`; `Collins & Pinch p. 117–118`).

### 7. Selection

- **Definition**: The inevitable choice made during data creation regarding which real-world phenomena, variables, instruments, locations, and timeframes to capture and feature, and which to leave out (`Lecture 3, slide 4`).

### 8. Abstraction

- **Definition**: The process of decontextualizing complex real-world phenomena, places, or individuals by converting them into standardized, simplified units or data points (`Lecture 3, slide 4`; `Scott p. 11`).
- **Differentiating Contexts**:
    - _In Data Creation (Lecture 3 & 4):_ Stripping away local context so data can circulate across different systems (`Lecture 4, slide 5`).
    - _In Statecraft (James Scott):_ The state's reduction of old-growth forests into geometric tree metrics (_Normalbaum_) or complex communal land tenure into flat cadastral maps (`Scott p. 11–15`).
    - _In Educational Computing (Lubar / Free Speech Movement):_ The reduction of students to two-dimensional IBM punch cards (`Lecture 4, slide 2`).

### 9. Mediation

- **Definition**: The technical instruments, sensors, measurement protocols, and institutional processes that intervene between a physical or social phenomenon and its resulting data representation (`Lecture 3, slides 4, 8`).

### 10. Perspective

- **Definition**: The specific positionality, institutional goals, and framing assumptions of those who design and make data, which shape what the resulting dataset highlights or obscures (`Lecture 3, slides 4, 8`).

### 11. Naturalize / Denaturalize

- **Definitions**:
    - **Naturalize**: The social process by which human choices, classification categories, and technical standards become so deeply embedded and routine that they appear neutral, natural, and self-evident (`Lecture 3, slide 7`; `Bowker & Star p. 199`).
    - **Denaturalize**: The critical practice of unpacking data representations, algorithms, and technical systems to expose the hidden human judgments, political interests, and historical choices behind them (`Lecture 3, slide 7`).

## Lecture 4: Making Personal Data

### 12. Database

- **Definition**: An organized, persistent collection of digital records that allows personal and administrative data to be stored, queried, cross-referenced, and circulated across institutional domains (`Lecture 4, slide 8`).
- **Case Examples**: The Allegheny County DHS data warehouse used for predictive child welfare screening (`Eubanks p. 120`) or the central reference book database used for race classification under South African Apartheid (`Bowker & Star p. 200`).

### 13. Circulation

- **Definition**: The movement of data across different institutional contexts, systems, and platforms over time, during which data is repeatedly decontextualized from its origin and recontextualized for new uses (`Lecture 4, slide 5`).

### 14. Relationality

- **Definition**: The principle that personal data is not strictly individual, but fundamentally collective and relational—revealing health, genetic, financial, and behavioral information about family members, social networks, and demographic groups (`Lecture 4, slide 7`).

### 15. Representational Harm

- **Definition & Differentiation**:
    - **Representational Harm**: Harm that occurs when a system, algorithm, or data categorization misrepresents, degrades, or stigmatizes a group or individual (e.g., Google Photos auto-tagging Black people as "gorillas" or offensive demographic labels), regardless of whether material resources are withheld (`Lecture 4, slide 6`).
    - _Allocative Harm:_ Contrastingly, allocative harm involves the unfair or discriminatory distribution of concrete opportunities or resources (e.g., denial of housing, loans, or bail) (`Lecture 4, slide 6`).

## Lecture 5: Making People Out of Data

### 16. Self-Quantification

- **Definition**: The practice of tracking and measuring one's own physical, mental, and behavioral metrics (e.g., heart rate, steps, sleep stages, productivity) using wearable digital sensors and self-tracking applications (`Lecture 5, slide 6`).

### 17. Self-Making

- **Definition**: The ongoing social and individual process by which human beings construct their identities, agency, and self-understanding through interactions with social classifications, data metrics, and cultural tools (`Lecture 5, slides 3–5`).

### 18. Cyborgs

- **Definition**: A concept (derived from Donna Haraway and Colin Koopman) arguing that human identity and personhood are not purely isolated biological selves, but are fundamentally extended into and constituted by material devices, digital networks, and data trails ("we are cyborgs who extend into our data") (`Lecture 5, slide 5`; Koopman quoted in `Lecture 5, slide 5`).

## Lecture 6: Making Robust Knowledge

### 19. Underdetermination

- **Definition**: The philosophical principle that empirical data alone underdetermines scientific theories—meaning that data is insufficient to uniquely prove a single theory, and alternative, logically incompatible theories can always be constructed to fit the same observations (`Lecture 6, slide 7`; `Kuhn p. 146`).

### 20. Theory-Dependence of Observation

- **Definition**: The principle that observations and experimental data are never pure or neutral, but are always gathered, filtered, and interpreted through preexisting theoretical commitments and paradigm frameworks (`Lecture 6, slide 7`; `Kuhn p. 146`).

### 21. Thomas Kuhn

- **Definition**: The historian and philosopher of science whose book _The Structure of Scientific Revolutions_ demonstrated that scientific progress does not occur through linear, cumulative addition of facts, but through periods of consensus (_normal science_) interrupted by revolutionary paradigm shifts (`Lecture 6, slides 3–6`; `Kuhn p. 10, 144–158`).

### 22. Normal Science

- **Definition**: Research firmly grounded in an accepted scientific paradigm, wherein practitioners engage in puzzle-solving under shared rules and commitments without testing or questioning the paradigm itself (`Lecture 6, slide 4`; `Kuhn p. 10, 144`).

### 23. Scientific Community

- **Definition**: The institutionalized, social group of experts bound by shared training, professional commitments, and paradigm standards, whose collective intersubjective work produces robust knowledge (`Lecture 6, slides 4–5`; `Kuhn p. 151, 158`).

## Lecture 7: Science in Society

### 24. Expertise

- **Definition & Differentiations**:
    - **Formal Scientific Expertise**: Credentialed, institutionalized knowledge based on abstract models, standardized experiments, and laboratory conditions (`Collins & Pinch p. 117`).
    - **Lay / Practical Expertise**: Localized, experiential craft knowledge possessed by non-credentialed practitioners (e.g., Cumbrian sheep farmers) based on long-term observation of local environments and practices (`Collins & Pinch p. 117–120`).

### 25. Public Trust

- **Definition**: The degree of social credibility and confidence that citizens place in scientific institutions and government experts, which can be severely eroded when experts project false certainty, ignore local knowledge, or conceal errors (`Lecture 7, slide 8`; `Collins & Pinch p. 117, 124`).

### 26. Problem of Legitimation vs. Problem of Extension

- **Definitions & Differentiation**:
    - **Problem of Legitimation**: The challenge of how technical and scientific experts justify their authority and secure democratic backing for their policy recommendations (`Lecture 7, slide 8`).
    - **Problem of Extension**: The democratic question of how far participation in technical decision-making should be extended beyond credentialed experts to include lay communities and affected publics (`Lecture 7, slide 8`).

## Lecture 8: Populations and States

### 27. State

- **Definition**: A centralized political authority that exercises administrative governance, territory management, resource extraction, and law enforcement over a bounded domain (`Lecture 8, slides 2, 7`; `Scott p. 11`).

### 28. Population

- **Definition**: An abstract, aggregated collective entity created by tabulating individuals across a territory, converting unique human beings into a statistical unit governed by demographic laws and averages (`Lecture 8, slides 3, 7`; `Scott p. 11`).

### 29. Biopower

- **Definition & Differentiating Contexts**:
    - _Foucault / State Governance (Lecture 8):_ The set of administrative mechanisms, statistical tools, and state institutions used to manage, regulate, norm, and optimize the biological processes of human populations (birth rates, mortality, health, hygiene) (`Lecture 8, slide 7`).
    - _Eugenic Statistics (Lecture 9):_ The historical deployment of biopower by early statisticians (Galton, Pearson, Fisher) to justify state-directed selective breeding, social class hierarchy, and racial discrimination (`Lecture 9, slides 2–7`; `French / Nautilus p. 63`).

### 30. Political Arithmetic(k)

- **Definition**: The 17th-century precursor to statistics developed in England (John Graunt, William Petty) that used bills of mortality and population tallies to calculate the health, wealth, and power of the state (`Lecture 8, slide 4`).

### 31. Statistik

- **Definition**: The 18th-century German academic discipline of state description that compiled comprehensive qualitative and quantitative inventories of a principality's geography, population, and administrative resources (`Lecture 8, slide 5`).

### 32. Normal Curve

- **Differentiating Historical Interpretations**:
    - _Quetelet's "Average Man":_ Adolphe Quetelet adapted the astronomical error curve to human traits, viewing the population average as nature's ideal type and deviations as accidental errors (`Lecture 8, slide 6`; `Lecture 9, slide 2`).
    - _Galton's Eugenic Curve:_ Francis Galton reinterpreted the bell curve into a hierarchical tool of "genetic worth," ranking desirable elite traits above "undesired" deviations to justify eugenic intervention (`Lecture 9, slide 2`; `French / Nautilus p. 63`).

## Lecture 9: Eugenics and Statistics

### 33. Eugenics

- **Definition**: The social movement and scientific movement coined by Francis Galton in 1883 aimed at "improving" human stock by encouraging reproduction among elite classes (_positive eugenics_) and restricting reproduction among "inferior" or "feeble-minded" populations (_negative eugenics_) (`Lecture 9, slides 2–7`; `French / Nautilus p. 63`).

### 34. Regression to Mediocrity

- **Definition**: Francis Galton's original mathematical formulation of linear regression, discovered while studying parent-child height and talent inheritance, which demonstrated that offspring of extreme parents tend to revert back toward the population average (`Lecture 9, slides 2, 7`).

### 35. Karl Pearson

- **Definition**: Founder of the Department of Applied Statistics at UCL and co-founder of _Biometrika_, who developed fundamental statistical tools (chi-squared test, correlation coefficient) specifically to quantify racial purity, defend colonial genocide, and advance eugenics (`Lecture 9, slides 3, 7`; `French / Nautilus p. 63`).

### 36. Ronald Fisher

- **Definition**: Eminent British statistician and geneticist (creator of ANOVA and randomized control trials) who synthesized Darwinian evolution with Mendelian genetics while actively lobbying for eugenic sterilization laws to prevent the fertility of lower social classes (`Lecture 9, slides 3, 7`; `French / Nautilus p. 63–64`).

### 37. Significance Testing

- **Definition**: The statistical methodology developed by Pearson and standardized by Fisher that uses a "null hypothesis" straw-man and a p-value threshold (e.g., \(p < 0.05\)) to evaluate experimental results, historically deployed to reify group differences and present eugenicist claims as objective science (`Lecture 9, slides 3, 7`; `French / Nautilus p. 63`).

### 38. Lewis Terman / IQ Testing / Stanford

- **Definition**: The American psychologist at Stanford University who adapted the Binet intelligence scale into the Stanford-Binet IQ test, using standardized quantitative scores to rank human intelligence, assert racial hierarchies, and advocate for eugenic sterilization and restrictive immigration laws (`Lecture 9, slides 3, 7`).

## Lecture 10: Taking the Global View

### 39. Administrative State

- **Definition**: The modern bureaucratic state apparatus that relies on standardized administrative data, surveys, and registries to govern territory and manage populations from above (`Lecture 10, slides 2–5`).

### 40. United Nations (UN)

- **Definition**: Global international organization that establishes standardized global data collection protocols, indicators, and development frameworks (e.g., Sustainable Development Goals, Human Development Index) to guide global policy (`Lecture 10, slides 4, 7`).

### 41. International Monetary Fund (IMF)

- **Definition**: Global financial institution that monitors national economies using standardized metrics like Gross Domestic Product (GDP) and real growth rates to enforce global macroeconomic policies (`Lecture 10, slides 4, 7`).

### 42. Development

- **Definition & Differentiating Frameworks**:
    - _Mainstream Linear Model (Rostow / UN):_ Depicts development as a universal, step-by-step modernization ladder where poor nations are simply "behind" affluent Western nations (`Brooks p. 82–85`).
    - _Relational Comparison (Andrew Brooks):_ Critiques linear development models as developmental fantasies, showing that Western core wealth is historically and structurally codependent on the active underdevelopment and exploitation of the Global South (`Brooks p. 82–94`).

### 43. Fertility

- **Definition**: The demographic calculation of live birth rates per woman within a given population or geographic region (`Lecture 10, slides 3, 7`).

### 44. Demography

- **Definition**: The quantitative and statistical study of human populations, including their size, structure, territorial distribution, birth/death rates, and migration patterns (`Lecture 10, slides 3, 7`).

### 45. "Family Planning"

- **Definition**: State-sponsored and international health programs designed to regulate birth rates and access to contraception, historically shaped by post-WWII Malthusian and eugenicist narratives surrounding global population control in developing nations (`Lecture 10, slides 3, 7`).


# Case Studies and Examples

### 1. Robert Moses’s Parkway Overpasses on Long Island

- **Source**: Langdon Winner, _"Do Artifacts Have Politics?"_ (1980) & _Lecture 1_
- **Summary**: Urban planner Robert Moses designed over two hundred overpasses on Long Island, New York, with extraordinarily low clearance (some as low as nine feet at the curb).
- **Additional Context**: Moses intentionally built the overpasses low to physically block public buses from accessing Jones Beach and state parks. Because low-income families and racial minorities relied heavily on public transit while upper- and middle-class white families owned private automobiles, the physical design of the bridges enforced class and racial segregation prior to and independent of their use. This case demonstrates that **technical artifacts possess inherent political qualities**.

### 2. Cyrus McCormick’s Pneumatic Molding Machines

- **Source**: Langdon Winner, _"Do Artifacts Have Politics?"_ (1980)
- **Summary**: In the mid-1880s, Cyrus McCormick II added expensive, untested pneumatic molding machinery to his Chicago reaper manufacturing plant at a cost of $500,000.
- **Additional Context**: Standard economic logic would assume the machinery was added for efficiency. However, historian Robert Ozanne revealed that McCormick installed the machines specifically to break the National Union of Iron Molders. The machines produced higher-cost, lower-quality castings, but they allowed McCormick to replace skilled union molders with unskilled non-union workers. Once the union was destroyed, McCormick discarded the inefficient machinery, proving that technological choices can be deployed as tools of workplace domination.

### 3. California Mechanical Tomato Harvester

- **Source**: Langdon Winner, _"Do Artifacts Have Politics?"_ (1980)
- **Summary**: Developed by researchers at the University of California, a mechanical tomato harvester dramatically automated harvesting, requiring agricultural scientists to simultaneously breed hard, uniform, thick-skinned tomatoes.
- **Additional Context**: While advertised as a productivity triumph, the machine required massive capital investments that forced thousands of small family farms out of business, concentrating agricultural wealth in the hands of large agribusiness conglomerates while displacing thousands of farm laborers. Later additions, such as electronic color sorters, further consolidated corporate power in California agriculture.

### 4. YieldStar & RealPage Rental Pricing Algorithms

- **Source**: _Lecture 2_ (citing _ProPublica_, _Wired_, _Inc._)
- **Summary**: RealPage’s YieldStar software collects non-public, real-time lease data from competing landlords to dynamically set rental prices for apartment units across the US.
- **Additional Context**: Demonstrates how datafication structures modern social order. The algorithm operates as an automated price-fixing mechanism, suppressing market competition and driving up housing costs. It illustrates a **sociotechnical system** where landlords, software algorithms, housing shortages, and DOJ antitrust regulations intersect in everyday life.

### 5. Bay Area Air Quality Management District (BAAQMD) & Chevron Richmond Refinery

- **Source**: _Lecture 3_
- **Summary**: The BAAQMD monitors regional air pollution across the San Francisco Bay Area using the Air Quality Index (AQI) and collects flaring emission reports from oil refineries like Chevron Richmond.
- **Additional Context**: Illustrates the concepts of **selection, abstraction, mediation, and perspective** in data creation. The regional AQI provides an aggregated "view from above" that can smooth out and hide hyper-local toxic events. Conversely, local Richmond residents living near refinery flares possess a "view from below"—experiencing immediate respiratory distress and health risks that standard regional data points frequently obscure.

### 6. Free Speech Movement (FSM) & IBM Punch Cards

- **Source**: _Lecture 4_ (citing Steven Lubar)
- **Summary**: During the 1964 Free Speech Movement at UC Berkeley, student protesters wore and distributed replica IBM punch cards stamped with the slogan: _"I am a University of California student: Do not fold, spindle, or mutilate me."_
- **Additional Context**: The punch card became an iconic symbol of **bureaucratic abstraction and dehumanization**, representing a social order where human beings felt reduced to two-dimensional numeric records manipulated by university machines.

### 7. Race Classification & Reclassification under Apartheid South Africa

- **Source**: Geoffrey Bowker & Susan Leigh Star, _Sorting Things Out_ (Ch. 6)
- **Summary**: Under the Population Registration Act of 1950, the South African apartheid state mandated that every citizen be registered into rigid, official racial categories (White, Native/African, Coloured, Asian).
- **Additional Context**: Demonstrates the tragic tension between rigid, clear-cut administrative categories and the messy reality of human lives. Bowker and Star detail iconic borderline cases:
    - **Lize Venter**: An abandoned infant whose racial classification was debated by state officials based on physical traits like hair waviness and nose shape.
    - **Johannes Botha**: A white mail carrier whose home was raided by race inspectors because a Coloured man had visited his doorstep to discuss buying a used car.
    - **Ronnie van der Walt**: A famous white boxer suddenly reclassified as Coloured on the eve of a major match.
    - **Colander High School**: A "buffer school" where principals juggled admissions for light-skinned "borderline" children to preserve the school's social standing.


### 8. Chernobyl Nuclear Fallout & Cumbrian Sheep Farmers

- **Source**: Harry Collins & Trevor Pinch, _The Golem at Large_ (Ch. 6)
- **Summary**: Following the 1986 Chernobyl nuclear meltdown, radioactive fallout rained down on the Cumbrian fells in northern England, leading to long-term government restrictions on moving and selling sheep.
- **Additional Context**: Government scientists used theoretical models based on lowland clay soils to predict that caesium would quickly lock into the soil, claiming restrictions would last only three weeks. In reality, Cumbria’s acidic, peaty soil kept the caesium mobile, causing bans to last for years. Scientists dismissed the practical craft knowledge of local sheep farmers (e.g., regarding sheep grazing habits or localized rainwater runoff). Later, scientists admitted the farmers were right that much of the radiation actually originated from the nearby 1957 Windscale/Sellafield nuclear fire.

### 9. Multiple Personality Disorder (MPD) & "Making Up People"

- **Source**: Ian Hacking, _"Making Up People"_ & _Lecture 5_
- **Summary**: The rapid surge in diagnoses of Multiple Personality Disorder (now Dissociative Identity Disorder) during the 1980s following its inclusion in the DSM-III.
- **Additional Context**: Used by philosopher Ian Hacking to explain **performativity** and the **"looping effect."** When medical experts create a new category, institutions (clinics, talk shows, insurance forms) and knowledge networks disseminate it, creating a "moving target." Individuals adopt the classification to make sense of their suffering, changing how they act and ultimately altering the category itself.

### 10. The Copernican Revolution in Astronomy

- **Source**: Thomas Kuhn, _The Structure of Scientific Revolutions_ & _Lecture 6_
- **Summary**: The historic shift from Ptolemaic geocentric astronomy (which relied on epicycles) to Copernican heliocentric astronomy.
- **Additional Context**: Thomas Kuhn’s central case study for a **paradigm shift**. It illustrates how normal science operates within an accepted paradigm until persistent anomalies trigger a crisis, culminating in extraordinary science and a revolutionary "gestalt switch" or conversion experience across the scientific community.

### 11. The L'Aquila Earthquake Trial

- **Source**: _Lecture 7_
- **Summary**: In 2012, an Italian court convicted seven scientists and government officials of manslaughter following a 2009 earthquake in L'Aquila, Italy, that killed over 300 people.
- **Additional Context**: Illustrates the crisis of **public trust, risk communication, and expertise**. Prior to the tremor, officials had given overly reassuring public statements to prevent panic. The trial highlighted the danger of projecting absolute certainty in the face of inherently uncertain scientific phenomena.

### 12. Scientific Forestry (_Normalbaum_) in Prussia

- **Source**: James C. Scott, _Seeing Like a State_ (Ch. 1) & _Lecture 8_
- **Summary**: In the late 18th century, Prussian state foresters replaced diverse, wild forests with standardized, single-species, row-planted spruce and pine forests (_Normalbaum_).
- **Additional Context**: Scott uses this to illustrate state **legibility** and the "view from above." The state simplified complex forest ecosystems into measurable units of timber revenue. While initially successful, this extreme abstraction caused ecological disaster within a generation (_Waldsterben_ / forest death), as single-species forests proved highly vulnerable to pests, storm damage, and soil exhaustion.

### 13. John Graunt’s Bills of Mortality & Quetelet’s "Average Man"

- **Source**: _Lecture 8_
- **Summary**: 17th-century London mortality tallies compiled by John Graunt (_Political Arithmetick_) and 19th-century work by Belgian astronomer Adolphe Quetelet (_l'homme moyen_).
- **Additional Context**: Marks the birth of modern demography, statistics, and **biopower**. Graunt showed that aggregated mortality data revealed stable social patterns. Quetelet adapted the astronomical normal curve to human physical and moral traits, conceptualizing the mathematical "average" as nature's ideal type.

### 14. Eugenic Statistics (Galton, Pearson, Fisher, and Terman)

- **Source**: Audrey Clayton / Kristen French, _"How Eugenics Shaped Statistics"_ (Nautilus) & _Lecture 9_
- **Summary**: Victorian and early 20th-century scientists—Francis Galton, Karl Pearson, Ronald Fisher, and Lewis Terman—developed core statistical tools while advancing eugenics.
- **Additional Context**: Proves that modern mathematical statistics was co-produced with eugenics:
    - **Francis Galton**: Invented linear regression and correlation specifically to measure the inherited talent of elite families.
    - **Karl Pearson**: Created the chi-squared test to measure "homogeneity," arguing for racial purity and opposing Jewish immigration into the UK.
    - **Ronald Fisher**: Developed ANOVA and p-value significance testing while lobbying for state-mandated sterilization of the "feeble-minded."
    - **Lewis Terman**: Adapted the Binet IQ test at Stanford to rank intelligence, justify racial hierarchies, and influence US immigration restrictions.

### 15. Sierra Leone Healthcare vs. UK NHS & the "Medical Brain Drain"

- **Source**: Andrew Brooks, _Why Are Certain Countries Poor?_ (Ch. 8)
- **Summary**: A comparative analysis of healthcare outcomes at Connaught Hospital in Freetown, Sierra Leone, versus the British National Health Service (NHS).
- **Additional Context**: Brooks rejects linear development models (Rostow's stages) and Western volunteer narratives that frame Sierra Leonean hospitals as "going back in time." Using **relational comparison**, he shows that high Western health rankings are subsidized by the active extraction of medical labor from the Global South: 60% of Sierra Leonean-trained nurses emigrate, and 16% of Sierra Leone's public health workforce works in the UK NHS.

### 16. Gapminder.org & Global Fertility Data

- **Source**: _Lecture 10_
- **Summary**: Hans Rosling’s Gapminder platform, which animates global income, life expectancy, and fertility data across nations over time.
- **Additional Context**: Demonstrates how post-WWII international organizations (UN, IMF) built a synoptic, global "view from above." Highlights how demographic data collection transitioned from Malthusian Cold War anxieties over "population explosions" in developing nations to modern frameworks centered on women's empowerment and economic metrics.
