# Week 4 How Was the World Datafied?
This week asks **how the capacity to see and govern whole populations was historically constructed**. The world did not become datafied simply because better tools accumulated. States and other institutions developed reasons to count, built infrastructures for making people and resources comparable, and used the resulting representations to intervene. Statistical knowledge and the objects it described developed together.
The [[Course Review Guide]] joins [[James C. Scott's Seeing Like a State (Chapter 1)|Scott]], [[Aubrey Clayton's How Eugenics Shaped Statistics|Clayton]], and Lectures [[L8 Population and States|8]], [[L9 Eugenics and Statistics|9]], and [[L10 Taking the Global View|10]]. Read them as a connected movement: **make a population legible → identify regularities and norms → govern through comparisons and targets → extend that view across the globe**. This is a conceptual thread, not an inevitable sequence in which every statistical practice leads to eugenics.

## States, populations, and statistics make one another
A [[State]] governs a territory and its residents through institutions and claims to sovereign authority. A [[Population]] is more than people counted one by one: it is a collective object whose births, deaths, health, wealth, and distribution can be studied and managed. In [[L8 Population and States]], states seek this knowledge to tax, recruit armies, administer resources, and increase power.
This produces a reciprocal relationship. Governing ambitions create demand for records and standards. Those practices make a population visible as an aggregate. The aggregate then makes further forms of government possible. [[Co-Production]] names this mutual formation of knowledge and social order.
### From local records to population regularities
[[Political Arithmetic(k)|English political arithmetic]] emphasized counting, tabulation, and calculation. John Graunt's mortality tables assembled parish records of births and deaths into an overview of London. Individual events became a pattern visible across categories and years. Yet uneven categories and missing records also reveal how much work was required to make observations comparable.
[[Statistik|German Statistik]] emphasized descriptive knowledge of territories, populations, and resources for rulers. Unlike the modern association of statistics with mathematical inference, this tradition was often qualitative. Both traditions organized knowledge useful to state administration, despite their different methods.
The shift was not from no counting to counting. Earlier societies already kept records. The distinctive development was increasingly systematic production of comparable information across territories, organized around population as an object of knowledge and government.
### Counting distributes political standing
The lecture's early US census case shows that administrative categories also allocate power. Age, race, gender, and legal status mattered because enumeration served particular purposes, including representation and military concerns. The three-fifths rule increased slaveholding states' representation by counting enslaved people without granting those people political rights. Counting someone did not necessarily mean representing that person's interests.
[[Biopower]] identifies the wider management of collective life: health, reproduction, growth, and vitality become matters of government. This can support welfare and public health as well as coercive projects. The crucial question is who defines the population's flourishing and how benefits and burdens are distributed.

## Legibility makes complex places governable from a distance
In [[James C. Scott's Seeing Like a State (Chapter 1)|Scott's account]], **legibility** means translating complex, locally understood realities into simplified forms that central authorities can read and use. The questions are always **legible to whom, and for what?** A practice familiar to villagers can be opaque to an official who needs standardized information across a territory.
An administrative grid is therefore both a representation and an institutional arrangement. Common categories, measures, maps, and records enable comparison. Courts, officials, and enforcement can then make those simplifications authoritative in the world.
### Why scientific forestry and the metric system belong together
Scientific forestry reduced a diverse forest to features relevant to timber yield and state revenue. Foresters did more than prepare tables: planting uniform species in regular rows made the physical forest easier to count and manage. Practices and ecological relations excluded from the fiscal view could be damaged, including the diversity supporting the forest's long-term health.
The metric system replaced varied local measures with common units usable across a national territory. It supported administration and exchange and was connected to ideals of uniform citizenship. It also changed who could interpret, negotiate, and control measurement. Local systems were not necessarily free of domination; customary measures could serve local elites, while standardization could challenge some privileges.
The comparison is about a **shared administrative logic**, not identical outcomes. Both projects replace context-specific knowledge with a portable, standardized representation and reorganize practices around it. Forestry exposes the danger of treating what a simplified representation leaves out as dispensable. The metric case shows why standardization can be useful and politically consequential at the same time.
### Description becomes intervention
[[Performativity (HCE)|Performativity]] is visible when the world is changed to fit the scheme used to describe it. A forest planted to match yield tables is a concrete example. A property map backed by legal title can similarly reorganize whose claims are recognized.
Scott's point is stronger than saying a map is incomplete. **An incomplete map backed by authority can reshape the territory.** But reality never perfectly fits the grid. Resistance, ecological dependencies, and local practices limit what administrative simplification can accomplish.

## From the average person to a hierarchy of human worth
The movement from population counts to distributions introduces a new question: what do regularities and differences mean? [[L8 Population and States]] and [[L9 Eugenics and Statistics]] connect this question through Quetelet and Galton.
### Quetelet and the normal curve
Quetelet used population data to investigate regularities beyond individual intentions. The [[Normal Curve]] offered a way to represent the distribution of human traits, drawing on mathematical practices previously used for measurement error. His average man treated a statistical center as an image of the population and, in the lecture's account, an ideal against which deviations could be judged.
The key distinction is between **normal as statistically typical** and **normal as socially desirable**. A frequency distribution does not itself decide what people ought to be. Moving from the first meaning to the second introduces a normative judgment that can be hidden by mathematical presentation.
### Galton and the attempt to manage heredity
Galton redirected attention toward the ends of the distribution, ranking people and traits by supposed worth. He treated eminence, class position, intelligence, and other socially valued characteristics as evidence of inherited ability. The argument could therefore naturalize social hierarchy by presenting unequal outcomes as biological differences.
His work on parent-child height and heredity contributed to regression and correlation. [[Regression to Mediocrity]] names the historical framing of offspring of exceptional parents tending, on average, closer to the population mean. The mathematical pattern became a problem for Galton because of his ambition to preserve or increase supposedly superior traits.
[[Eugenics]] joined measurement to a program for directing human reproduction. Its advocates promoted reproduction among people they favored and restricted it among people categorized as undesirable. The institutions defining worth could then distribute opportunity, safety, and reproductive freedom according to those classifications.

## Clayton shows why the history matters to statistical practice
[[Aubrey Clayton's How Eugenics Shaped Statistics|Clayton]] argues that eugenic ambitions helped shape the questions, methods, institutions, and claims of objectivity of mathematical statistics. The point is not merely that accomplished statisticians also held objectionable private opinions. Their statistical and political projects were connected in practice.
### Methods, institutions, and authority
[[Karl Pearson]] developed mathematical tools while working within eugenic research institutions and journals. Clayton discusses how apparent differences in distributions and measures of association could be interpreted as evidence of distinct populations or inherited hierarchies. [[Ronald Fisher]] likewise combined influential statistical work with eugenic commitments and concern about reproduction across social classes.
These cases demonstrate co-production: eugenics supplied problems and institutional support for statistical work, while statistics supplied authority and methods for eugenic claims. Mathematical sophistication did not establish that the categories were appropriate, the interpretation was causal, or the proposed policy was justified.
[[L9 Eugenics and Statistics]] brings this into education through [[Lewis Terman and IQ Testing]]. Terman's adaptation of intelligence testing toward measuring presumed innate ability supported sorting and selection. Institutional adoption made scores consequential for who received opportunities and how people were valued. The Stanford case links a theory of human difference to the organization of education, rather than leaving it at the level of a scientist's beliefs.
### Statistical significance does not establish social importance
Clayton's critique of [[Significance Testing]] highlights the gap between asking whether a difference can be detected and asking how large it is, what causes it, or what action is warranted. With sufficient data, small differences can be statistically detectable. That alone does not show a meaningful hierarchy, a hereditary cause, or grounds for differential treatment.
The appropriate response is to examine the categories, comparison groups, assumptions, effect sizes, causal reasoning, and institutional purposes of an analysis. The lecture explicitly cautions against concluding that regression or correlation is inherently eugenic. A method's history does not mechanically determine every later use; it helps expose assumptions that a supposedly neutral presentation can conceal.
The same distinction applies to biopower. Eugenics is a particularly coercive way of managing populations, not a synonym for every public-health measure or demographic study. The task is to identify how particular institutions define desirable life and exercise authority over it.

## The global view extends administrative comparison across countries
[[L10 Taking the Global View]] scales the argument from national populations to international knowledge. The [[Administrative State]] supplies agencies, censuses, surveys, forms, and recurring procedures. Organizations such as the [[United Nations]] and [[IMF]] coordinate national statistics and standards to make global comparisons possible without a single world sovereign.
The guide locates the modern global view after World War I; the lecture emphasizes its consolidation after World War II through international institutions, the Cold War, decolonization, and development programs. The shared lesson is that global data has a history. It requires institutions that decide what to compare and infrastructures that make different countries' records commensurable.
### Indicators help define the objects of government
GDP represents economic activity through an aggregate that can become a policy target. This does not mean exchange and production began when GDP was invented. It means that a bounded national economy became legible and manageable in a particular way through accounting and administration.
Similarly, global indicators select versions of health, education, prosperity, or progress that can be compared. Their usefulness comes with authority to define what improvement means. [[Development]] can become a narrative in which countries are ranked along a common path toward a destination modeled on particular powerful states.
### Gapminder: a view of the world from somewhere
The lecture's Gapminder example plots fertility against child mortality, with country circles sized by population and grouped by region. The visualization makes large patterns easy to see and can challenge mistaken beliefs. But countries, axes, colors, averages, historical estimates, and projections all structure its story.
A trajectory toward lower fertility and lower child mortality can invite a developmental interpretation in which countries are expected to follow one path. The chart does not by itself explain how the changes occurred. [[Demography]] and [[Family Planning]] belong to histories of health, reproductive choices, political intervention, and sometimes coercion that an aggregate cannot distinguish on its own.
A national fertility measure can combine freely chosen contraception with experiences of pressure or sterilization. From a [[View from Above Below|view from below]], those differences are central to evaluating change. Supplementing the chart with people's accounts is not a rejection of comparison; it helps determine what the comparison means and what conclusions it can support.

## Study the week as one argument
Trace **the institution that wants to know → the standardization that makes comparison possible → the representation produced → the intervention it supports → the lives and relations it leaves out**.

1. Explain how states, populations, and statistics are co-produced, using Graunt and the contrast between political arithmetic and Statistik.
2. Compare forestry and the metric system to explain legibility without assuming that all simplification has identical consequences.
3. Distinguish Quetelet's average from Galton's hierarchy, and identify the step from a statistical description to a judgment about human worth.
4. Explain what Clayton's history changes about responsible statistical practice without concluding that useful mathematics must be discarded.
5. Use Gapminder to connect representation, development narratives, positionality, and population management, identifying what a national average cannot tell you about reproductive lives.
