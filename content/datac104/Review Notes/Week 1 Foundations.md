# Week 1 Foundations
The central question of this week is **how technologies help build social order**. A bridge or algorithm does more than accomplish a task: it makes some actions easier, blocks others, and distributes authority among people. Reading [[Do Artifacts Have Politics by Langdon Winner|Winner]] alongside [[L1 - Technology, Politics, and Context 08-26|Lecture 1]] and [[L2 Making the Datafied World 2|Lecture 2]] establishes the course's starting method: explain the relations through which a technology works, then ask who can shape those relations and who must live within them.
The [[Course Review Guide]] frames this unit around political artifacts, the layers of the datafied world, and the first four HCE tools. These are connected parts of an argument: ethical analysis requires understanding how an arrangement was made before deciding how it should change.

---

## Technologies organize power through their design
For Winner, politics concerns arrangements of power and authority in human associations. A technology can be political even when nobody calls it political and even when it works efficiently. Its material organization can settle questions about access, control, and participation that might otherwise be debated through law or public deliberation.
### Two ways artifacts can have politics
Winner distinguishes **particular designs that settle political questions** from **technologies claimed to require, or strongly favor, particular political arrangements**. The difference is between how a technology has been configured and what its operation may demand.

| Mode | How politics enters | Case and analytical question |
|---|---|---|
| A chosen technical arrangement establishes a form of order. | A design embodies decisions about whose activities it accommodates. Other configurations may have been possible. | Moses's overpasses: how does clearance, within a car-oriented road network, distribute access between car owners and bus riders? |
| A technology requires or is strongly compatible with a political arrangement. | Operational requirements may constrain how the system is governed. | Winner's atomic-bomb example: what centralized command and control does operating the technology require? Claims about decentralized solar power concern compatibility, which is weaker than necessity. |

The second mode is not a claim that every technology determines an entire society. Distinguish **required** from **compatible**, and distinguish organization **inside the system** from effects on the wider political order. A centralized technical operation does not by itself prove that all surrounding social institutions must be authoritarian.
### Moses's overpasses: from a design feature to a social relationship
In Winner's account, low overpasses restricted buses on Long Island parkways, favoring automobile access and disadvantaging people dependent on public transport. The political significance lies in the relationship among bridge height, the road network, available transportation, and racial and class inequalities. Height alone, removed from that system, does not explain the exclusion.
Lecture 1 qualifies the reading by noting disputes about intention and the extent of the physical restriction. Keep the distinction between **Winner's interpretation of the case** and **the general analytical claim**: an arrangement can systematically exclude people even when discriminatory intent has not been established. The lecture's stairs example makes the same point through disability access; neglect can produce an exclusionary environment without an explicit plan to exclude.
Once built, infrastructure lasts. Changing a law or a person's intentions does not automatically rebuild a road. Technical commitments can preserve an arrangement of power through maintenance costs, investments, habits, and the difficulty of creating alternatives. This is why design decisions can have a durability comparable to political institutions.

## The datafied world is made through social and technical choices
[[Technological Determinism]] treats technology as an autonomous force that drives social change, leaving people to adapt to its effects. The opposite reduction, social determinism, treats technology as a mere expression of social or economic forces and overlooks what specific designs enable or constrain. Winner and the lectures direct attention to the interaction: social choices shape technologies, and technologies reorganize the conditions under which people act.
Lecture 2 describes the [[Datafied World]] through four interdependent layers. **Data** turn selected activities and phenomena into records; **analytics** use those records to characterize, predict, or support decisions; **algorithms** regularize and automate procedures; **AI and machine learning** build on these capacities to learn, adapt, monitor, or plan. This is an analytical stack, not a claim that the layers are always separable in practice.
Every layer depends on people, institutions, and infrastructure. A prediction requires a choice of target, a dataset requires collection practices, and a recommendation requires someone with authority to use it. The material demands of data centers in Lecture 1 also make clear that digital systems depend on energy, water, land, and labor.
### Everydayness and world-making
[[Everydayness]] explains how technologies become familiar background conditions. A staircase, phone, or administrative system may disappear from the attention of people it serves while remaining an obvious barrier to people it excludes. Familiarity can make a historical design choice appear inevitable.
[[World-Making]] names the broader process: technologies organize the environments, relationships, and routines through which people live. An **obligatory passage point** is one mechanism of this power. If access to housing or public space requires passing through a particular system, the people controlling that system can structure others' options before any individual decision occurs.

## Rental pricing brings the week's argument together
In [[L2 Making the Datafied World 2]], rental-pricing systems such as RealPage connect landlords, property data, software firms, tenants, and housing markets. Their recommendations are described as optimal, but **optimal for whom and for what objective?** Maximizing predicted landlord revenue is a selected goal; it does not automatically maximize housing affordability or renters' well-being.
The algorithm therefore enters an existing social relationship and can reshape it. Landlords supply detailed property and rental information and purchase decision tools. Renters encounter the resulting prices and may have little ability to avoid the data practices surrounding access to housing. Shared information across landlords also raises questions about coordination among actors otherwise expected to compete. These questions concern system structure and market power, not just the correctness of a calculation.
The case does not establish that software alone causes housing unaffordability. It asks how software interacts with ownership, scarcity, investment, incentives, and regulation. The lecture's competing diagnoses matter because each implies a different intervention: change what data can be pooled, change the objective, change adoption practices, or address broader housing conditions.
### Use the four HCE tools together
The [[The Human Contexts and Ethics Toolkit|HCE toolkit]] supplies questions that make the case more precise. The tools work together rather than producing four separate verdicts.

| Tool | What it reveals in rental pricing |
|---|---|
| [[Power (HCE)]] | Who sets the optimization objective, controls the data, adopts recommendations, and can challenge the system? Power includes setting the premises of decisions, not only making a final decision. |
| [[Sociotechnical Systems (HCE)]] | What network of software, data, landlords, tenants, finance, laws, and institutions allows the pricing system to operate? |
| [[Identity and Positionality (HCE)]] | How do renter, landlord, developer, and regulator positions change what people see, know, gain, or risk? These are unequal relationships, not merely different opinions. |
| [[Narratives (HCE)]] | Does the system appear as inevitable progress, ordinary business software, exploitation, or a consequence of financialization? Each story assigns causes and makes some responses seem more reasonable. |

A sociotechnical explanation connects the optimization target to the actors who chose it, the infrastructure that makes it possible, and the relationships its use changes. That is more informative than calling an algorithm good or bad without explaining its operation in context.

## Study the week as one argument
A strong answer moves from **technical configuration → social relationship → distribution of power → possible intervention**. Be able to apply that sequence to both the overpasses and rental pricing.

1. Explain Winner's two modes of political artifacts, including the distinction between necessity and compatibility.
2. Show why the overpass case requires a whole transportation system and an account of unequal access, rather than bridge height alone.
3. Trace rental pricing through the datafied-world layers and identify where people choose goals or exercise authority.
4. Explain how everydayness makes technical politics harder to notice, and use the four HCE tools to make it visible again.
