# The Right to Oblivion: Privacy and the Good Life

## Executive Summary

Lowry Pressly’s _The Right to Oblivion_ challenges the dominant paradigm of the "Information Age," arguing that contemporary society has become profoundly "blinded by information". Under the sway of a pervasive "ideology of information," we have come to view all of human life, selfhood, and social relations through the narrow lens of data, assuming that more knowledge is always inherently better. Pressly systematically deconstructs the mainstream legal and philosophical concept of "informational privacy"—which reduces privacy to a procedural preference of "controlling the flow" of already existing data—and critiques it as a dangerous concession to surveillance capitalism. Instead, he asserts that privacy's true moral value lies not in managing information after the fact, but in protecting against the very creation of information in the first place, thereby preserving essential limits to the knowable.

At the center of Pressly's framework is the concept of **"oblivion"**—a positive, structured state of obscurity and unknowing that is fundamentally distinct from secrecy and hiding. While secrecy protects known, definite facts that can be shared, discovered, or leveraged, privacy protects and produces oblivion, an impenetrable realm of ambiguity and potentiality about which there is no fact of the matter one way or the other. Unlike hiding, which induces a state of anxiety and perpetual psychological tethering to the external world, privacy allows individuals to detach, exist without self-reflection, and "come apart" from their documented identities.

By weaving together historical perspectives on the rise of mass media in the nineteenth century with literary and philosophical insights from artists like Virginia Woolf, Werner Herzog, and Jorge Luis Borges, Pressly demonstrates that limits on self-knowledge are vital for psychological well-being, trust, self-relation, and democratic life. These notes outline the core arguments, categorizes their theoretical elements, and uses the Human Contexts and Ethics (HCE) toolkit to evaluate how the social cultivation of oblivion serves as an indispensable shield against the complete instrumentalization and exhausting exposure of modern human life.

## The Ideology of Information and the Critique of Informational Privacy

### The Blindness of the Information Age

- **The Seduction of Datafication**: Modern society is saturated with endless streams of updates, notifications, and precise biometrics tracking everything from heartbeats and daily steps to sleep quality and domestic temperatures. This unprecedented abundance of information fosters a cultural illusion that we see human life more deeply, when in reality, it impairs our faculty of critical and moral vision by reducing lived experience to transactional data.
- **The "You Are Your Data" Fallacy**: Under the quantified-self movement and modern data economies, human beings are increasingly reduced to "data subjects". Pressly critiques this reductionist perspective, arguing that by treating conversation as a mere exchange of information and selfhood as a collection of recorded data points, we lose sight of the valuable regions of life that depend on the preservation of limits to the knowable.
- **The Delphic Balance**: Philosophers historically champion the Enlightenment motto of _sapere aude_ ("dare to know") and the Delphic commandment to "know yourself". However, Pressly reminds us that this commandment was carved alongside another crucial maxim: "Nothing in excess". This pairing implies that ethical well-being requires moderation and a deliberate boundary to self-knowledge, a concept fundamentally at odds with today's information-based political economy of self-branding and constant exposure.

### Deconstructing "Informational Privacy" and Procedural Consent

- **The Neo-Liberalization of Privacy**: Over the past several decades, public discourse has shifted from discussing privacy's intrinsic humanistic value to debating its transactional "costs and benefits". Under this neoliberal framework, privacy is stripped of its status as a fundamental human right and recast as a form of personal property to be bought, sold, or traded in a market. Individuals routinely make counterfactual "consensual" transactions—surveilling their keystrokes or eye movements in exchange for a diploma or a paycheck—because the culture treats privacy as a fungible preference rather than an essential condition of well-being.
- **The Mirage of "Privacy Settings"**: Mainstream academic and legal definitions of privacy—including the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's entry on "informational privacy"—rely heavily on the concept of "contextual integrity" and the control over information "flows". Pressly argues that this paradigm is a dangerous trap because it naturalizes the existence of personal information _prior_ to the question of privacy. By the time a user interacts with online privacy settings to restrict access to their social media posts or biometric watch data, the platform owners already possess the extracted information. These touted privacy features are not genuine protections, but rather corporate cheerleading designed to legitimize a diminished view of privacy in service of profit maximization.

## The Conceptual Architecture of Oblivion

### Oblivion vs. Secrecy

- **A Secret Must Be Known**: For a secret to exist, it must be known definitively and unambiguously by at least one person; if it is forgotten or distorted, the secret ceases to exist. Secrets can be shared, and they are inherently durable—if someone discovers a secret, the secrecy is not destroyed, but they are now simply "in on it," a durability that enables the coercive leverage of blackmail.
- **Oblivion as Resolute Ambiguity**: Unlike secrecy, oblivion describes a state of affairs about which there is no information or knowledge one way or the other. Oblivion is essentially resistant to articulation, and it is instantly destroyed rather than shared when translated into definitive information. For example, telling a lie to an invasive question may successfully preserve a _secret_, but it violates _privacy_ and destroys _oblivion_ by introducing a false piece of information where before there was none.

### Oblivion vs. Hiding

- **The Tethered Mind of Hiding**: Experientially and psychologically, hiding is an intensely isolating and anxious state. The person in hiding remains constantly fixated on, and tethered to, the world beyond the barriers of their concealment, living in fear of the seeker.
- **The Detachment of Privacy**: Privacy, when properly understood, is characterized by the complete absence of this outward, anxious orientation. It is not a defensive act of keeping oneself or an object away from others, but an ease of existence that allows one to be completely untethered from surveillance and self-documentation. When life in private is reduced to "hiding"—as occurs under pervasive surveillance—the healthy, rejuvenating effects of privacy devolve into the alienating and lonely isolation of being cornered.

### Acts of Oblivion and Social Obliviousness

- **Deliberate Ignoring in Post-Conflict States**: The historical and legal precedent for the social construction of oblivion is found in "Acts of Oblivion"—historical amnesty laws passed in the wake of civil wars (such as the Treaty of Westphalia in 1648) designed to legally "bury in eternal oblivion" the past acts of conflict to prevent endless cycles of reprisal. These acts did not physically erase memories, but rather enforced a vital social ethic of deliberate, willed obliviousness in interpersonal affairs.
- **The Pantomime of Obliviousness**: In daily life, we respect privacy not merely by avoiding physical trespass, but by maintaining an attitude of deliberate obliviousness to the lives of others. When we turn away from someone relieving themselves in public or struggling with an embarrassing situation, we are executing a "pantomime of obliviousness". The comfort this provides has nothing to do with information control—both parties know what has occurred—but rather with the socially constructed message: "It is as if it never happened".

### The Artist's Sense of Privacy

- **Preserving Life's Mystery**: Pressly references Joshua Rothman’s distinction between the "citizen’s sense of privacy" (which focuses on informational control and preventing external interference) and the "artist’s sense of privacy" (typified by Virginia Woolf, which views privacy as preserving life’s mystery and shielding oneself from one's own prying eyes). He argues that these two senses are deeply intertwined, and that civic privacy has always been animated by this artistic need to preserve a condition of play, potentiality, and flux.
- **Werner Herzog’s Apartment Metaphor**: The filmmaker Werner Herzog warningly notes that "explaining and scrutinizing the human soul, [peering] into all its niches and crooks and abysses and dark corners, is not doing good to humans". He compares the human soul to an apartment: just as an apartment becomes completely uninhabitable if every single corner under the table is constantly illuminated, a marriage, friendship, or human life becomes uninhabitable if everything is perpetually illuminated, explained, and put out on the table.

### Memory, Agency, and the Right to Be Forgotten

- **Funes the Memorious and the Curse of Total Recall**: Contemporary cognitive scientists often misinterpret Jorge Luis Borges' 1942 story _Funes the Memorious_ as a study in memory capacity, rather than a tragic fable about a boy cursed to perceive and remember everything. Drawing on Borges' assertion that imagination requires a delicate blending of memory and oblivion, Pressly argues that a mind incapable of forgetting is incapable of generalizing, thinking, or dreaming.
- **The Right to be Forgotten as Oblivion**: The "right to be forgotten" is unique because it applies to information that is already publicly accessible on the internet. Rather than protecting secret data, this right is a tool of oblivion that allows individuals to sever the links connecting their present selves to their past actions, preserving the freedom of personal reinvention and preventing the past from permanently fixing the future.

## Exploration via the Human Contexts and Ethics (HCE) Toolkit

### Power

- **Surveillance Capitalism as Resource Extraction**: Power in the digital age is concentrated in surveillance states and corporate platforms that seek to convert all human existence into commodifiable information. The "ideology of information" serves as an administrative and political shield that naturalizes this extraction, treating data generation as an inevitable, neutral fact of nature rather than a highly political and lucrative intervention by tech elites.
- **Coercive Data Transactions**: The asymmetry of power in neoliberal markets forces individuals into coercive trade-offs, such as submitting to invasive eye-movement or keystroke tracking as a prerequisite for receiving a diploma or earning a paycheck. The rhetoric of "consent" in these transactions is a manifestation of corporate power, shifting the moral burden of surveillance onto the vulnerable individual who must "accept" the terms to survive.

### Sociotechnical Systems

- **The Information-Harvesting Infrastructure**: Modern sociotechnical systems—comprising mobile phones, smart watches, social media platforms, and centralized databases—are structurally optimized to maximize the continuous generation and documentation of personal lives.
- **The Co-optation of Privacy Settings**: Online "privacy settings" are technical artifacts that operate as part of this harvesting infrastructure. Rather than protecting the user, these settings act as a system mechanism that validates the initial extraction of data; they allow users the illusion of choice regarding who else sees their data, while the platform owners have already secured, analyzed, and stored the raw information for their own purposes.

### Identity/Positionality

- **The Reduction to "Data Subjects"**: The quantified-self movement and corporate datafication impose a rigid, quantified positionality on individuals, asserting that "you are your data". This reductionist identity strips away the natural ambiguity, fluidity, and internal depth of the self, forcing individuals to conform to the documented "facts" of their digital profiles.
- **Oblivion as the Guard of Human Potentiality**: For a person to maintain a healthy self-relation and the capacity for moral growth, they must have the positionality of being "impenetrable to knowledge" at some level. The background of oblivion allows individuals to "come apart" in the present, to play with identities, and to experiment with thoughts without the suffocating weight of permanent, unyielding documentation.

### Narratives

- **The Teleological Narrative of the Information Age**: The dominant social narrative of our time asserts that more knowledge, more tracking, and total documentation are universally beneficial and represent absolute progress. This narrative frames any attempt to restrict data as technophobic, backward, or indicative of "having something to hide".
- **The Counter-Narrative of the Habitable Soul**: Pressly constructs a powerful counter-narrative of "The Right to Oblivion." This narrative frames willed unknowing, deliberate forgetting, and willed obliviousness as essential prerequisites for a human world worth inhabiting. By bounding information, this counter-narrative asserts that the most precious and spiritual aspects of human life—such as deep friendship, love, trust, and creative imagination—flourish only in the protective shadow of the unexplained.
