# Objectivity and Authority: How French Engineers Reduced Public Utility to Numbers

## Executive Summary

Theodore M. Porter’s _Objectivity and Authority_ challenges the traditional view that the massive growth of quantification in public life is simply a natural, heroic outcome of scientific progress or a straightforward technocratic power grab. Instead, Porter argues that the expansion of mathematical objectivity is deeply intertwined with shifts in the political order. Rather than arising from the unyielding, secure authority of experts operating behind closed doors, quantification is often a strategy born of _weakness_—a defensive response to public resistance, political openness, and democratic suspicion. When administrative elites face the wrath of powerful outside forces, they rely on rigid, mechanical calculations to project disinterestedness, demonstrate fairness, and escape the personal responsibility of subjective decision-making.

Porter illustrates this dynamic through a detailed historical analysis of the elite French state engineering corps, the _Corps des Ponts et Chaussées_, whose members were recruited exclusively from the top graduates of the prestigious _École Polytechnique_. Armed with advanced mathematical training that served as a highly credible yet exclusive language, these engineers sought to interpret the legally mandated "declaration of public utility" for state infrastructure projects in purely quantitative terms. This quantitative turn began in 1832 with L. M. H. Navier’s mathematical formulation of public utility, which defended state-subsidized canals over short-sighted British market models by quantifying long-term externalities and transport cost savings. Over the course of the nineteenth century, this methodology evolved through Jules Dupuit’s introduction of marginal utility and differential pricing, transforming mathematical calculation from a tool designed to judge the market to one designed to summarize it.

Ultimately, Porter distinguishes between the "mania for quantification" and true "technocracy". While the public might associate the graduates of the _École Polytechnique_ with cold, mathematical decision-making, the elite engineers themselves—particularly after establishing secure, closed bureaucratic structures—showed little inclination to reduce their decisions to mechanical operations on numbers, choosing instead to rely on "general culture," leadership, and "instinct". Thus, Porter concludes that quantification is a unique tribute to democracy: a public rhetoric made necessary only when the personal authority commanded by an administrative elite is insufficient to withstand public controversy.

## Objectivity, Consensus, and the Rhetoric of Numbers

### Objectivity as Intersubjectivity and Standardization

- **Defining Objectivity**: Porter defines scientific objectivity first as "intersubjectivity"—the possibility of mutual understanding and public communication made possible by an explicit ethic of publication. Objectivity is not an unmediated window into the mind of the creative scientist; rather, it is a rigid, standardized form of communication that makes knowledge readily interpretable by diverse readers precisely because it is silent on personal, implicit, and tangled reasoning.
- **The Standardization Impulse**: Since the nineteenth century, scientists have acted as "disciples of Henry Ford avant la lettre," striving to standardize physical units (such as electricity, magnetism, heat, and mechanical work) to technically master nature and aid communication. This impulse is reflected in the astronomers' "theory of errors," which established quantitative rules to correct discrepancies caused by the "personal equations" of different observers and reduce inconsistent observations to a single objective value.
- **Consensus and Planned Societies**: Karl Pearson, an avowed socialist and key founder of mathematical statistics, identified objectivity as the decisive social virtue of science. Pearson argued that an integrated, planned society could not depend on arbitrary personal judgment; it required impersonal, social knowledge where individual biases disappeared into a collective product, establishing a terrain of uncontested consensus to coordinate human activities.

### The Anti-Rhetorical Rhetoric of Public Science

- **Impersonally Constructed Authority**: Scientific papers are constructed according to a canonical style designed to imply adherence to a rigorous, formal method. This anti-rhetorical perspective supports the presumption that scientific knowledge is impersonal and dictated by nature rather than by fallible humans, masking the deep negotiations and personal judgments that actually occur in the laboratory.
- **Judicial vs. Scientific Objectivity**: While a judge's or jury's claim to objectivity rests on "fair-mindedness" and having no personal stake in the outcome of a private dispute, scientists must couple impartiality with expert knowledge. For scientists, the best guarantee of objectivity is that they have done no more than apply established, general principles to a case using known formulas and empirical numbers, enacting a "rule of law" within science.
- **Mechanical Fairness**: When large government expenditures or state dealings with private businesses invite public suspicion of favoritism or corruption, the "objectivity" of science is invited to speak. In these situations, pure truth claims are less essential than the appearance of mechanical fairness; purely mechanical measures and calculations seem fair because they stand outside private interests and human fallibility.

## Bureaucracy and the Rise of "Arithmétique Sociale" in France

### The Politics of Insecure Elites

- **A Movement Born of Weakness**: Porter challenges the view that public quantification is a sign of technocratic strength. Secure, traditional elites—such as hereditary aristocracies raised to govern by "instinct"—have historically had little use for statistics. Rather, statistics are introduced as a substitute for broad practical experience when inexperienced individuals rise to office, or when decision-makers face the potential wrath of powerful, hostile outside forces.
- **The Role of Bureaucratic Growth**: The growth of centralized state bureaucracy in the nineteenth century was a crucial prerequisite to the quantification of public life. Bureaucrats used statistics (which Charles Dickens' character Thomas Gradgrind in _Hard Times_ called simply "facts") to define administrative categories, compile synoptic reports on social problems, enlist public support, and justify state actions.
- **Defensive Rationality**: Highly technical calculations are rarely produced for direct public consumption; instead, they serve as defensive instruments within the state's administrative apparatus. By reducing decisions to calculations, a bureaucratic agency can satisfy the public that it is acting responsibly, proving that it stands aloof from petty politics, favoritism, and corruption.

### The École Polytechnique and the Language of the Corps

- **Recruiting the Mathematical Elite**: Following the French Revolution, the French state engineering corps (_Corps des Ponts et Chaussées_) recruited exclusively from the top graduates of the _École Polytechnique_. These engineers were the first to study advanced mathematics as an integral part of their professional identity, setting them apart from entrepreneurial, private builders.
- **An Incomprehensible Shield**: Mathematics served as the special language of French state engineers. Because it was highly credible to the public but largely incomprehensible to it, mathematics became an invaluable professional resource for fending off controversy and making controversial public decisions appear routine and automatic.
- **The Mandate of Public Utility**: To secure land for highways, canals, and railroads under the right of eminent domain, the French government required a formal "declaration of public utility". The engineers of the _Corps des Ponts et Chaussées_ chose to interpret this mandate in terms of calculation, successfully excluding the general public from criticizing their decisions by framing these matters as the sole concern of an authorized corps of specialists.

## The Evolution of Cost-Benefit Formulations

### L. M. H. Navier and the Quantified Public Good (1832)

- **The French vs. British Model**: In 1832, engineer L. M. H. Navier published a foundational paper outlining a mathematical method to evaluate the utility of public works. Navier drew a sharp contrast between the British market model—driven by self-seeking capitalists who only built profitable works in already wealthy regions—and the French state model, which stood above subjective entrepreneurship to serve the public good.
- **Quantifying Externalities**: Navier argued that the state could construct public works in anticipation of growth, taking account of regional benefits ("externalities") that were not immediately realizable as direct toll revenue.
- **The Break-Even Formula**: To justify state-subsidized canals, Navier provided a concrete calculation:
    - A canal costs **700,000 francs per league** to construct.
    - At a 5 percent interest rate, this equals an annual interest charge of **35,000 francs per league**.
    - Maintenance and administration require an additional **10,000 francs per league per year**, bringing the total annual cost to **45,000 francs**.
    - The difference in transport costs between canals and roads is **0.87 francs per ton-league**.
    - Therefore, a canal is mathematically "publicly useful" and worth building if at least **52,000 tons** (calculated as \(45,000 / 0.87\)) are transported over it annually, even if the state sets tolls low to maximize usage and fails to recoup its direct investment.

### Jules Dupuit and the Marginal Utility Revolution

- **The Critique of Navier**: In 1844, engineer Jules Dupuit challenged Navier's formula. Dupuit observed that the utility of a transport line is not a fixed property of the good itself, but a variable quantity that depends on the specific circumstances of each individual user.
- **The Demand Schedule**: Dupuit introduced the concept of marginal utility, arguing that the demand for any good or service must be represented as a function, or schedule, of price. At high prices, only users who derive extreme benefit will participate; at low prices, demand expands. The true utility of a public work is the sum of these differing utilities derived by all rational users.
- **Differential Pricing and Market Synthesis**: A militant laissez-faire liberal, Dupuit proposed a system of "differential prices" where each user was charged in proportion to the specific benefit they realized, arguing that through this system, utility and revenue could be made commensurable. Unlike Navier, Dupuit used quantification to summarize and validate the values of the market rather than to judge the market against an external state standard.

### The Late Nineteenth-Century Synthesis

- **The Cost-Toll Split**: By the late nineteenth century, the tradition of practical quantification in French engineering had incorporated an eclectic mix of Navier’s and Dupuit's ideas. Engineers were required to separate _frais_ (costs of transport) from _péages_ (tolls).
- **Prix de Revient Partiel**: To determine the actual costs of transport, French engineers adopted quantitative accounting methods developed in 1847 by Belgian state engineer Alphonse Belpaire, who had meticulously analyzed the nationalized Belgian rail accounts for the calendar year 1844.
- **The Exclusion of Discretionary Judgment**: Clement-Leon Colson, a dominant authority on transport economics, insisted that tolls must be governed by fixed, mathematical rules to completely eliminate individual "judgment" or administrative discretion, ensuring absolute bureaucratic objectivity in state-run monopolies.

## Exploration via the Human Contexts and Ethics (HCE) Toolkit

### Power

- **Mathematical Exclusivity and Epistemic Authority**: Power is exercised through the elite engineering corps' monopolistic control over the highly advanced mathematical language taught at the _École Polytechnique_. By translating "public utility" into recondite formulas, the engineers established a form of epistemic authority that was highly credible to the public but completely incomprehensible to it, successfully fending off political interference and silencing public criticism.
- **Quantification as a Defensive Shield**: The state's reliance on rigid calculations represents a relational struggle for power. Secure elites raised to govern do not voluntarily subject themselves to mathematical constraints. Rather, the "mania for quantification" is forced upon administrators by powerful, hostile outside forces, using the "rule of law" in science to defend their actions against charges of corruption and favoritism.

### Sociotechnical Systems

- **The Infrastructure of the State Engineering Corps**: The _Corps des Ponts et Chaussées_ operated as a highly integrated sociotechnical system. This system tightly coupled the physical geography of France (canals, highways, and railroads) with abstract mathematical curricula, bureaucratic "declarations of public utility," and accounting methods like Belpaire's _prix de revient partiel_.
- **The Grid of Rules**: The technical tools of economic calculation were inseparable from the bureaucratic rules that governed them. Standardizing the mathematical methods of analysis was a sociotechnical intervention designed to standardize the social values of the nation, reducing the messy play of political interests to a routine, automatic, and centralized grid of numbers.

### Identity/Positionality

- **The Generalist Elite Standpoint**: The positionality of the _École Polytechnique_ graduates was defined by their self-image as a highly cultured, meritocratic elite. They believed their rigorous training in abstract mathematics and classical letters prepared them for "polyvalent" leadership, allowing them to move easily between different sectors of industry and government.
- **The Paradox of Professional Identity**: While these elite engineers used mathematical objectivity to secure their positions, their actual professional identity was not based on being "mere" technicians or mathematical computers. Once their bureaucratic positions were socially secure and closed to public scrutiny, they abandoned mechanical calculations in favor of trusting their "instincts" and "general preparation for leadership," revealing that their reliance on numbers was a situational response to political exposure rather than an intellectual necessity.

### Narratives

- **The Progress Narrative of Abstract Rationality**: The _Corps des Ponts et Chaussées_ actively deployed a narrative of "abstract rationality" and public service. Navier framed the state engineer as a disinterested servant of a higher logic, contrasting this with the short-sighted, selfish narrative of the private British capitalist to justify state-subsidized infrastructure.
- **The "Facts Alone" Narrative**: In public discussions, quantifiers present scientific and statistical results as raw, uncompromised "facts" (exemplified by Thomas Gradgrind). This narrative of "facts alone" serves to distance the statistician from the rhetorical and political work their numbers perform, presenting a highly crafted, value-laden decision-making process as an objective, natural, and unquestionable truth.
