# Tianyu M. Fang – Silicon Valley Goes To War

## Executive Summary

In his essay _"Silicon Valley Goes To War,"_ published in _Noema Magazine_, researcher and historian Tianyu M. Fang analyzes the American technology sector's multi-year pivot away from consumer-oriented digital platforms toward the direct embrace of warfare, defense contracting, and military technology. For nearly two decades, Silicon Valley cultivated a progressive, techno-utopian narrative, framing its smartphone applications, social networks, and gig-economy platforms as engines of a borderless "global village" dedicated to democratizing information, fostering creativity, and making the world more open and connected. Today, that ethos has been largely dismantled as premier startup incubators like Y Combinator—which once funded DoorDash, Airbnb, and Reddit—now underwrite autonomous weapons manufacturers like Ares Industries, whose stated goal is building low-cost anti-ship cruise missiles for a prospective war against China.

Fang demonstrates that this militarization is not an unprecedented disruption of Silicon Valley’s identity, but rather a return to its historical origins. The popular myth of the tech industry as a product of lone garage entrepreneurs working in a libertarian free market obscures the reality that the Bay Area’s foundational infrastructure was financed by massive Cold War defense budgets, military procurement contracts, and federal research grants. Under figures like Stanford Provost Fred Terman, the region developed as a "cold war university" and research hub where institutions like the Stanford Research Institute, Varian Associates, Fairchild Semiconductor, and Lockheed Missiles and Space Company engineered microwave tubes, satellites, and missile guidance systems for the Pentagon. This Cold War alliance was only temporarily interrupted by the 1960s counterculture and 1990s cyberlibertarian movements, which sought to reclaim computing tools from the military "Leviathan" for individual expression and decentralized community.

The contemporary resurgence of defense technology is driven by a convergence of economic saturation, industrial restructuring, and geopolitical competition. As consumer platforms run out of breakthrough concepts and face market exhaustion—a condition captured by the "stagnation hypothesis"—venture capital firms and startup founders have identified national security budgets as a lucrative growth frontier. Tech disruptors are actively challenging the legacy defense contractor oligopoly established after the Pentagon’s 1993 "Last Supper," leveraging hot wars in Ukraine and the Middle East, as well as an escalating artificial intelligence arms race with China, to capture billions in defense contracts.

Ultimately, Fang critiques the moral framework mobilized by Silicon Valley leaders to justify this militarization, identifying what he terms the "Democracy Paradox". While tech evangelists claim that developing lethal autonomous weapons and frontier AI is a moral imperative to safeguard liberal democracy against foreign autocracies, American tech giants are actively deploying those exact tools to expand domestic surveillance, support mass deportation operations, manipulate political discourse, and supply overseas allies accused of severe human rights violations. By subordinating democratic values, internal ethical standards, and worker dissent to state power and nationalistic commercial interests, Silicon Valley’s embrace of techno-nationalism risks eroding the very democratic institutions it claims to protect.

## The Transformation of Silicon Valley: From Consumer Tech to Warfare

### The Demise of the Consumer Internet Ethos

- **The Progressive Narrative of the 2000s and 2010s**: For over twenty years, American technology enterprises marketed their consumer applications through a utopian, border-transcending ethos. Tech leaders insisted that consumer internet innovations were not merely delivering daily conveniences, but were generating a global public good by democratizing access to information, connecting humanity across geographic borders, and fostering individual self-expression.
- **The Shift from Social Connection to Guided Missiles**: This consumer-facing orientation has been supplanted by an explicit focus on the technologies of warfare. A primary indicator of this transformation occurred in August 2024, when the prestigious startup accelerator Y Combinator (YC)—historically celebrated for launching consumer applications like Airbnb, DoorDash, Instacart, Coinbase, and Reddit—diverged from its traditional portfolio to back Ares Industries, an El Segundo-based defense startup dedicated to constructing low-cost anti-ship cruise missiles designed for a hypothetical conflict against China in the Taiwan Strait.
- **Mainstreaming Defense Technology**: While technology companies previously treated defense contracts as public relations liabilities or low-status corporate endeavors, defense tech has moved squarely into the industry mainstream. Industry leaders now openly argue that top software and hardware engineers must abandon trivial consumer diversions—such as food delivery platforms or photo-sharing apps—to apply their talents directly to military superiority.

### The Surge in Defense Venture Capital and Unicorn Valuations

- **Tenfold Capital Expansion**: Venture capital investments in defense-tech startups experienced an unprecedented expansion, multiplying more than ten times between 2019 and 2024 to reach approximately $3 billion. In 2025, venture capital deals in military technology reached record highs, with company valuations doubling year-over-year.
- **Emergence of Next-Generation Defense Primes**: A cohort of well-capitalized defense startups has rapidly scaled to rival traditional aerospace contractors:
    - _Anduril Industries_: Founded in 2017 by Oculus VR inventor Palmer Luckey, the enterprise raised $2.5 billion to reach a $30.5 billion valuation and initiated construction on a $1 billion weapons manufacturing facility in Ohio.
    - _Palantir Technologies_: Co-founded in 2003 by Alex Karp and Peter Thiel to provide intelligence analytics software to the military and law enforcement, Palantir was added to the S&P 500 index in September 2024.
    - _Saronic Technologies_: Founded in 2022 in Austin, Texas, the company secured $600 million in private funding to manufacture AI-driven autonomous warships.
    - _Shield AI_: Valued at $5.3 billion, the San Diego firm raised $240 million to scale its autonomy software suite for AI-controlled military aircraft and drone swarms.

## The Historical Genealogy: Cold War Origins and the Countercultural Interlude

### Cold War Foundations and the "Cold War University"

- **The State-Funded Foundation of Silicon Valley**: Fang refutes the libertarian myth that Silicon Valley was generated purely by private venture capital and autonomous garage innovators. The foundational electronic innovations of the region were directly underwritten by federal research grants, national security priorities, and Department of Defense subsidies throughout the Cold War.
- **Fred Terman and Stanford's Military Integration**: Under engineering dean and provost Frederick Terman, Stanford University deliberately tied its academic research programs to the post-WWII military-industrial complex. Federal research funding awarded to Stanford more than quadrupled from under $2 million in 1951 to $8.3 million by 1960, establishing what historian Rebecca S. Lowen conceptualized as the "cold war university".
- **Early Industrial Giants**: Military contracts formed the primary financial backbone of the early Bay Area electronics industry. The Stanford Research Institute (SRI) carried out classified computing research for defense clients; Varian Associates manufactured klystron tubes for radar and military communications; Fairchild Semiconductor, launched in 1957 by the "traitorous eight," designed cutting-edge integrated circuits for military satellites and missile guidance systems; and Santa Clara County’s single largest employer prior to the personal computer boom was Lockheed Missiles and Space Company in Sunnyvale.

### The 1960s Counterculture and the Reappropriation of Military Tools

- **Anti-War Protests and the Fear of Bureaucratic Control**: During the 1960s, students and activists at Stanford and UC Berkeley led fierce protests against local electronics manufacturers designing lethal technologies for the Vietnam War. As media historian Fred Turner documents in _From Counterculture to Cyberculture_, activists feared that centralized command-and-control computing architectures built for foreign battlefields would be turned inward as instruments of bureaucratic tyranny and domestic state surveillance.
- **New Communalism and the Homebrew Computer Club**: Rather than rejecting digital technology entirely, countercultural technologists sought to liberate computing tools from the institutional "Leviathan". In 1975, anti-war computer engineers like Lee Felsenstein joined Steve Wozniak and others in the Homebrew Computer Club, re-engineering military computing systems into decentralized personal computers intended to foster individual creativity, autonomy, and grassroots community.
- **The Cyberlibertarian 1990s and Hackers**: Following the conclusion of the Cold War, early internet culture organized itself around open-access cyberlibertarianism. Activists and hackers fiercely opposed Clinton administration initiatives like the Clipper Chip (a cryptographic backdoor) and fought U.S. export controls on encryption algorithms. This era culminated in John Perry Barlow’s 1996 "Declaration of the Independence of Cyberspace," which proclaimed that digital networks must remain completely free from government sovereignty, physical coercion, and military force.

## Economic and Geopolitical Drivers of the Defense Pivot

### Market Saturation and the Stagnation Hypothesis

- **The Exhaustion of Consumer Applications**: By the late 2010s, consumer-facing digital markets—including online marketplaces, programmatic advertising networks, social media platforms, and on-demand gig work—had reached structural saturation. Venture capitalists like Peter Thiel observed that the consumer internet had largely exhausted its major breakthroughs, prompting investors to search for higher-margin opportunities.
- **The Stagnation Hypothesis**: Thiel and economist Tyler Cowen popularized the "stagnation hypothesis," arguing that despite massive progress in digital consumer software, genuine technological development in physical and hard sciences—including transportation, energy, biotechnology, and manufacturing—had ground to a halt since the 1970s. This frustration was encapsulated in Founders Fund’s famous investment motto: _"We were promised flying cars, and instead, we got 140 characters"_.
- **Military Budgets as Innovation Vehicles**: Defense tech evangelists argue that renewed military contracts offer the capital necessary to revive heavy industrial manufacturing, aerospace engineering, and physical hardware innovation outside the limits of consumer advertising models.

### Disrupting the Defense Oligopoly: "The Last Supper" vs. "The First Breakfast"

- **The 1993 "Last Supper" and Defense Consolidation**: Following the end of the Cold War, Clinton administration Secretary of Defense Les Aspin held an infamous dinner with defense executives, informing them that federal procurement would contract drastically and that corporations must merge to survive. This policy triggered an aggressive consolidation wave that reduced the number of major American aerospace and defense prime contractors from over fifty down to just five giants, dominated by Lockheed Martin, Boeing, and General Dynamics.
- **The "First Breakfast" Call to Action**: Modern tech executives argue that this consolidation created an uncompetitive, sluggish procurement bureaucracy incapable of meeting contemporary threats. Palantir Chief Technology Officer Shyam Sankar argued that the Department of Defense operates a "centrally unplanned process" resembling Soviet planning, calling for an era of the "First Breakfast" where agile, venture-backed software and robotics startups displace legacy defense monopolies.
- **The Procurement "Valley of Death"**: Despite raising more than $70 billion in private capital by 2025, venture-backed defense firms secured only $29 billion in federal awards, receiving less than 1 percent of the total Department of Defense procurement budget. To overcome this "valley of death"—the administrative time lag between private technological innovation and federal contract allocation—the Pentagon under Secretary of Defense Lloyd Austin created the Office of Strategic Capital in 2022 to channel commercial venture capital into national security production.

### The Geopolitical AI Arms Race with China

- **The Narrative of Chinese Technological Acceleration**: Proponents of defense tech argue that while American innovation stagnated in physical technologies, China rapidly gained dominance in electric vehicles, industrial robotics, battery manufacturing, commercial drones, nuclear reactors, and domestic semiconductor manufacturing.
- **The Race for Superintelligence and National Survival**: Influential figures in Silicon Valley and Washington framed artificial intelligence as the decisive front in great-power competition. Former OpenAI researcher Leopold Aschenbrenner argued in his treatise _"Situation Awareness"_ that the United States must achieve Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) and superintelligence before Beijing to maintain military hegemony and preserve the free world. Similarly, Undersecretary of State and former Palantir advisor Jacob Helberg urged Congress to fund an "AGI Manhattan Project".
- **Questioning the Arms Race Premise**: Fang notes that this geopolitical narrative is frequently unsubstantiated; independent analysts observe that Chinese strategic policy focuses primarily on domestic industrial supply-chain resilience and the broad diffusion of practical AI across manufacturing, rather than chasing speculative, abstract superintelligence. Nevertheless, the narrative of an existential arms race has been utilized by technology leaders to justify unchecked commercial expansion, extract federal subsidies, and dismantle regulatory oversight.

## Ideological Realignment: Accelerationism and Corporate Militarism

### Effective Accelerationism (e/acc) and the Techno-Optimist Doctrine

- **The Ideological Mandate for Unconstrained Growth**: Venture capitalist Marc Andreessen helped formalize the intellectual foundation for this military pivot through his October 2023 _"Techno-Optimist Manifesto"_. Embracing "effective accelerationism" (e/acc), Andreessen argues that technological and scientific acceleration must be pursued unconditionally, branding ethical debates, environmental sustainability, and precautionary risk management as enemies that promote "de-growth" and human suffering.
- **National Power as the Guardian of Liberty**: Andreessen explicitly linked technological acceleration to military strength, stating that _"Technologically strong liberal democracies safeguard liberty and peace. Technologically weak liberal democracies lose to their autocratic rivals, making everyone worse off"_.
- **National Security as a Moral Imperative**: Prominent founders have adopted similar nationalist framing; Palantir CEO Alex Karp declared that his company’s core purpose was always to make the American military the strongest force in global history, while Scale AI co-founder Alexandr Wang characterized the deployment of AI for national defense as an absolute moral imperative.

### The Rollback of Ethical Safeguards and Employee Dissent

- **The 2018 Project Maven Revolt**: In 2018, over 4,000 Google employees signed internal protest petitions and several resigned over the company's participation in Project Maven, an initiative using computer vision algorithms to interpret military drone footage for the Pentagon. Under intense pressure from workforce dissent rallying around the motto "Google should not be in the business of war," Google leadership allowed the Maven contract to lapse and introduced strict AI principles prohibiting the deployment of AI for weapons systems or operations violating human rights.
- **Systematic Erasure of Ethical Prohibitions**: By 2024 and 2025, tech leadership systematically reversed these internal ethical barriers:
    - _OpenAI_: In early 2024, OpenAI quietly excised clauses from its terms of service that banned the use of its models for "military and warfare" or "weapons development," subsequently securing a $200 million Department of Defense contract.
    - _Anthropic_: In June 2024, Anthropic announced "Claude Gov," a specialized variant of its frontier model engineered specifically for deployment within U.S. defense and intelligence agencies.
    - _Google_: In February 2025, Google officially deleted commitments from its public AI principles that prohibited the development of AI weapons or technologies designed to cause injury or violate international human rights norms.
- **Formal Military Integration**: The boundary separating corporate tech leadership from the armed forces blurred significantly in mid-2025 when the U.S. Army swore in four prominent tech executives—including Palantir CTO Shyam Sankar—as active lieutenant colonels in an army innovation advisory corps.

## The "Democracy Paradox" and Ethical Contradictions

### The Paradox of Authoritarian Means for Democratic Ends

- **Defining the Democracy Paradox**: Fang defines the "Democracy Paradox" as the structural contradiction where American technology corporations justify the development of coercive, militarized technologies under the banner of defending liberal-democratic values, while simultaneously utilizing those exact technologies to undermine democratic rights at home and abroad.
- **Building the Domestic Police State**: While tech executives condemn foreign autocracies for using facial recognition and AI to police political dissidents, American tech firms profit from constructing equivalent domestic surveillance architectures. Notably, Palantir actively develops surveillance, predictive data-mining, and biometric identification infrastructure deployed by federal agencies to execute mass deportation sweeps of undocumented immigrants.
- **Susceptibility to Political Censorship and Disinformation**: Despite warning against foreign propaganda, domestic American AI systems remain vulnerable to partisan capture and ideological manipulation. Elon Musk’s xAI chatbot Grok has output Adolf Hitler roleplay responses and repeatedly generated rhetoric concerning alleged "white genocide" in South Africa, while the forced sale of TikTok to American investors, including billionaire Larry Ellison, raised deep concerns regarding domestic political censorship.

### Foreign Entanglements and Human Rights Complicity

- **Subsidizing Overseas Warfare**: American cloud and software providers maintain lucrative contracts with foreign governments engaged in severe humanitarian crises. Microsoft and Google continue to provide enterprise cloud computing and artificial intelligence infrastructure—including Google's controversial Project Nimbus contract—to the Israeli military and government, despite widespread international condemnation by human rights monitoring organizations regarding abuses in the West Bank and Gaza Strip.
- **Financial Compromises and Autocratic Capital**: Despite framing their mission as an ideological war between democracies and autocracies, American AI developers actively court financing from authoritarian regimes. In July 2024, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei sought massive investment capital from the sovereign wealth funds of Qatar and the United Arab Emirates, defending the ethical compromise in an internal memo by noting that _"‘No bad person should ever benefit from our success’ is a pretty difficult principle to run a business on"_.
- **Corporate Alignment with Illiberal Power**: Tech executives have enthusiastically aligned themselves with the political administration of Donald Trump, trading nationalistic loyalty for deregulation, federal contracts, and massive infrastructure commitments. These deals include the "Stargate" project announced in 2025—a $500 billion joint venture between Oracle, SoftBank, and OpenAI to build massive AI datacenters across the United States.

## Key Definitions and Conceptual Terminology

### Techno-Nationalism

- **Definition**: A political and economic ideology that links technological innovation, private venture capital, and digital software development directly to national sovereignty, state power, and military competitiveness in an era of great-power rivalry.

### The "Democracy Paradox"

- **Definition**: The profound ethical hypocrisy wherein technology companies invoke the preservation of liberal democracy to legitimize the development of autonomous weapons and surveillance systems, while deploying those same tools in ways that erode civil liberties, expand domestic police powers, and facilitate human rights violations.

### The "Last Supper" (1993) vs. The "First Breakfast"

- **The "Last Supper"**: A historic 1993 meeting organized by Secretary of Defense Les Aspin where the Pentagon directed defense executives to consolidate their companies due to post-Cold War budget contractions, shrinking the defense industrial base from over fifty prime contractors to just five.
- **The "First Breakfast"**: A counter-concept coined by Palantir CTO Shyam Sankar calling for the dismantlement of legacy defense monopolies and the rapid scaling of competitive, venture-backed startups to revitalize America's military manufacturing base.

### The Procurement "Valley of Death"

- **Definition**: The prolonged, bureaucratic administrative gap separating private venture-backed technology research and development from the multi-year federal procurement cycles required to secure formal Department of Defense acquisition contracts.

### Effective Accelerationism (e/acc)

- **Definition**: A philosophical movement prominent in Silicon Valley advocating for the unrestricted, rapid advancement of artificial intelligence and technological capacity at all costs, explicitly dismissing societal risk management, ethical guardrails, and regulatory oversight.

## Concluding Summary

Tianyu M. Fang’s investigation in _"Silicon Valley Goes To War"_ demystifies the ideological journey of the American technology industry. By contextualizing the rise of modern defense technology within the historical continuum of the Cold War, Fang proves that the tech sector's recent militarization is not an aberration, but a structural realignment with its foundational origins. The anti-war, cyberlibertarian counterculture of the 1970s and the progressive "don't be evil" consumer internet boom of the 2000s were brief historical intervals, enabled by temporary geopolitical peace and expanding digital consumer markets. As those commercial markets reached maturity, the tech industry quickly returned to state patronage to guarantee financial growth.

Today, the convergence of hot wars abroad, a geopolitical arms race against China, and the rise of accelerationist ideology have fully legitimized the militarization of Silicon Valley. Founders, venture capitalists, and incubator directors now openly pursue defense contracts, building autonomous warships, missile systems, and surveillance algorithms. The historical protests of tech workers—exemplified by Google employees walking away from Project Maven in 2018—have been neutralized as corporate leadership across OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google systematically rewrote their ethical standards to compete for lucrative national security initiatives.

Ultimately, Fang warns that this wave of technological militarism carries profound perils for civil society. By invoking existential foreign threats to justify unconditional technical development, tech leaders have constructed a self-serving moral shield that obscures domestic surveillance, political manipulation, and complicity in international human rights violations. In trading its original aspirations of building an open, connected global community for the commercial profits of the national security state, Silicon Valley has entered an era of techno-nationalism that threatens the democratic freedoms it claims to defend.
