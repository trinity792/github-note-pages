# Mona Sloane – Predicted (Chapter 4: The Business of AI)

## Executive Summary

In Chapter 4 of _Predicted_, titled "The Business of AI," sociologist Mona Sloane presents a critical political-economic analysis of artificial intelligence as both a cultural myth and an emerging market force. Sloane challenges the prevailing corporate narrative that frames AI as an autonomous, inevitable engine of unprecedented economic productivity and labor efficiency. Despite widespread public belief that AI is generating massive industrial profits through automation, Sloane demonstrates that systemic macroeconomic productivity gains remain largely unproven. Instead, the modern business of AI functions through a profound financialized divergence between market valuation and actual corporate revenues—exemplified by generative AI firms like OpenAI commanding a $300 billion valuation (roughly thirty times its actual revenue) while operating at substantial financial losses. The commercial viability of AI rests on the speculative "prediction paradigm": the problematic assumption that an algorithmic prediction can be neatly packaged, priced, and circulated as a calculable commodity asset.

To dismantle the rhetoric surrounding the AI economy, Sloane investigates how AI businesses depend on pervasive regimes of extraction that operate across digital, human, and physical domains. Rejecting the common industry metaphor that "data is the new oil," she demonstrates that data is neither an infinite nor a naturally occurring natural resource; rather, data is the social product of an "ordinal society" in which ordinary citizens are structurally coerced into producing behavioral traces simply by participating in fundamental societal institutions. This data extraction pipeline is backstopped by an invisible, precarious global underclass of annotators and cleaners in the Majority World, while its physical infrastructure demands massive water and energy consumption, rare earth mineral extraction, and monopolistic semiconductor manufacturing. At its core, the prediction paradigm reorients public and private institutions—from universities and hospitals to schools—away from context-specific social utility and toward speculative institutional data hoarding.

Furthermore, Sloane exposes how the deployment of AI automation reinforces historical hierarchies of labor, social status, and servitude. Tracing the cultural history of automatons from classical mythology and the 18th-century Mechanical Turk to modern workplace technologies, she reveals that the determination of whether a job is "automatable" depends not on the actual technical difficulty or skill required by the task, but on the social status, perceived replaceability, and demographic privilege of the workers performing it. Through contrasting case studies—such as London black cab drivers facing Uber, delicate strawberry harvesting in indoor farms, low-volume recruiters battling algorithmic semantic search, and dermatologists deploying dermoscopy classification tools—Sloane demonstrates that AI reconfigures professional discretion along sharply gendered and status-driven lines. While high-status, historically male-dominated fields like medicine leverage AI to expand professional authority, lower-status, feminized professions like recruiting see their autonomy, meaning, and epistemic agency systematically stripped away by black-boxed predictive systems.

## The Political Economy of Prediction and AI Valuation

### The Disconnect Between Valuation and Productivity

- **The Myth of AI as an Immediate Engine of Productivity**: Popular culture and corporate marketing present AI as an unprecedented scaling technology that enables enterprises to achieve exponential productivity gains with reduced human labor. However, Sloane points out that there is virtually no empirical evidence demonstrating that AI has produced systemic macroeconomic growth or measurable aggregate productivity surges.
- **The Speculative Financialization of Generative AI**: Despite the absence of proven systemic productivity impacts, the global AI market was projected to reach $244 billion in 2025—eclipsing the entire 2023 gross domestic product of Greece ($243 billion). The disconnect between corporate valuation and revenue is starkly illustrated by OpenAI, which achieved a $300 billion valuation in early 2025 despite operating at thirty times its actual revenue and failing to turn a profit. This gap highlights how the business of AI is fueled by financial speculation and the allure of future predictive dominance rather than present commercial profitability.

### Predictions as Fictitious Assets and 21st-Century Capitalism

- **The Calculative Problem of Pricing a Prediction**: In capitalist market economies, exchange depends on calculating a specific price; however, AI companies face severe difficulty pricing their core output because an algorithmic prediction is not an easily tradable, discrete commodity. Unlike tangible consumer goods (such as food, shelter, or clothing), an AI prediction possesses no obvious standalone use value and is nonsensical when divorced from its operational context. Consequently, access to predictions must be marketed through layered subscription licenses and platform infrastructures.
- **AI as an Asset and Property Conflicts**: Framing AI as an asset—a private resource controlled by an entity to capture future economic rents—suggests the formation of new social relations centered on proprietary intellectual property rights. However, AI’s status as a stable asset remains legally and economically precarious due to fierce copyright lawsuits alleging that AI developers unlawfully appropriated original human works to assemble training datasets, alongside operational spending that drastically outstrips income.
- **Congruence with 21st-Century Capitalist Structures**: Sloane argues that the business of AI cannot be understood through traditional patterns of product sales, but must be viewed as an embodiment of contemporary capitalism's core tendencies: hyper-financialization, monopoly, monopsony (where a single buyer controls the market), the extreme concentration of wealth, and the prioritization of short-term shareholder value over democratic public interests.

## Data Extraction and the Material Foundations of AI

### The Fallacy of "Data as the New Oil" and Precarious Data Work

- **Deconstructing Clive Humby’s Resource Metaphor**: In 2006, marketing scientist Clive Humby famously coined the slogan "data is the new oil," drawing a parallel between 19th-century petroleum transportation booms and 21st-century digital commerce. This metaphor was subsequently co-opted by global elites, including Klaus Schwab at the World Economic Forum, to proclaim a data-driven "Fourth Industrial Revolution" championed by corporate executives like Sundar Pichai and Satya Nadella.
- **The Erasure of Human Data Labor**: While Humby noted that raw data—like unrefined crude oil—possesses no inherent value until it is cleaned, refined, and contextualized, the "data as oil" narrative erases the human labor of data production. It obscures the essential question of who actually works on the computational "rigs". Preparing data for modern machine learning requires massive, precarious armies of data annotators, content moderators, and dataset cleaners, predominantly located in the Majority World, who perform low-wage gig labor mediated through exploitative digital platforms.
- **Historical Origins of Behavioral Extraction**: Digital platforms originally viewed user data as operational exhaust rather than capital. It was only after the collapse of the dot-com bubble in the early 2000s that Google pivoted to using search logs to predict web clicking behavior, inventing targeted online advertising. Similarly, Facebook transitioned from an online college directory into an advertising giant by rendering social connections calculable for behavioral prediction.

### Institutional Data Financialization and the Limits of Data Ownership

- **Coerced Participation in the "Ordinal Society"**: Drawing on sociologists Marion Fourcade and Kieran Healy, Sloane notes that modern individuals inhabit an "ordinal society" centered on the continuous measurement, scoring, and ranking of human beings. Participating in modern civic life, employment, education, and healthcare requires interacting with digital infrastructures that extract personal data by default, converting societal participation into involuntary data production.
- **The Flaw of Individual Data Ownership Models**: Policy proposals suggesting that individuals be granted legal property rights to own and sell their personal data are conceptually flawed. Sloane argues that market-based individual ownership approaches fail because they treat data as fragmented property while ignoring the systemic macro-level ways in which data extraction and AI governance reshape society and entrench structural power imbalances.
- **Institutional Speculation and Data Brokers**: Commercial data brokers commodify internet user histories, cookies, and IP addresses, trading them based entirely on their imagined future predictive utility. This prediction paradigm has corrupted traditional public institutions—including universities, hospitals, and primary schools—encouraging them to view their records not as administrative necessities, but as speculative assets to be accumulated, monetized, and maintained via invasive AI collection software.

### Physical Extraction, Semiconductors, and Extraterrestrial Frontier Expansion

- **The Ecological and Material Costs of AI Compute**: Contrary to myths of digital immateriality, AI computation requires immense physical resources, including energy-intensive, water-consuming data centers that strain local municipal utilities and drive up electricity costs for surrounding communities.
- **Semiconductor Geopolitics and Rare Earth Extraction**: Modern AI models depend entirely on advanced semiconductor microchips containing tens of billions of microscopic transistors patterned onto thin silicon wafers. Manufacturing these chips requires multi-million-dollar lithography machinery and a global supply chain reliant on finite, non-renewable materials, including seventeen rare earth elements from the lanthanide series (elements 57 to 71), platinum, and rapidly depleting reserves of helium.
- **Billionaire Space Mining and Legal Voids**: Faced with terrestrial resource limits, tech billionaires—including Elon Musk, Jeff Bezos, and Richard Branson—have directed venture capital toward extraterrestrial space mining on the moon, Mars, and asteroids. Sloane emphasizes that these corporate actors are intentionally racing to establish private extraction infrastructures in the absence of international property treaties, seeking to capture space resources before democratic governments can establish regulatory frameworks.

## Valuing People: Automatons, Social Status, and the Politics of Automation

### Historical Automatons and the Ideology of Servitude

- **The Dual Impulses of Early Automation**: Early computational machines, such as Blaise Pascal’s 17th-century mechanical calculator built to assist his father with tax administration, were designed to automate tedious mathematical calculations rather than extract profit. However, the broader cultural history of humanoid automatons has historically been animated by the twin impulses of exoticism and a desire for subservient domestic labor.
- **Mythological and Colonial Roots of Servitude**: From the Greek mythological constructs of Talos and Daedalus’s living statues to Wolfgang von Kempelen’s 1770 Mechanical Turk, automatons have historically materialized social desires for captive, autonomous servants. The Mechanical Turk—an elaborate hoax concealing a human chess player inside an Ottoman-attired cabinet—symbolized the Habsburg court's imperial fascination with the "Orient," functioning, as Edward Said conceptualized, to simultaneously fetishize, dominate, and project subservience onto Middle Eastern populations. Today's commercial AI systems mirror this historical lineage by treating human workers as mechanical servants designed for corporate domination.

### The Social Construction of Skill: High-Skill vs. Low-Skill Distinctions

- **Symbolic Hoops vs. Actual Expertise**: Sloane demonstrates that the economic categorization of jobs into "high-skill" and "low-skill" occupations is an ideological construct rather than an objective measure of competence. A farmworker possesses vastly more physical and agricultural expertise in harvesting crops than an academic, yet agricultural labor is categorized as low-skill because the worker has not jumped through the formalized, institutional "symbolic hoops" of higher education.
- **The Replaceability Rationalization**: The belief that an occupation can and should be automated via AI does not stem from the technical feasibility of the task, but from the low social status and perceived replaceability of the human beings performing it. Designating an occupation as "low-skill" serves to legitimize wage depression, strip workers of dignity, and justify capital investments in automation.

### Case Studies in Devaluation: London Cabbies, Strawberry Harvesters, and White-Collar Generative AI

- **London Black Cab Drivers vs. Uber**: To earn a license, London black cab drivers must master "the Knowledge"—a grueling two-year pedagogical process requiring candidates to memorize 25,000 streets, 60,000 landmarks, and complex traffic patterns without GPS navigation aids, an achievement with a 50 percent failure rate that rivals medical or legal training. Rideshare platforms like Uber devalue this embodied cognitive skill by substituting algorithmic predictive navigation, eroding the cabbies' hard-won social status and prompting over 13,000 drivers to file a £330 million lawsuit against Uber in 2024. The social devaluation was formalized in May 2025 when the UK government announced the deployment of driverless robot taxis in London by 2027.
- **The Strawberry Picking Paradox**: Harvesting strawberries requires immense sensory skill, physical dexterity, and contextual judgment, as strawberries ripen unevenly, are delicate, and must be harvested at night when cooler temperatures protect the fruit. Despite agricultural workers vastly outperforming automated computer-vision harvesters, tech investors continue spending millions building complex, energy-hungry indoor vertical farms. These expenditures are incurred solely to force the natural environment into a predictable, structured layout that machines can handle, rationalized by the social devaluation of farmworkers.
- **The Devaluation of White-Collar Creative Labor**: Generative AI tools deployed in programming, copywriting, graphic design, and paralegal research demonstrate that automation devalues high-status knowledge work. By prioritizing predictive generation over human creation, workers are relegated to editing, checking, and debugging AI hallucinations, transforming creative professions into low-status maintenance tasks.

## Professional Discretion, Epistemic Clashes, and Connective Labor

### The Myth of Machine Objectivity and Workplace Meaning

- **Work as a Foundation of Identity and Dignity**: Sloane underscores that labor is not merely an economic transaction; it is a primary source of human identity, social meaning, autonomy, and personal dignity. The exercise of professional discretion—whether a janitor choosing a cleaning route, a teacher drafting a syllabus, or a doctor diagnosing an illness—is central to the human experience of agency.
- **Rationalizing AI via "Unbiased" Objectivity**: AI prediction is marketed to organizations as an objective, unbiased alternative to flawed human judgment. This rhetoric of machine objectivity is deployed to justify integrating predictive algorithms into high-stakes decision-making environments, including criminal justice sentencing, public welfare distribution, and employee hiring. In practice, AI does not eliminate human bias, but restructures the decision-making process by forcing human professionals to justify, interpret, or react to an algorithmic prediction that is generated before human evaluation occurs.

### Boolean Epistemology vs. Semantic Search in Recruiting

- **Boolean Epistemology as Craft Discretion**: In specialized, low-volume hiring, professional recruiters locate elite candidates by writing intricate Boolean search strings using logical operators (AND, OR, NOT) within candidate databases. Sloane terms this worldview "Boolean epistemology": recruiters develop an intuitive, deductive mastery over applicant databases by directly translating qualitative job requirements into deterministic keyword strings. This technical mastery forms the core of their professional discretion and workplace identity.
- **The Epistemic Clash with Semantic Search**: Modern recruitment databases have replaced transparent Boolean querying with AI-driven "semantic search". Instead of executing an operator's exact commands, semantic algorithms attempt to predict the "meaning" and intent of a recruiter's query behind an opaque computational black box. This creates an epistemic clash: the direct relationship between input and output vanishes, undermining the recruiter's ability to exercise professional judgment.
- **Tactics of Resistance and Bureaucratic Workarounds**: In response to algorithmic opacity, recruiters actively resist AI systems to claw back their decision-making space. Recruiters deliberately subvert search algorithms by repeatedly refreshing targeted profiles, inputting redundant queries, scrolling directly to the final pages of search results, and manually inferring demographic markers (such as gender) from candidate photographs. In attempting to regain agency from black-boxed systems, recruiters create additional labor, while subjective human biases find new ways to re-enter the hiring process.

### Connective Labor and Gendered Status: Recruiters vs. Dermatologists

- **Allison Pugh’s Concept of "Connective Labor"**: Both recruitment specialists and medical doctors engage in what sociologist Allison Pugh defines as "connective labor"—the essential, emotionally demanding work of recognizing, understanding, and "seeing" another human being in an institutional setting.
- **Dermatologists Leveraging Machine Objectivity**: When dermatologists utilize AI-assisted dermoscopy systems to classify suspicious moles, the technology does not displace their diagnostic authority. The AI provides cancer risk scores alongside contextual reference images, which physicians combine with embodied clinical experience—such as assessing mole pigmentation relative to a patient's natural hair color, an environmental nuance algorithms miss. Dermatologists actively co-opt the cultural authority of machine objectivity, using the AI's risk score as a persuasive rhetorical tool to convince reluctant patients to undergo necessary biopsies.
- **Gender Demographics and Structural Valuation**: Sloane explains the stark divergence between recruiters and dermatologists through the sociological intersections of gender and professional prestige. Human resources and recruitment is a heavily feminized profession (approximately 75 percent women in the United States) and is corporately categorized as an overhead cost center, making its tasks prime targets for total automation and deskilling. Conversely, clinical medicine remains historically high-status and male-dominated (in 2022, only 38 percent of active U.S. physicians were women), ensuring that medical AI is designed as a contextual clinical aid that preserves physician discretion rather than an aggressive management system that eliminates professional agency.

## Key Concepts and Definitions

### The Prediction Paradigm

- **Definition**: The governing economic and epistemological assumption of the AI industry that calculates, monetizes, and organizes human behavior and social life around algorithmic predictions, framing these probabilistic outputs as calculable asset units.

### Fictitious Assets

- **Definition**: Resources—such as proprietary machine learning models or algorithmic predictions—that are framed by corporations as private property capable of yielding future economic rents, despite possessing unstable legal ownership, unproven utility, and a reliance on stolen or coerced data.

### Boolean Epistemology

- **Definition**: Sloane’s concept describing the craft-based, deductive worldview of information retrieval specialists (such as specialized recruiters) who understand databases, evaluate human credentials, and exercise professional discretion through the transparent, deterministic logic of Boolean operators (AND, OR, NOT).

### Connective Labor

- **Definition**: Sociologist Allison J. Pugh’s term for the interpersonal, empathetic labor of emotionally recognizing, validating, and "seeing" another human being, an essential dimension of professions such as healthcare, education, and recruitment that is profoundly disrupted by standardized AI systems.

### The Ordinal Society

- **Definition**: Sociologists Marion Fourcade and Kieran Healy’s term for a digitally mediated social order that continuously classifies, scores, and ranks individuals based on behavioral data traces extracted across everyday civic, professional, and commercial interactions.

## Concluding Summary

Mona Sloane’s analysis in "The Business of AI" demystifies the dominant technological and economic narratives surrounding modern artificial intelligence. By situating AI prediction within the structural dynamics of 21st-century capitalism, Sloane exposes the fundamental contradiction between the tech industry’s astronomical market valuations and its lack of systemic macroeconomic productivity. Rather than functioning as a self-sustaining technological revolution, the business of AI operates through multi-tiered extraction: the uncompensated harvesting of human behavioral data, the exploitation of a precarious gig workforce in the Majority World, the consumption of critical water and energy supplies, and the ecological exhaustion of finite terrestrial and extraterrestrial minerals.

Crucially, Sloane reveals that the drive toward automation is an ideological project aimed at social discipline and labor devaluation rather than technical efficiency. Drawing on historical parallels with imperial automatons like the Mechanical Turk, she shows how the labels "high-skill" and "low-skill" are socially constructed to rationalize the replacement and degradation of workers deemed politically disposable. Whether through rideshare algorithms undermining the artisanal knowledge of London cabbies or tech investors pouring capital into indoor strawberry farms, the corporate impulse to automate frequently defies economic logic to entrench managerial control.

Ultimately, Sloane illustrates that the integration of AI prediction into the workplace is an epistemic struggle over professional discretion, human dignity, and gendered power. While high-status, male-dominated medical fields are equipped with AI tools that amplify professional authority, feminized service professions like recruiting have their craft knowledge erased by opaque semantic algorithms, forcing workers into covert resistance to preserve their agency. To understand the true costs of artificial intelligence, Sloane concludes, society must look past the grandiose rhetoric of global technological races and confront the exploitative labor practices, environmental damage, and institutional disempowerment that form the material foundation of the prediction paradigm.
