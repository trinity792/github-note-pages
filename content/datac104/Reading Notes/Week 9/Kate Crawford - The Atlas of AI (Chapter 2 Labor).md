# Kate Crawford – The Atlas of AI (Chapter 2: Labor)

## Executive Summary

In Chapter 2 of *The Atlas of AI*, Kate Crawford demystifies the popular illusion that artificial intelligence is an autonomous, clean, and purely immaterial technology. Crawford argues that modern AI systems are neither genuinely artificial nor inherently intelligent; instead, large-scale computation is deeply rooted in and sustained by the extraction, surveillance, and physical exploitation of human labor. Rather than asking whether robots will ultimately replace human workers, Crawford reorients the debate toward how human beings are increasingly being treated like robots—subjugated to algorithmic assessment, continuous physiological surveillance, and the relentless modulation of their time. Through field observations of Amazon fulfillment centers, she demonstrates how human workers serve as disposable "connective tissue" to perform fiddly physical tasks that robots cannot, while being bound to algorithmic pacing metrics that extract maximum bodily exertion at severe human cost.

Crawford situates contemporary workplace AI within a deep historical lineage of industrial discipline and labor management. She traces modern algorithmic tracking back through Adam Smith’s division of labor, Samuel Bentham’s 18th-century industrial inspection houses (which predated and inspired Jeremy Bentham’s Panopticon), Charles Babbage’s vision of the factory as a calculating engine where human workers are viewed merely as sources of error, and Frederick Winslow Taylor’s stopwatch-driven scientific management. Far from representing a radical break with the past, workplace AI intensifies these older methods by deploying machine learning, wearable sensors, and predictive analytics to observe and modulate the most intimate micromovements and bodily data of workers—territories of human life previously beyond managerial reach.

Furthermore, Crawford exposes the phenomenon of "Potemkin AI" and what writer Astra Taylor terms "fauxtomation"—technological facades where companies claim tasks are executed autonomously by sophisticated algorithms while secretly relying on vast, underpaid armies of digital pieceworkers. From Amazon Mechanical Turk (brazenly branded by Jeff Bezos as "artificial artificial intelligence") to startup digital assistants (like x.ai’s "Amy"), the illusion of algorithmic magic is backstopped by global crowdsourced laborers, content moderators enduring psychological trauma, and everyday users completing unpaid tasks like Google reCAPTCHAs. At the macro-infrastructural level, planetary tech monopolies like Google invent proprietary standards of time (such as Google Spanner’s "TrueTime") to synchronize global computation, echoing 19th-century railroad monopolies. In response, workers from Amazon warehouses in Minnesota to ride-share drivers and tech workers are organizing cross-sector resistance to challenge "the rate" and reclaim time sovereignty.

## The Factory Floor of Modern Logistics: Inside Amazon's Fulfillment Centers

### The Machine-Readable Warehouse and Bodily Degradation
* **Pervasive Temporal Surveillance**: At Amazon's 1.2-million-square-foot fulfillment center in Robbinsville, New Jersey, every second of work is monitored, quantified, and tallied by ubiquitous time clocks and digital scanners. Workers, referred to euphemistically as "associates," are restricted to a maximum of fifteen minutes "off-task" per ten-hour shift, alongside a single unpaid thirty-minute meal break, with every entry and exit from break rooms tracked.
* **Kiva Robotics and Human Connective Tissue**: While hundreds of bright orange Kiva robots glide smoothly across concrete floors in an effortless algorithmic ballet moving three-thousand-pound product racks, human workers are relegated to the role of "connective tissue". Associates are positioned behind bare chain-link fences to execute the irregular, fiddly tasks that computer vision and mechanical grippers cannot handle—such as rapidly identifying, picking, and scanning oddly shaped domestic consumer items.
* **Physical Toll and Painkiller Dispensaries**: To keep pace with the strict "picking rate"—the mandatory quota of items workers must scan and box within an allotted countdown—associates wear support bandages, knee braces, and wrist guards. The physical trauma generated by repetitive stress and relentless quotas is normalized by management through on-site vending machines stocked with over-the-counter painkillers for worker consumption.

### Algorithmic Pacing: "The Matrix" and Package "Time to Live"
* **Cardboard Boxes as Data Packets**: Crawford observes that just as digital internet networks operate around the "network packet" with an assigned "time to live" timestamp, Amazon treats cardboard boxes as domestic data packets with strict temporal lifespans dictated by customer shipping promises.
* **Optimization via "The Matrix"**: Amazon relies on a machine learning algorithm ironically dubbed "the matrix," which processes customer damage reports to automatically reassign box sizes, strengths, and packaging mailers without human intervention. While this automated tuning optimizes corporate shipping margins, it forces warehouse workers into a state of continuous adaptation, preventing them from developing bodily habits or utilizing tacit craft knowledge on the floor.

## Prehistories of Workplace AI and Industrial Discipline

### The Division of Labor: From Adam Smith to Fossil-Fueled Mechanization
* **Subdividing Tasks to Cheapen Labor**: In *The Wealth of Nations*, Adam Smith demonstrated that decomposing complex craft manufacturing into minute, subdivided steps enabled factories to replace skilled artisans with cheaper, lower-skilled workers using single-purpose tools, dramatically scaling output without increasing wage bills.
* **Workers as Appendages of the Machine**: With the introduction of fossil-fueled steam power, machines ceased to function as passive tools assisting workers; instead, continuous mechanical movement became the primary center of production, dictating the tempo of human labor. As Karl Marx observed in 1848, mechanization abstracts labor and turns the living worker into a mere "appendage of the machine".
* **The Struggle over Time as Currency**: As historian E. P. Thompson documented, the Industrial Revolution fundamentally replaced task-oriented rhythms with rigid clock-time discipline, enforced through fines, time sheets, and moral sermons that conceptualized time as an economic currency. This rigid enforcement prompted labor movements throughout the 19th century to organize around reducing the working day from sixteen hours, establishing time as a primary site of class struggle.

### The Panoptic Genealogy: The Inspection House and Plantation Oversight
* **Samuel Bentham’s Industrial Inspection House**: Contrary to the common assumption stemming from Michel Foucault’s *Discipline and Punish* that the Panopticon was conceived primarily for prisons, its design originated in manufacturing. In the 1780s, English naval engineer Samuel Bentham designed a circular "inspection house" for Prince Potemkin in Russia to allow supervisors on a raised central platform to monitor peasant workers and master shipbuilders. When visited by his brother Jeremy Bentham, this factory surveillance design was adapted into the famous panoptic prison.
* **Colonial Plantation Surveillance Regimes**: Crawford highlights that industrial surveillance also draws heavily from colonial slave plantations in the Americas. As Nicholas Mirzoeff details, the plantation economy relied on the overseer, whose explicit directive was to maintain constant, violent surveillance over enslaved workers to prevent even an instant of inaction. In modern workplaces, this coercive oversight is deputized to algorithmic monitoring systems, digital badges, and predictive tracking software.

## Theoretical Lineages of Automation and Disassembly

### Charles Babbage: The Factory as a Calculating Engine
* **The Computational Analogy of the Corporation**: In addition to inventing the Difference Engine and Analytical Engine, Charles Babbage conceptualized the industrial factory as an analogue to a computational system composed of specialized units whose internal labor is hidden within the finished output.
* **Labor as a Source of Error, Not Value**: Diverging from Adam Smith's labor theory of value, Babbage argued that economic value originates in the design and logistical organization of the manufacturing process rather than human labor. Under Babbage’s gaze, labor was viewed purely as a negative vulnerability—a frail, untrustworthy factor prone to fatigue, error, and resistance that must be contained, monitored, and disciplined through computational tracking.

### Chicago Disassembly Lines and Taylorist Scientific Management
* **The Chicago Meatpacking Disassembly Line**: In the 1870s, Chicago slaughterhouses mechanized the butchering of livestock using overhead rail trolleys, creating the "disassembly line". As labor historian Harry Braverman observed, this system perfected Babbage’s vision: workers were reduced to performing single, repetitive cuts on animal carcasses, making human labor entirely commoditized and interchangeable. While Upton Sinclair’s *The Jungle* prompted food safety regulations (the Meat Inspection Act of 1906), the structural exploitation of packinghouse workers was left untouched.
* **Frederick Winslow Taylor’s Stopwatch Surveillance**: In *Principles of Scientific Management*, Frederick Taylor introduced the stopwatch to decompose bodily actions into microscopic, timed motions. Taylor sought to eliminate wasted movement to derive maximum output at minimum cost, fulfilling Marx’s dictum that under industrial capitalism, "Time is everything, man is nothing; he is, at most, time’s carcass".
* **Fordism, McDonaldization, and Foxconn**: Henry Ford adapted Chicago's disassembly line into the automotive assembly line, scrutinizing worker "cycle times" with stopwatches. This Fordist model was later applied to service work by Ray Kroc at McDonald's, where sandwich assembly was timed to the exact second (e.g., twenty-two seconds to build a sandwich, fourteen seconds to wrap it) and all conceptual work was removed from task execution. In electronics manufacturing, Foxconn's rigid, militaristic management led to worker suicides in 2010, after which chairman Terry Gou publicly referred to his one million employees as "animals" to be managed.

## Potemkin AI, Fauxtomation, and the Hidden Labor Pipeline

### The Historical Mechanical Turk vs. Amazon Mechanical Turk
* **Wolfgang von Kempelen’s 1770 Hoax**: In 1770, Wolfgang von Kempelen built a chess-playing automaton known as the Mechanical Turk, displaying it before Austrian Empress Maria Theresa dressed as an "oriental sorcerer". The automaton was an elaborate visual illusion concealing a human chess master inside who manually executed the moves.
* **Amazon's "Artificial Artificial Intelligence"**: In 2005, Amazon launched its crowdsourcing platform, naming it Amazon Mechanical Turk after its own machine learning algorithms failed to identify duplicate retail product pages. Amazon chief executive Jeff Bezos branded this crowdsourcing infrastructure "artificial artificial intelligence"—an engine where unseen human workers bid pennies to complete repetitive microtasks to maintain the illusion of autonomous machine intelligence.

### Ghost Work and Fauxtomation
* **Ghost Work and Human-Fueled Automation**: Scholars Mary Gray and Sid Suri coin the term "ghost work," and Lilly Irani uses "human-fueled automation," to describe the hidden, distributed labor required to train, test, and correct AI models. An International Labour Organization study of 3,500 crowdworkers across 75 countries revealed that most workers earned well below local minimum wages despite being highly educated. Furthermore, commercial content moderators reviewing extreme violence and hate speech suffer lasting psychological trauma without adequate compensation or support.
* **Astra Taylor’s Fauxtomation**: Writer Astra Taylor defines "fauxtomation" as the deceptive practice where technology vendors oversell systems as automated when they merely coordinate, relocate, and disperse human labor across space and time. Examples include supermarket self-checkouts (shifting data-entry labor onto unpaid consumers) and startup virtual assistants.
* **Faking Artificial Intelligence**: Startups frequently deploy "Potemkin AI" as marketing facades to secure venture capital. For instance, startup x.ai claimed its personal assistant "Amy" scheduled meetings using autonomous AI; in reality, contract workers endured exhausting fourteen-hour shifts manually reading, annotating, and editing emails around the clock to sustain the illusion of automation. Similarly, Facebook's virtual assistant "M" relied on human editors reviewing every single message.

## The Macrophysics of Power: Algorithmic Scheduling and Planetary Time

### Algorithmic Scheduling and Modern Time Theft
* **Automated Demand Forecasting**: Retail and fast-food chains utilize predictive algorithms that monitor historical foot-traffic data to dynamically allocate worker shifts. Software automatically directs managers to cut staff the moment demand dips, forcing employees to wait unpaid near the premises on call.
* **Transferring Volatility onto Workers**: This algorithmic scheduling creates severe volatility—ranging from one-hour shifts to unpredictable on-call schedules—effectively committing corporate "time theft" by externalizing business volatility directly onto workers' personal and family lives.
* **Judy Wajcman on Silicon Valley’s Masculinized Temporality**: Sociology professor Judy Wajcman points out that the productivity and time-tracking software policing global workers is built by an elite Silicon Valley demographic that is disproportionately young, male, and committed to round-the-clock work. This tech monoculture naturalizes a hyper-masculinized benchmark of unencumbered productivity that ignores familial responsibilities and relies on the invisible care labor of others.

### Infrastructural Time: From the Telegraph to Google's "TrueTime"
* **The Racist Lineage of "Master-Slave" Clocks**: Computing protocols governing network synchronization (such as NTP and PTP) rely on a hierarchical clock architecture designated as "master" and "slave". This terminology originated in 1904 astronomical descriptions and was popularized in 1968 by John Kemeny and Thomas Kurtz for the Dartmouth timesharing system, embedding racist metaphors of mastery and control into computational architectures.
* **Google Spanner and Proprietary "TrueTime"**: Google’s globally distributed database, Spanner, coordinates planetary search, Gmail, and advertising across hundreds of data centers using a proprietary protocol called "TrueTime". By combining atomic clocks and GPS receivers, TrueTime manages network latency by intentionally slowing down operations to wait out clock drift uncertainty, bringing planetary computation under a single, corporate-controlled temporal standard.
* **The Telegraph and Imperial Temporal Arbitrage**: Crawford compares TrueTime to 19th-century railroad time and the telegraph. As communications theorist James Carey demonstrated, the telegraph unified time across vast geography, moving commerce from spatial arbitrage (buying low in one place, selling high in another) to temporal futures trading, while allowing imperial powers to exercise centralized administrative control over distant colonies.

## Worker Resistance and the Battle Over "The Rate"

### Historical Sabotage and Clock Destruction
* **Fordlandia's Rebellion (1930)**: In the 1930s, Henry Ford established Fordlandia in the Brazilian Amazon to secure a captive rubber supply chain; when management attempted to impose rigid factory time discipline, local workers rioted and smashed the facility's time clocks.
* **Pouget's Concept of Sabotage**: French anarchist Émile Pouget formulated "sabotage" as a deliberate factory "go-slow," wherein workers intentionally withdraw efficiency and slow their working pace to challenge time as a capitalist currency.

### The Amazon Struggle: Abdi Muse and the Stand Against "The Rate"
* **"The Rate" as an Inflexible Business Model**: At Amazon fulfillment centers, "the rate" is an algorithmic productivity quota set by remote engineers and corporate executives in Seattle. If a warehouse worker falls below the rate three times, they are automatically terminated regardless of tenure, compelling associates to skip bathroom breaks and endure severe spinal and joint strain.
* **Organizing at the Awood Center**: In Minnesota, community organizer Abdi Muse and the Awood Center mobilized East African immigrant warehouse workers to demand humane working conditions. When workers walked off the job at the Eagan delivery center, Amazon management agreed to discuss wages and box weights, but flatly refused to negotiate "the rate," stating: *"the rate is our business model. We cannot change that"*.
* **Cross-Sector Labor Solidarity and "We Are All Tech Workers"**: Crawford concludes by documenting how algorithmic surveillance has united disparate workforces—from Amazon warehouse associates and Uber drivers to white-collar software engineers—under the protest banner "We are all tech workers". However, Crawford warns against centering the identity of "tech work" in labor struggles, emphasizing that all workers deserve humane conditions regardless of whether their labor is branded as technology.

## Concluding Summary

In Chapter 2 of *The Atlas of AI*, Kate Crawford dismantles the pervasive myth of artificial intelligence as an autonomous, immaterial, and post-human technological frontier. By grounding her analysis in the physical reality of Amazon fulfillment centers, crowdsourced microwork platforms, and planetary server farms, Crawford demonstrates that AI is fundamentally powered by the exploitation, regimentation, and surveillance of human bodies. Rather than liberating society from rote labor, computational systems are frequently deployed to revive and intensify centuries-old mechanisms of industrial discipline—drawing directly from Adam Smith’s division of labor, Samuel Bentham's inspection houses, Charles Babbage's calculating factories, and Frederick Winslow Taylor's stopwatch management.

Crucially, Crawford exposes how corporate technology developers deploy "Potemkin AI" and "fauxtomation" to project an illusion of algorithmic magic to investors and consumers, while relying in secret on vast, underpaid armies of ghost workers, content moderators, and uncompensated end-users to keep systems operational. At the infrastructural scale, tech conglomerates like Google construct proprietary forms of absolute time, such as TrueTime, to coordinate global operations, consolidating an unprecedented macrophysics of power that disciplines human temporality across the planet.

Ultimately, Crawford highlights that the core struggle of contemporary automation centers on time sovereignty and human dignity. From historical riots smashing time clocks in Fordlandia to Amazon warehouse associates striking against algorithmic productivity rates in Minnesota, workers are actively resisting computational control. Crawford calls for broad, cross-sector labor solidarity, reminding us that the future of work must not be dictated by algorithmic optimization, but by the collective demand for a just, humane social order.
