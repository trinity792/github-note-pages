# Making Up People: Clinical Classifications and the Looping Effect
## Executive Summary
In "Making Up People," philosopher Ian Hacking explores the dynamic nominalist view of human classification, arguing that the social and human sciences do not merely discover pre-existing categories of people but actively bring them into existence. This phenomenon, termed **"making up people,"** occurs through a **"looping effect"** where the act of classifying individuals alters their self-conception, behavior, and social interactions. As classified individuals change in response to these scientific and administrative labels, they become a **"moving target,"** which in turn forces experts to revise their diagnostics, knowledge systems, and institutional practices.

To dissect this interactive process, Hacking establishes a **five-part analytical framework** consisting of the classification itself, the people classified, the institutions that manage them, the conjectural and expert knowledge surrounding them, and the professionals who validate these categories. He illustrates this dynamic through two primary case studies: **Multiple Personality Disorder** (now Dissociative Identity Disorder), which emerged as a transient mental illness shaped by clinical and talk-show interest, and the **Autistic Spectrum**, which expanded to include "high-functioning autists" who subsequently developed their own identity narratives. Through these examples, Hacking distinguishes between the historical existence of a biological condition and the historical emergence of a specific **"way to be a person"** in society.

Hacking outlines **ten distinct "engines" of discovery, practice, and administration**—ranging from counting, quantifying, and medicalizing to bureaucratizing and identity reclamation—that drive the creation and modification of human classifications. He contrasts these engines' effects on **"real kinds"** (like autism, which exhibits a vast, multidimensional web of shared characteristics) with **"finite kinds"** (like obesity, where individuals share little beyond the physical trait itself). Ultimately, Hacking’s framework demonstrates that while the tools of the human sciences are objective, the subjects they target are perpetually in motion, reshaping the moral and social landscapes of identity, suicide, and intelligence.
## The Epistemological Framework of Dynamic Nominalism

### Static vs. Dynamic Nominalism

- **Static Nominalism**: Traditional nominalism is static, treating classifications as fixed, convenient labels applied to stable, unchanging targets in nature. Under this view, classifications simply group pre-existing entities without altering their fundamental properties or behaviors.
- **Dynamic Nominalism**: Hacking introduces dynamic nominalism, which asserts that names and the named interact in a continuous, co-constitutive feedback loop. The categories established by the human sciences do not remain static because human beings are self-aware agents who react to being classified, thereby reshaping the category itself.

### The "Looping Effect" and "Moving Targets"

- **The Moving Target**: Because individuals modify their actions, feelings, and self-understanding in response to being classified, the scientific "target" of inquiry is perpetually in motion. What scientists find out about a group of people may be true at the time, but the very dissemination of that knowledge alters the group's behavior, rendering prior classifications obsolete or incomplete.
- **Sentence A vs. Sentence B**: To clarify the historical reality of these classifications, Hacking proposes two distinct assertions using multiple personality and autism as guides:
    
    - **Sentence A**: _"There were no multiple personalities in 1955; there were many in 1985"_. This statement triggers unproductive, heated debates between realists (who claim the condition was simply undiagnosed) and skeptics (who claim the condition is entirely fabricated).
    
    - **Sentence B**: _"In 1955 this was not a way to be a person, people did not experience themselves in this way... but in 1985 this was a way to be a person, to experience oneself, to live in society"_. Hacking argues that while Sentence A is highly controversial, Sentence B is demonstrably true for both multiple personality and high-functioning autism, as these identities did not exist as active, lived social realities prior to their institutional formulation.

## The Five-Part Framework of Human Classification

Hacking models the process of making up people through five interacting components that sustain any clinical or social classification:

- **(a) The Classification**: The specific label or diagnosis (such as multiple personality or autism) associated with a recognized condition or "disorder".
- **(b) The People**: The individuals who are subjected to the classification, often described as unhappy, unable to cope, or otherwise deviating from established norms.
- **(c) The Institutions**: The organizational scaffolding—including clinics, academic societies, talk shows, and training programs—that propagate, validate, and manage the classification.
- **(d) The Knowledge**: The body of conjectural and expert beliefs disseminated within these institutions, consisting of both professional assertions and popular understandings (e.g., the belief that multiple personality is caused by childhood sexual abuse).
- **(e) The Experts**: The professionals who generate, judge, and apply this knowledge within legitimizing institutional contexts to advise, treat, or control the classified individuals.

## The Ten Engines of Human Classification

Hacking identifies ten engines divided into tools of scientific discovery, clinical practice, administration, and social resistance, which collectively shape human kinds:

### Engines of Discovery (1 through 7)

- **1. Count!**: The institutional drive to census populations. Hacking contrasts autism, where rising numbers (from 4.5 to 57 per 10,000) provoke debates over whether the biological prevalence is increasing or if diagnostics have simply expanded, with obesity, which has experienced a genuine global surge.
- **2. Quantify!**: The development of metric scales. Obesity is heavily quantified using bathroom scales and the Body Mass Index (BMI)—calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. Conversely, autism resists direct quantification, as deficits in social communication are difficult to measure on a simple numerical scale.
- **3. Create Norms!**: The establishment of developmental and medical benchmarks. Drawing on Georges Canguilhem’s _The Normal and the Pathological_, Hacking notes how pediatric medicine established child development norms (such as expected ages for speech or tying shoelaces), framing deviations like autism as "developmental disorders".
- **4. Correlate!**: The statistical association of variables. Originating with Francis Galton's correlation coefficient, the human sciences correlate classifications with risk factors (e.g., correlating BMI with life expectancy, or autism with infant testosterone levels and male gender).
- **5. Medicalise!**: The translation of deviance into clinical diagnoses. Behaviors once viewed through moral or cultural lenses—such as obesity (formerly seen as gluttony) or child abuse—are systematically brought under the authority of clinical medicine.
- **6. Biologise!**: The attribution of classifications to neurological or chemical causes. Biologizing provides a significant moral benefit by relieving classified individuals of personal moral responsibility; for instance, attributing overeating to a chemical imbalance reframes it from a moral failing to a medical condition.
- **7. Geneticise!**: The search for genetic markers underlying human behavior. This engine drives researchers to trace biological traits back to genetic anomalies, seeking permanent physical anchors for complex social behaviors.

### Engines of Practice, Administration, and Resistance (8 through 10)

- **8. Normalise!**: The clinical effort to bring deviants back toward the established norm. This is achieved through behavioral therapies (in the case of autism) or pharmaceutical interventions (such as anti-craving medications for obesity).
- **9. Bureaucratise!**: The administrative sorting of individuals by state apparatuses. Well-intentioned school and welfare bureaucracies identify children with developmental delays to assign them special resources, but the rigid bureaucratic criteria used to do so actively shape and reinforce what it means to be "autistic".
- **10. Reclaim Our Identity!**: The collective resistance of classified individuals against experts and institutions. Marginalized groups actively seek to seize control of their labels, as seen in gay pride movements, fat-acceptance groups (like the French _Groupe de Réflexion sur l'Obésité et le Surpoids_ or GROS), and proposals for an "Autism Liberation Front".

## Comparative Case Studies: Real Kinds vs. Finite Kinds

### Real Kinds (Autism and the Spectrum)

- **John Stuart Mill's Distinction**: Hacking utilizes J.S. Mill's distinction to analyze human classifications. A "real kind" (or natural kind) is a category whose members share an infinite, open-ended series of common properties (e.g., horses or phosphorus).
- **Autism as a Real Kind**: Hacking suggests that autism behaves like a real kind. Parents of autistic children often assert that "autism is who my son is, not just a characteristic," because those on the autistic spectrum share a deep, multidimensional web of behaviors, cognitive styles, and hidden neuro-biological traits.
- **High-Functioning Autism as a Moving Target**: The historical trajectory of autism shifted from a rare, narrowly defined childhood condition in 1943 to an expansive "autistic spectrum" by the late 20th century. This expansion allowed individuals who "recovered" or developed compensatory social skills to establish a new way of being: the "high-functioning autist" (epitomized by Temple Grandin).

### Finite Kinds (Obesity and Characterization)

- **Defining Finite Kinds**: A "finite kind" is a category whose members share nothing in common except the single defining characteristic used to classify them (e.g., "white things" have nothing in common except their color).
- **Obesity as a Finite Kind**: Hacking argues that obesity is generally a finite kind. Obese or overweight individuals typically share nothing in common besides their high BMI; being overweight is an "enduring property" or characteristic, not a core identity or "who the person is". However, Hacking concedes that if specific genetic anomalies are discovered for certain subcategories of obesity, those subcategories might behave more like real kinds.

## Societal Repercussions: Suicide, Genius, and IQ

### The Changing Face of Suicide

- **The Intertwined History of Suicide**: Hacking notes that our modern understanding of suicide as a "cry for help" tied to clinical depression is a historically contingent arrangement of feelings and meanings co-constructed with medical and statistical sciences since 1825.
- **Laudable and Confusing Anomalies**: Because our cultural knowledge of suicide is heavily medicalized and individualized, society experiences profound conceptual confusion when confronted with historical anomalies or modern geopolitical phenomena like suicide bombing, which operates under a completely different logic of asymmetric resistance.

### The Measurement and Expulsion of Genius

- **Measuring the Immeasurable**: Historically, "genius" was a fluid, romantic concept mapping the unique fantasies of different eras. However, the rise of statistical intelligence testing (initiated by Galton and formalized by the US Army in 1917) forced genius onto a rigid linear scale.
- **Manipulating the Curve**: IQ tests were designed statistically to produce a normal distribution with a mean of 100. When women initially scored higher (with a mean of 105), psychometricians deliberately modified test questions to make them harder for women, artificially adjusting the results to keep both male and female means at 100. Hacking concludes that by measuring genius on linear scales, the statistical sciences effectively expelled it from our culture.

## Exploration via the Human Contexts and Ethics (HCE) Toolkit

### Power

- **Expert Jurisdictions and Classification Control**: Power manifests as the asymmetric capacity of professionals and institutions to define human normalcy and deviance. By determining diagnostic criteria, psychiatrists and school administrators structure how individuals are treated, educated, and legally recognized.
- **Gender Bias in Metric Design**: The historical adjustments made to IQ test questions to lower women's mean scores from 105 to 100 illustrate how scientific tools are non-neutral; they are actively configured by those in power to maintain existing socio-political hierarchies.

### Sociotechnical Systems

- **The Materiality of Classification**: Classifications do not exist as abstract concepts; they are tightly coupled with material technologies (bathroom scales, IQ tests, health insurance cards) and sprawling administrative bureaucracies (clinics, public schools, life insurance actuarial systems).
- **The Ontario Health Card Check**: This intertwining is exemplified by the skeptical Ontario physician who refused to treat "multiple personalities" by checking the patient's photo ID card, asserting, _"This is the person I am treating, nobody else"_. The physical identification card becomes the sociotechnical anchor that legally overrules the fluid, self-reported identity of the patient.

### Identity/Positionality

- **Categorical Coercion and Lived Experience**: Hacking's formulation of "Sentence B" directly addresses positionality: how a classification shapes not only how society treats an individual, but also how that individual experiences their own selfhood and interacts with friends, family, and employers.
- **Biographical Looping**: Individuals classified with a mental illness or developmental disability undergo a profound shift in identity. For example, multiples in the 1980s "obligingly retrieved" memories of childhood abuse to fit the clinical etiologies established by their therapists, showing how deeply positionality is negotiated within scientific systems.

### Narratives

- **The Construction of Clinical Mythologies**: The rapid rise of multiple personality diagnoses was fueled by highly sensationalized media narratives, particularly afternoon talk shows (like Oprah Winfrey and Geraldo Rivera), which popularized the pseudo-Freudian narrative of repressed memories.
- **Counter-Narratives of Liberation**: The emerging resistance among high-functioning autists and overweight individuals represents a powerful narrative shift. By demanding that experts _"stop trying to make us like you,"_ organizations like the "Autism Liberation Front" reject the dominant medical narrative of "disorder" or "pathology" in favor of a narrative of "disability" and neurological diversity.
