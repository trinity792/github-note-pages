# David F. Noble – Social Choice in Machine Design: The Case of Automatically Controlled Machine Tools

## Executive Summary

In _"Social Choice in Machine Design: The Case of Automatically Controlled Machine Tools,"_ historian David F. Noble challenges the pervasive doctrine of technological determinism, which falsely portrays technological advancement as an autonomous, neutral, and purely logical progression. Examining the post-World War II automation of metalworking machine tools, Noble demonstrates that the design and ultimate dominance of numerical control (N/C) over alternative technologies was not dictated by market efficiency, technical necessity, or natural cost-minimization. Instead, the trajectory of machine tool automation was actively shaped by social choices embedded within both horizontal relations of production (power dynamics between large defense contractors and small commercial shops) and vertical relations of production (class struggles for authority between management and skilled shop-floor labor).

Noble contrasts two competing technological paths developed in the late 1940s: "record-playback" and "numerical control". Record-playback recorded the actual physical movements of a skilled machinist producing a prototype part onto magnetic tape, functioning as a "skill multiplier" that kept the intelligence, judgment, and operational control of production firmly in the hands of workers on the shop floor. Conversely, numerical control (N/C) translated mathematical engineering blueprints into numerical code to directly drive machine servomechanisms. Promoted heavily by John Parsons, MIT's Servomechanisms Laboratory, and the U.S. Air Force—which poured over $62 million into N/C research, development, and procurement between 1949 and 1959—N/C was engineered specifically to circumvent human machinists, transferring operational planning and process control from the shop floor to centralized managerial offices.

Ultimately, Noble reveals that N/C was selected and standardized despite its immense cost, software complexity, and initial unreliability because it embodied an engineering ideology of control that mirrored capitalist management's deep distrust of organized labor. Driven by a desire to eliminate shop-floor "pacing" (workers controlling output speeds), bypass militant unions, and replace skilled craftsmen with low-wage "button-pushers," corporate management embraced N/C as an instrument of social control. However, Noble concludes by stressing that managerial intentions do not automatically dictate shop-floor reality; because early N/C machinery was fragile and prone to costly breakdowns, management was forced to retain skilled machinists on the floor to maintain production quality, proving that human expertise remains indispensable despite automation.

## Critique of Technological Determinism and the Social Shaping of Technology

### The Myth of Autonomous Technical Evolution

- **Rejection of Deterministic Narratives**: Noble explicitly rejects technological determinism, which treats industrial concentration and the centralization of managerial authority as inevitable, logical outcomes of technological progress. Determinists view technology as an external cause and social organization as its passive effect. Noble argues that this perspective masks the reality that technologies are developed under specific social auspices and reflect the immediate interests and ideologies of those who fund and design them.
- **Going Behind the Economic Mask**: Conventional economic accounts assume that technologies succeed purely because they minimize production costs through an "invisible hand". Noble asserts that critical historians must look behind the economic category of cost-minimization to uncover the underlying social relations of power. In many cases, massive capital investments in automation are made not out of meticulous balance-sheet calculations, but on a quasi-religious faith that substituting capital for labor is inherently superior.

### Horizontal and Vertical Relations of Production

- **Horizontal Relations (Inter-Firm Power Dynamics)**: The horizontal relations of production encompass the market dynamics between large, well-capitalized corporate manufacturers (such as prime defense contractors) and small, independent job shops. The design of N/C hardware and software reflected the specific needs of large aircraft manufacturers and military buyers, creating massive financial and technical barriers to entry that disadvantaged small metalworking firms constituting 83 percent of the industry.
- **Vertical Relations (Class Power on the Shop Floor)**: The vertical relations of production refer to the ongoing struggle for power, authority, and control between corporate management and shop-floor labor. The choice to pursue numerical control rather than record-playback was fundamentally driven by management's desire to alter these vertical relations by stripping skilled machinists of their operational autonomy.

## Competing Paths of Automation: Record-Playback vs. Numerical Control (N/C)

### Record-Playback as a Skill Multiplier

- **Mechanics of Record-Playback**: Developed in 1946–1947 by General Electric (directed by Lowell Holmes), Gisholt (the "Factrol" system designed by Hans Trechsel), and smaller firms, record-playback automated general-purpose machine tools by recording a machinist's manual movements on magnetic tape. Once the machinist produced a flawless prototype part, the tape played back the recorded electrical signals to automatically replicate identical parts.
- **Preserving Worker Autonomy and Skill**: Record-playback acted as a "multiplier of skill" and a mechanism for obtaining repeatability without displacing the worker. Because tape preparation occurred directly at the machine, the machinist remained the sole source of production intelligence, retaining full authority over cutting speeds, tool feeds, and operational adjustments. Technical tests confirmed that record-playback was fully as reliable and accurate as N/C, holding tolerances to within a thousandth of an inch.

### Numerical Control (N/C) and the Transfer of Control

- **Origins at Parsons and MIT**: Conceived by John Parsons in 1949 to machine helicopter rotor blades and complex aircraft wing panels, N/C bypassed manual operation entirely. Parsons subcontracted research to MIT's Servomechanisms Laboratory (led by William Pease and James McDonough), which demonstrated the first N/C vertical milling machine in 1952.
- **Formal Circumvention of the Machinist**: N/C converted blueprint specifications into a mathematical representation of the part, calculated tool paths across up to five axes, and translated these coordinates into numerical code stored on tape. N/C was based on an entirely different manufacturing philosophy: it formally circumvented the machinist, transferring the origin of production intelligence from the shop floor to office programmers.

### The Role of Military Subsidies and Air Force Standardization

- **Massive U.S. Air Force Funding**: Between 1949 and 1959, the U.S. Air Force underwrote N/C development with over $62 million in subsidies. In 1955, the Air Force created an artificial market by mandating N/C specifications in its budget for stockpiling machine tools, purchasing over 100 N/C machines for prime aircraft contractors and paying them to learn the technology.
- **Imposition of the APT Software Standard**: Developed by Douglas Ross at MIT in 1956, the Automatically Programmed Tools (APT) software language was designed to meet Air Force demands for complex 5-axis contour machining and national inter-factory standardization. Although highly flexible, APT was extremely complex, cumbersome, prone to error, and required massive mainframe computers. By forcing APT as the industry standard, the Air Force suppressed simpler alternative programming languages (such as NUFORM). This created a permanent dependency on expensive computers and elite programmers, effectively pricing small, independent job shops out of the market.

## Managerial Ideology, Class Conflict, and Shop-Floor Reality

### The Ideology of Control and Distrust of Labor

- **Engineering Ideology as Capitalist Distrust**: Noble highlights that the drive for total automation was fueled by an engineering ideology of control that equated human agency with "human error". This engineering distrust of workers was a direct manifestation of capital's antagonism toward labor. Engineers and managers sought technical systems that promised power, order, predictability, remote control, and the illusion of an "automatic factory".
- **"Do What We Say, Not What We Do"**: When GE demonstrated record-playback to corporate executives, management rejected it despite its technical success, complaining that it recorded whatever the machinist did. Management demanded a technology that enforced what management commanded: _"Give us something that will do what we say, not what we do"_. N/C was explicitly embraced as a management system rather than a mere metal-cutting technology because it enabled executives to dictate production from executive suites without "getting their hands dirty or arguing".

### Pacing, Scientific Management, and Union Struggles

- **Eliminating "Systematic Soldiering"**: Since the 19th century, labor-intensive machine shops were centers of skilled worker power, where machinists engaged in "pacing" (or "systematic soldiering")—controlling machine speeds and outputs to protect health, maintain piece-rates, ensure safety, and express autonomy. While Frederick Taylor's scientific management attempted to stop pacing by transferring knowledge into management handbooks, machinists retained effective control because machining relies on machine-based craft skills.
- **Bypassing Union Resistance**: The development of N/C occurred during a period of intense postwar labor militancy, marked by major strikes across GE, Bell Aircraft, Boeing, and Douglas Aircraft. Corporate managers viewed N/C as a technological extension of Taylorism that could break union leverage. By replacing troublesome skilled craftsmen with tractable "button-pushers" and replacing contested time-studies with unyielding "tape time," management sought to permanently eliminate pacing and defeat organized unions (such as the UE, UAW, and IAM).

### The Limits of Managerial Intentions on the Shop Floor

- **Reality Cannot Be Extrapolated from Intention**: Noble cautions that social reality cannot be deduced directly from managerial designs or technological capability. Although management intended for N/C to fully deskill the workforce and automate production, the actual shop-floor reality proved vastly different.
- **The Persistence of Skilled Labor**: Because early N/C machinery and APT software were highly unreliable, fragile, and prone to catastrophic tool crashes, management quickly realized that unskilled "button-pushers" caused devastating financial losses. To protect expensive capital equipment and ensure finished parts met exact tolerances, manufacturers were forced to retain highly skilled machinists on N/C equipment, proving that the intelligence of production could not be fully built into the machine or removed from the shop floor.

## Key Definitions and Core Concepts

### Numerical Control (N/C)

- **Definition**: An automated manufacturing technology that translates mathematical blueprint coordinates into coded numerical instructions (on tape or cards) to directly drive machine tool servomechanisms, transferring production planning from shop-floor machinists to office programmers.

### Record-Playback

- **Definition**: An alternative automation technology developed in the late 1940s that recorded the actual physical machine movements executed by a skilled machinist onto magnetic tape, allowing identical parts to be reproduced automatically while preserving the machinist's role as the source of production intelligence.

### Automatically Programmed Tools (APT)

- **Definition**: A complex, highly flexible computer programming language created at MIT in 1956 under Air Force sponsorship. APT became the mandatory industry standard for N/C contour programming, but its extreme computational complexity favored large defense contractors while pricing smaller machine shops out of the market.

### Pacing / "Systematic Soldiering"

- **Definition**: The informal practice wherein skilled workers collectively regulate their working speed and output to exercise autonomy, prevent piece-rate cuts, avoid layoffs, and resist managerial coercion.

### Ideology of Automation

- **Definition**: A pervasive managerial and engineering worldview rooted in capitalist class relations that views human labor as inherently unreliable, treats worker judgment as "human error," and holds an unexamined, faith-like belief that replacing human agency with centralized mechanical control is always inherently superior.
