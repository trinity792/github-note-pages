# Why Are Certain Countries Poor?

## Executive Summary

Andrew Brooks challenges mainstream development frameworks that conceptualize global poverty as a series of linear historical stages. He systematically critiques Walt Rostow's highly influential "stages of economic growth" model and the UN’s Human Development Index (HDI) ladder, both of which posit that impoverished nations are simply "behind" the West and must climb the same path of modernization to catch up. Brooks argues that this comparative approach represents a developmental fantasy that ignores how the wealth of affluent Western nations is historically and structurally codependent on the active underdevelopment and exploitation of the Global South.

To move beyond these "bullshit comparisons" and simplistic models of environmental or cultural determinism, Brooks advocates for a "relational comparison" framework. Rather than looking strictly inside a nation's borders to blame internal factors like corruption or geography, a relational approach analyzes the global political-economic networks, core-periphery dynamics, and historical legacies of colonialism that actively produce and maintain uneven development. He demonstrates this structural lock-in through the concept of the "development of underdevelopment," where Western core economies leverage control over international banking, trade policies, and intellectual property to keep peripheral nations subordinate.

Brooks illustrates his relational framework through a devastating case study of healthcare disparities between Sierra Leone and the United Kingdom. While Western volunteers frequently construct historical analogies that compare Sierra Leonean hospitals to 19th-century "hellholes" or Civil War medical tents, Brooks argues that this "going back in time" narrative is fundamentally incorrect. The severe shortage of clinicians in Sierra Leone's Connaught Hospital is directly linked to the contemporary "medical brain drain". Underpaid local nurses and doctors, trained to exceptionally high standards, rationally emigrate to meet the severe staffing demands of the UK's National Health Service (NHS), revealing that the high health rankings of Western nations are relationally subsidized by the active depletion of resources in the poorest countries.

## Deconstructing Comparative Development Models

### The Critique of the Human Development Index (HDI)

- **Beyond Raw GDP**: The UN’s Human Development Index represents a step forward by combining statistics on life expectancy, education, and per capita income rather than reducing development to raw economic growth (GDP). As evidenced in Mozambique, raw economic growth can expand the size of the national economic cake without actually increasing the slice received by the impoverished.
- **The Tax Haven Distortion**: Despite its advantages, the HDI remains highly flawed and over-reliant on national economic performance, which can produce severely distorted comparisons. For example, in 2020 and 2023, Ireland was ranked second and eighth in the world for development, ahead of affluent nations like Switzerland and Britain. This ranking was heavily exaggerated by a Gross National Income (GNI) per capita of over $68,371 (rising to over $76,169 in 2023), which was inflated by the inflow of profits to multinational corporations taking advantage of Ireland's controversial tax haven status. This foreign wealth did not reflect or lift the actual living standards of most Irish people.

### Walt Rostow’s Stages of Economic Growth and the Linear Fantasy

- **The Five-Stage Ladder**: Writing in 1960, American economist Walt Rostow theorized development as a series of five historical stages: traditional society, preconditions for take-off, take-off, drive to maturity, and the age of high mass consumption. Using Britain and the post-WWII United States as model societies, Rostow's model placed poorest African nations in the "traditional society" stage, conceptualizing them as being fifty or a hundred years "behind" the West on a teleological ladder of progress.
- **The Erasure of Exploitative Interdependence**: Rostow’s model fails because it treats national processes in isolation, ignoring the global trade relationships that drove Western wealth. High mass consumption in Europe and America was built upon the extraction of cheap natural resources from the Global South (e.g., Mozambican cotton for Portuguese textiles, Indian tea, and Liberian rubber for American cars). Britain and the US triggered their "take-offs" via colonial exploitation; because there is nobody left for the Global South to colonize, the idea that they can follow the same upward path is a fantasy. Poorer nations do not exist on a separate timeline or in a "waiting room" of history; they are part of a shared global history that produces uneven development.

## Deterministic Explanations of Poverty

### Environmental Determinism as an Administrative Shield

- **The Geography "Prison"**: Environmental determinists, such as Tim Marshall (_Prisoners of Geography_), argue that physical geography acts as a "prison" that overwhelmingly defines and constrains what a nation can be. This intuitive argument is widely discredited by academic geographers as naive.
- **The Reality of Economic Geography**: Environmental determinism ignores the fact that many seemingly inhospitable locations have produced highly prosperous societies. Florida (humid and hurricane-prone), Hong Kong (poor geology and drinking water supply), and the Netherlands (low-lying and flood-vulnerable) have all escaped their physical limitations because of their broader, highly strategic economic and political geography.

### Cultural Stereotypes and the "Colonizer's Model of the World"

- **Cultural Hierarchies**: Modernization theories historically categorized early non-Western societies as culturally inferior, arguing that their "particularistic" values (such as strong kinship and family ties) were obstacles to development compared to Western "universalistic" values. This "colonizer's model of the world" continues to be replicated in mainstream culture, such as computer strategy games like _Civilization_, which reduce cultural advancement to a linear march from the Stone Age to the future.
- **The Selective Blindness of Comparative Hierarchies**: In the 1960s, Mozambican cotton farmers were framed as "backward" compared to Portuguese industrial workers, even though both worked within the same economic system. This was a distorted comparison: the labor of Mozambican peasants actively enabled the factories of Lisbon to modernize, while many Portuguese colonial settlers arriving in Mozambique were themselves so impoverished that they did not own shoes.

## Relational Comparison and the Core-Periphery Dynamic

### The Development of Underdevelopment

- **Moving to Relational Models**: Brooks argues that comparisons must be relational, focusing on the historical and contemporary political-economic connections between countries rather than treating nations as isolated boxes.
- **The Structural Lock-In**: Capitalist relations between affluent core nations and marginalized peripheral nations generate a continuous "development of underdevelopment". Colonialism established crucial comparative advantages that protect rich economies—such as control of global banking networks, elite education sectors, legal systems, capital reserves, and dominance over international bodies like the UN and WTO.
- **The Subordinate Periphery**: The peripheral regions of the world are locked into a permanent subordinate status, committed to serving core economies by providing cheap natural resources, agricultural products, tourism, and low-cost migrant labor. This pattern is sustained by unequal terms of trade, indebtedness, and the forced transplanting of neoliberal policies.

### Escaping the Trap: The Geopolitics of South Korea

- **Realignment of Geopolitical Relations**: When countries like South Korea successfully escaped poverty, the crucial ingredient was not internal culture or resources, but a fundamental realignment of their relations with the wider geopolitical world.
- **Nurturing Domestic Autonomy**: South Korea benefited from preferential relationships with Washington, controlled its own imports and exports, avoided indebtedness, and nurtured domestic industries rather than allowing its economy to be exploited by a neocolonial power. Its distinct indigenous culture and lack of natural resources did not hold the nation back from strategically inserting itself into the global economy.

## Case Study: The Relational Architecture of Global Healthcare

### The Illusion of "Going Back in Time" in Sierra Leone

- **The Health Chasm**: The disparities between the UK and Sierra Leone are stark: infant mortality is four per 1,000 births in the UK versus seventy-nine in Sierra Leone, and life expectancy is eighty-one versus fifty-four years respectively.
- **The Anachronistic Trap**: Western medical volunteers at Connaught Hospital in Freetown frequently frame the lack of resources (such as anemic patients dying from lack of basic blood transfusions) as "going back in time" or being worse than US Civil War hospitals. Brooks argues that this historical framing is deeply misleading. Wards with poor hygiene and a lack of drugs are not museums of the past; they exist in a highly globalized present where patients use smartphones, access the internet, and discuss contemporary English football.

### The Medical Brain Drain and the NHS Subsidy

- **Highly Trained, Underpaid Clinicians**: The primary problem in Sierra Leone is not the quality of local clinicians, whose anatomy knowledge and technical skills are trained to an exceptionally high standard. Rather, because local government salaries are too low to support a basic standard of living, an estimated 60 percent of Sierra Leonean-trained nurses emigrate to seek better lives in Canada, the US, and the UK.
- **The Relational Subsidy**: There are 520 Sierra Leonean health workers employed in the UK's National Health Service (NHS), which is equivalent to 16 percent of Sierra Leone's entire public health workforce. Furthermore, 27 percent of all doctors in the UK were trained in developing countries, including 5,819 African nationals.
- **The Failure of Aid**: Western aid programs and medical volunteers act merely as "a sticking plaster on a gaping wound". The outflow of highly skilled African healthcare workers to high-paying British hospitals completely dwarfs the counter-movement of UK aid and volunteers. The high-ranking health outcomes and high HDIs of Western nations are relationally dependent on this ongoing extraction of human capital from the poorest nations, leaving Sierra Leone trapped by unequal trade and a lack of funds to build domestic medical systems.

## Exploration via the Human Contexts and Ethics (HCE) Toolkit

### Power

- **Epistemic and Structural Hegemony**: Power manifests as the asymmetric capacity of core nations, multinational corporations, and global governance institutions (the WTO and UN) to structure the international trade, tariff, and banking rules to their own advantage. This structural power locks peripheral nations like Mozambique into a permanent, subordinate role as resource providers.
- **Market-Driven Extraction of Labor**: The globalized health employment market operates as an asymmetric power structure. By offering higher wages, wealthy nations like the UK exercise market power to drain vital human resources (16% of Sierra Leone's public health workforce) from impoverished nations, leaving the donor countries physically and financially unable to sustain basic healthcare.

### Sociotechnical Systems

- **The Distortion of Quantitative Metrics**: The UN's Human Development Index (HDI) serves as a sociotechnical system that translates complex human lives into clean, numerical rankings. However, this technical system is easily co-opted by political-economic configurations; for instance, Ireland's tax haven status artificially inflates its GNI, creating a distorted "silver medal" rank that masks real-world inequalities and misrepresents actual development.
- **The Global Medical Pipeline**: Healthcare systems cannot be understood in isolation. The high standards of the UK's NHS and the fragile, understaffed wards of Sierra Leone's Connaught Hospital are part of a single, tightly coupled sociotechnical system of global labor migration. This pipeline integrates educational training, immigration policies, digital connectivity, and wage disparities to redistribute medical expertise from the poor to the wealthy.

### Identity/Positionality

- **Categorical Subjugation**: Colonial modernization narratives systematically hierarchize human identities, characterizing Mozambican cotton farmers as "backward" or "savage" to justify economic subordination.
- **The Blindness of Expert Positionality**: The positionality of Western doctors volunteering in Sierra Leone—who are experts in medicine but untrained in the sociology of development—leads them to rely on localized, internal explanations (such as corruption, climate, or weak governments) to explain poverty. Their positionality blinds them to the broader global history of colonialism and unequal trade that actively produces the very medical "hellholes" they seek to fix.
- **The Rational Agent in the Borderlands**: Local Sierra Leonean nurses occupy a difficult positionality. Faced with meagre government salaries that cannot support a family, their decision to emigrate to London is entirely logical and self-protective, even though this collective flight of talent structurally undermines their home country's healthcare system.

### Narratives

- **The Teleological "Ladder" Narrative**: The dominant narrative of international development, pioneered by Rostow and echoed in policy slogans like "leaving no one behind," depicts progress as a linear, step-by-step climb. This narrative frames poor nations as simply living in the past, awaiting their turn to reach the Western ideal of high mass consumption.
- **The Anachronistic "Going Back in Time" Metaphor**: Medical volunteers and surgical reports construct a narrative that entering a Sierra Leonean hospital is "like going back in time" to the US Civil War. This narrative is highly dangerous because it suggests that poverty is an internal, historical phase rather than a contemporary, actively maintained product of global relationships.
- **The Counter-Narrative of Relational Inequality**: Brooks constructs a powerful relational counter-narrative. By showing that the wealth of some nations is structurally connected to the impoverishment of others, this narrative reframes poverty not as a state of being "behind," but as a permanent, actively produced condition generated by centuries of colonialism and unequal world trade.
