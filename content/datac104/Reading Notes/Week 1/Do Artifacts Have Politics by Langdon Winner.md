# Do Artifacts Have Politics? (Winner, 1980)

## Core Arguments and Interpretive Perspectives
- **The Political Qualities of Material Culture**: Winner argues that physical artifacts, machines, and sociotechnical systems cannot be judged merely for their efficiency, productivity, or environmental side effects. Instead, they must be evaluated for the ways in which they actively embody, enforce, and maintain specific forms of power, authority, and systematic inequality.
- **The Limits of "Neutrality" and "Uses" Frameworks**: The conventional understanding that "people have politics, not things" treats technology as neutral tools whose political impact depends entirely on how they are used. Winner asserts that this view is dangerously narrow; it blinds us to how physical designs establish enduring social orders and power relationships long before—and entirely separate from—their immediate or professed uses.
- **Complementing the Social Determination of Technology**: Social determination theory holds that the social or economic system in which technology is embedded explains everything, rendering the study of technical things themselves irrelevant. While Winner accepts that social and economic forces heavily shape technology, he proposes his "theory of technological politics" as a vital complement. This perspective focuses directly on "the things themselves" to analyze their material momentum and the ways in which human purposes are adapted to technical imperatives.

## Key Definitions and Conceptual Foundations
- **Politics**: Winner defines politics as the arrangements of power and authority in human associations, alongside the specific activities and decisions that occur within those arrangements.
- **Technology**: In the context of this essay, technology refers to modern practical artifice—specifically, individual pieces or large-scale systems of physical hardware.
- **Two Modes of Political Artifacts**:
  - **Category 1 (Technical Arrangements as Forms of Order)**: Highly flexible technologies where a specific physical design or arrangement is chosen to settle a social or political issue in a community.
  - **Category 2 (Inherently Political Technologies)**: Inflexible, intractable technologies that functionally require, or are strongly compatible with, specific social and political structures as their operating environment.

## Category 1: Technical Arrangements as Forms of Order
- **The Decisive Nature of Initial Commitments**: Flexible technologies offer a wide latitude of choice when they are first introduced. However, once a specific design is built, it becomes physically fixed in steel, concrete, and economic investments, hardening into a permanent framework that shapes social habits for generations, much like a political founding or legislative act.

### Detailed Historical Case Studies
- **Robert Moses’s Low Overpasses on Long Island**: Robert Moses, the master builder of New York's public infrastructure, deliberately built over two hundred overpasses on Long Island parkways with clearances as low as nine feet. This design specification was chosen due to Moses's racial and class prejudices: it physically prevented twelve-foot-tall public transit buses from utilizing the parkways. As a result, low-income citizens and racial minorities who relied on public transit were systematically blocked from accessing his acclaimed public parks, such as Jones Beach, while wealthy, automobile-owning white families enjoyed free access. This design engineered a lasting social inequality that persisted long after Moses's personal political alliances dissolved.
- **Baron Haussmann's Parisian Boulevards**: Under the direction of Louis Napoleon, Baron Haussmann engineered broad Parisian thoroughfares to replace narrow medieval streets. This physical reconstruction had an explicit counter-revolutionary political purpose: the wide streets made it virtually impossible for citizens to construct the effective street barricades that had fueled the revolution of 1848, while simultaneously facilitating the rapid movement of military troops.
- **Cyrus McCormick II's Pneumatic Molding Machines**: In the mid-1880s, Cyrus McCormick II added untested pneumatic molding machines to his Chicago reaper plant at an immense cost of $500,000. Economically, the decision was highly inefficient: the machines produced inferior castings at a higher cost and had to be abandoned after three years. Politically, however, the investment was a resounding success; the machines allowed McCormick to employ unskilled workers and systematically "weed out" the skilled, unionized workers of the National Union of Iron Molders, effectively destroying the local union.
- **The UC Davis Mechanical Tomato Harvester**: Agricultural researchers at the University of California developed a giant mechanical harvester alongside a hardier, less flavorful variety of tomato that could withstand the machine's rough handling. While the technology cut harvesting costs by $5 to $7 per ton, its $50,000 purchase price restricted its use to massive, concentrated agricultural firms. Consequently, California's tomato growers plummeted from 4,000 to 600, eliminating 32,000 farmworker jobs and devastating small rural communities. This case shows how scientific research and corporate profits can structurally stack the technological deck to favor large agribusiness, even without an active, malicious conspiracy.
- **Infrastructure and the Handicapped Rights Movement**: In the 1970s, disabled rights organizations demonstrated that the physical design of standard public works—buses, sidewalks, plumbing, and buildings—prevented handicapped individuals from moving freely, excluding them from public life. Unlike Moses's bridges, this exclusion did not stem from active malice but from systemic neglect. Once identified, however, it became clear that restoring justice required a systematic physical redesign of these public artifacts.

### Design Choices within Flexible Systems
- **Levels of Technological Choice**: Winner notes that political choices occur at two levels: the initial "yes or no" decision of whether to adopt a technology at all (such as a pesticide or an supersonic transport aircraft), and the subsequent, highly significant design features implemented after that choice is made.
- **The Automation of Machine Tools (David Noble)**: David Noble's study of machine tool designs highlights the choice between record/playback systems and numerical control systems. Although both use similar electronic and mechanical components, the choice of design has massive political consequences on the shop floor, determining whether operational control remains with skilled labor or is transferred entirely to management.

## Category 2: Inherently Political Technologies
- **The Concept of Material Intractability**: Some technologies possess inflexible physical properties that cannot be altered by changing their design or the broader political-economic system. In these cases, the initial choice to adopt the technology is decisive: once built, the system's physical properties dictate specific social and political arrangements.

### Theoretical Frameworks on Authority and Technology
- **Friedrich Engels and "On Authority"**: In his 1872 essay, Engels argued against anarchists by claiming that modern industrial systems (like cotton mills, railways, and ships at sea) naturally require a rigid hierarchy and individual subordination. He claimed that "the authority of the steam" imposes a strict discipline that is far more despotic than any capitalist employer. For Engels, relationships of authority and subordination are practical, material necessities dictated by the nature of modern science and industry, completely independent of social organization.
- **Plato's Ship of State**: In the *Republic*, Plato utilized the practical necessity of a ship's captain on the high seas to justify non-democratic state rule. Because a sailing vessel requires a single, decisive hand to steer safely, a crew must show absolute obedience; Plato used this technical reality to argue that state governance similarly requires authoritarian control.
- **The Marx-Engels Tension**: While Engels saw technology as inherently requiring authoritarian discipline, Karl Marx argued in *Capital* that modern industry's technical capacity would eventually render the hierarchical division of labor obsolete. Although capitalism reproduces this hierarchy in a "monstrous shape" by turning the worker into a living appendage of the machine, Marx believed industrial technology itself contained latent liberating conditions that would facilitate proletarian revolution.

### The Two Versions of Inherent Politics
- **Technologies Requiring Specific Social Structures**: This strong version asserts that a technology cannot exist as an effective operating entity unless specific social structures are maintained. For example, the operation of nuclear power plants functionally requires a highly centralized, techno-scientific-industrial-military elite to manage its extreme dangers.
- **Technologies Compatible with Specific Social Structures**: This weaker version suggests a technology is highly compatible with, but does not strictly require, certain political relationships. Solar energy advocates argue that solar technology is physically decentralizing, making it highly compatible with democratic, egalitarian, and self-governing local communities, even though it does not strictly guarantee them.
- **Internal vs. External Relations**: Winner distinguishes between internal relations (the social organization required within the operation of the technology itself, like factory discipline) and external relations (how the technology complements or impacts the broader society, like solar energy supporting local political autonomy).

### Case Studies of Intractable Technologies
- **The Atom Bomb as the Ultimate Authoritarian Artifact**: The atom bomb's immense lethal power demands that its internal social system be governed by a centralized, rigidly hierarchical chain of command closed to democratic influence. This authoritarian structure is a practical necessity that exists regardless of the larger political system in which the bomb is embedded. Democratic states must actively attempt to contain this mindset to prevent it from spilling over into the civilian polity.
- **Alfred Chandler's *The Visible Hand***: Chandler's historical analysis of modern business enterprise demonstrates that high-speed, large-scale systems of production, transportation, and communication (like railroads) require a centralized, hierarchical administrative organization run by professional managers. The previously dominant family-owned firms were physically incapable of coordinating these systems. Chandler shows that the material scale and speed of these technologies strictly demanded corporate hierarchies to run safely and efficiently.
- **Plutonium Recycling and the Threat to Civil Liberties**: The proposed recycling of plutonium as a reactor fuel illustrates how technical requirements can systematically erode public freedom. Because plutonium is highly toxic and valuable to terrorists or criminals, securing it would require extraordinary policing measures. Russell Ayres's legal study warned that safeguarding plutonium would lead to intense pressure to override traditional checks on executive power, introducing widespread background checks, covert wiretapping, surveillance, informers, and potentially martial law. The practical necessity of preventing or recovering stolen plutonium would render governmental infringement of civil liberties compelling, dismissing constitutional objections as "idealistic".

## Cross-Cutting Themes and Synthesis
- **The Hegemony of "Practical Necessity"**: A primary characteristic of modern technological societies is that the "practical necessity" of keeping complex systems running smoothly routinely eclipses and neutralizes moral and democratic reasoning. Standard concerns regarding worker participation, liberty, or justice are immediately dismissed with the practical rebuttal: "that's no way to run a railroad".
- **The Imperialism of Corporate Governance**: The hierarchical and authoritarian governance structures deemed necessary to run modern business corporations frequently spill over into public life. Corporate executives, accustomed to top-down command, become highly impatient with democratic processes like "one man, one vote" and advocate for centralized, expert-driven public management—such as President Carter's proposed Energy Mobilization Board—as the ideal model for national government.
- **The Political Responsibilities of Technical Design**: Because technological arrangements physically fix social relations for decades, political analysis must be applied directly to technical objects themselves. Human beings frequently accept drastic changes to their lives when justified by technological innovation that they would fiercely resist if justified on political grounds. Therefore, understanding our world requires a thorough grasp of both technical systems and political theory.
