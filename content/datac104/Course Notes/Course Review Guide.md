# Week 2 \- Making Data

## Lecture 3\. Making Data

### Takeaways

* Get into the habit of asking: How was this data made?  
  * Who made it? How? Why? In what context? So what?  
* Look for choices that are made or taken for granted (“naturalized”) in  
  * Representations  
  * Classification systems  
* See the 2-sided nature of representation \- knowledge and politics/power  
  * Remember the relationship between counting and “counting” \- What can be counted “counts”  
* Reflect on how data travels  
  * Abstracted from the context it was originally made for  
  * Into places where it didn’t originate, including into ML/AI

## Lecture 4\. Making Personal Data

### Takeaways

* Get practice with key properties of data \- representation, classification, circulation, and abstraction \- and see how they matter when people are in the data  
  * See how data often classifies, sorts, and ranks people   
  * Consider how abstractions make you look away from the people made into data  
  * Understand how data circulates, in ways that serve others’ needs (besides the data subjects’)  
* Learn to see the historical and contextual stakes of personal data that go beyond traditional definitions focused on individual identifiability and privacy  
  * Recognize how data is relational and collective, not just individual  
  * Recognize representational harm   
  * Recognize how personal data is entangled with human vulnerability  
* Ask yourself: How am I looking for / listening to the data subjects?

## Lecture 5\. Making People out of Data

### Takeaways

The “self” is something made (not just found) together with technology and society; in other words, it’s contingent and variable. Quantified self-tracking is one technique by which the self in the datafied world is “made.”  What’s unique about self-making through quantitative datafication?

Ways of being a self or a person are the products of the **performativity** of classifications, sociotechnical systems, norms and practices.

The “cyborg” is a complex figure for thinking about the human being and selfhood as porous and relational – as constructed by personal, social, biological, and technical elements.

# Week 3 \- Making Robust Knowledge

## Lecture 6\. Making Robust Knowledge 

### Takeaways

* You can practice looking at science as a human and social activity  
  * Made by real human beings, not stick-figure scientists  
  * Carried and judged by the scientific community  
* You can draw conclusions about making knowledge  
  * No simple relationship between data and theories  
  * Our scientific view of the world can change, sometimes radically  
* So to understand how robust knowledge gets made,  
  * Look beyond just the data  
  * Look at the social, intersubjective work of the scientific community  
  * Look at both the content and the context of knowledge production

## Lecture 7\. Science in Society

### Takeaways

* It is crucial that **both scientists and the public** recognize how scientific knowledge aims less at absolute certainty and more at “socially robust knowledge” that recognizes **inevitable persistence of uncertainty**  
* Scientists have a responsibility to consider how they communicate their results to the public, especially—but not only—when making expert recommendations.  
* Openness to public collaboration/participation that respects local knowledge/expertise and local needs can increase trust/credibility of scientific experts \- and can improve the quality of science as well  
* Viewing science as the making of **socially robust knowledge** means paying attention to how science is:  
  * made by scientific communities   
  * sustained by its relationship to society at large  
  * makes social order, is also a form of politics

# Week 4 \- How Was the World Datafied?

## Lecture 8\. Populations and States

### Takeaways

* See how statistics, the state, and populations have made one another  
  * Through the state’s interest in governing territory and increasing power  
* States “see” their populations via simplifying abstractions  
  * Through creating an “administrative grid”  
  * Abstractions reflect assumptions, interests, and norms of people who hold power   
  * Performative**:** bring the phenomena they describe into being  
* Statistics lets society see itself in new ways  
  * View of the sovereign, view from above  
  * Abstract numbers, tables, and graphs  
  * Social regularities, constituting “society” or “social reality”  
  * Interests in “norming” populations, assessing difference

## Lecture 9\. Eugenics and Statistics

### Key Takeaways

* Key statistical methods like linear regression and correlation were developed through an inquiry into the characteristics of human heritability that was guided by an interest in controlling human heredity at the level of populations for the sake of social improvement. This practice became known as eugenics.   
* The founders of modern mathematical statistics founded and promoted eugenics as a scientific practice, political technique, and social movement.   
* In other words, modern mathematical statistics was co-produced with eugenics, a state and elite-driven practice of norming and managing populations in the late 19th/early 20th century.  
* Almost always linked to white supremacist, elitist, and ableist imaginaries of a “desirable” racial, economic, and social order.  
* Both are part of the longer history of the co-production of the state, populations, and statistics that marked the development of biopower.   
* Eugenicist ideas and practices still linger in powerful institutions today

## Lecture 10\. Taking the Global View

### Takeaways

* See how global views have gotten built up  
  * Look for the role of the administrative state (and its successors)  
* Don’t take global views for granted  
  * Know that “the global” is a very modern (post-WWI) view  
  * Understand what institutions and narratives go into making things “global”  
  * Recognize how global datasets come with the view from above  
  * Ask who can make global data, how they make it, why, what it leaves out  
* Have a concrete example to think with  
  * Gapminder.org and global fertility data  
  * And less innocent alternatives based on narratives centering human beings on the ground
