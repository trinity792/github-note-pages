# L3 Making Personal Data

## Executive Summary

The lecture presented personal data as an institutional and relational accomplishment rather than merely information about an isolated individual. Writing information down can make it more fixed, durable, portable, and detached from the context in which it was originally shared. Institutions make data represent people by supplying the classifications, norms, texts, and practices that allow records to speak and act on people's behalf.

The lecture developed this account through six linked properties of data: representation, classification, abstraction, circulation, durability, and relationality. University records and mid-century punch cards illustrated how institutions manage people through data. The imagined campus-seal observation and the apartheid case demonstrated the choices and consequences involved in classification. Direct-to-consumer genetic testing showed that data about one person can also become data about others.

These properties create vulnerability. Data can enable recognition and access to resources, but it can also expose people to misrecognition, exclusion, disrespect, and injury. The lecture therefore called for attention to what data practitioners owe the people represented in their data, including people who never directly participated in its collection.

## Materializing Personal Information

The lecture began by asking students to consider whether verbally sharing information and writing it down were meaningfully different acts. The instructor's synthesis emphasized that written information can feel more fixed and determinate than speech. Writing gives information a durable material form, widens its possible audience, and makes its later destination harder to anticipate.

Materialization also changes context. Face-to-face conversation includes relationships, facial presence, small cues, and a shared understanding of why an exchange is occurring. A written record can leave those elements behind. Someone encountering it later may not know why it was produced or what the original participants understood it to mean.

Collecting many written records in one place would change their significance again. Aggregation could connect otherwise isolated pieces of information and make them available for uses beyond the original exchange. The meaning and comparability of even apparently simple data points are not guaranteed. For example, a student's "class year" might refer to the year the student began, a label such as first-year or senior, or an expected graduation date.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The central claim was that writing information down can alter its social and institutional status without necessarily changing its literal content.

The evidence came from the instructor's synthesis of the classroom exercise: written answers appeared more permanent, more self-conscious, and open to a larger potential audience than spoken answers. The ambiguity of "class year" supplied evidence that a recorded field does not automatically have a stable or comparable meaning.

The instructor interpreted these differences as consequences of material form, durability, aggregation, and loss of originating context. Information does not become meaningful data simply because it has been written. Its significance depends on what happens to it, where it travels, and which practices interpret it.

## Universities and the Institutional Production of Personal Data

Students encounter university data collection through tax records, Social Security numbers, email, course platforms, grades, scores, and GPA. The instructor emphasized that third-party systems such as bCourses, Canvas, and Gradescope complicate the student's relationship with the university because it may be unclear where information goes and what uses a person has accepted.

University email offered another example of institutional context. Correspondence in a Berkeley.edu account may feel differently accessible than correspondence in an ordinary personal account. The instructor noted that university employees must be conscious of what they use work email for because those records may become relevant in matters such as litigation over labor relations.

Grades, test scores, and GPA are less dramatic but especially central ways the university knows, tracks, and manages students. The lecture argued that the relationship between educational institutions and students has long been mediated by data, although this process has intensified as more of the world has become datafied.

### Example or Case: University Punch Cards

A twelve-row, eighty-column IBM punch card illustrated an earlier machine-readable form of data storage. The University of California and other large bureaucracies used punch cards for tasks including class registration.

The lecture connected this administrative technology to a cartoon from the W.E.B. Du Bois Club newsletter, *The American University*. The cartoon portrayed the university as a machine that processed students as punch cards, with students entering the machine and emerging in mortarboards. Professors appeared as marionettes, while administrators and financiers occupied positions of control.

In the critical culture described by the lecture, punch cards became symbols of impersonal bureaucracy, abstraction, oversimplification, and dehumanization. The instructor quoted historian Stephen Lubar's account of cards as two-dimensional portraits of people abstracted into numbers machines could use.

Berkeley students appropriated this symbolism during the 1964 Free Speech Movement. Administrative instructions warned users not to "fold, spindle, or mutilate" punch cards because damaged cards could jam machines. Protesters transformed that instruction into the statement, "I am a UC student. Do not fold, spindle, or mutilate me."

The instructor did not conclude that representing people through data is intrinsically dehumanizing. Instead, the case established that the important issue is institutional context: what data allows an institution to do and how it transforms the institution's relationship with the people it manages.

## Representation: How Data Speaks for People

Representation is not a neutral, one-to-one reflection of the world. It is a selective abstraction that takes something from the world and makes it stand for something that is absent. Personal data can consequently speak for a person when that person is not present.

The lecture compared data representation to political representation. Data can speak for someone under conditions that person did not choose, understand, or anticipate. Once loosened from the original act of speaking, a record can enter new settings while continuing to represent its subject.

Representation often functions as an "obligatory passage point." To obtain institutional recognition, resources, or access, people must be represented by specified data. Census counts influence whose political voice counts. Financial information determines eligibility for financial aid. Welfare and housing programs may require unhoused people to provide personal histories so institutions can score whether they exhibit the required form of risk or vulnerability. Students need documented grades, prerequisites, and course completion to enroll, graduate, or apply for employment and internships.

Social Security numbers, demographic records, and other identifiers similarly attest that a person is who an institution requires them to be. This creates a practical question: through which categories and boxes does an institution count a person?

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The claim was that data represents people only through institutional arrangements. Without institutions, communities, supplemental texts, norms, and practices, stored marks or electronic states do not automatically become personal data that can stand for a particular person.

University enrollment requirements, financial-aid records, welfare eligibility systems, and census counts illustrated how data becomes capable of speaking and acting for people.

The instructor interpreted representation as politically important because institutions can make data obligatory. A record does not merely describe a person; it can determine whether that person is counted, recognized, or permitted to pass through an institutional process.

## Classification and the Ordering of People

Classification gives data order by placing observations into formats and categories. It also links the ordering of data to social order because classifications can determine who people appear to be, what institutions do with them, and what they are owed.

Categories can appear natural or simply present in the world, but the lecture emphasized that they are made. Producing a classification system requires labor, definitions, criteria, and choices about which differences matter. The world can push back against classifications, so categories are neither pure inventions nor transparent reflections. They are tools designed around the interests and purposes of their makers.

Every classification system includes some observations while leaving others out. When phenomena do not fit, they may go uncounted or be forced into categories. The lecture described denaturalizing categories as part of the work of a good data scientist: asking who made a classification, which interests it reflects, which alternatives were possible, and what disappears when its boundaries are taken for granted.

Because classification formats what data can say, the designer of a classification system exercises power over how the world can appear. When applied to people, classification sorts them into boxes and may rank them according to desirability or risk.

### Example or Case: Counting Avoidance of the Campus Seal

The instructor asked students to imagine observing a campus seal on Memorial Glade and calculating the proportion of students who intentionally avoided walking across it.

The exercise exposed several classification problems:

- An observer might count the same student more than once.
- It would be difficult to determine whether avoidance was intentional.
- Pedestrian traffic and available walking space might explain a person's path.
- Different campus seals might attract different populations and produce different results.
- The observer would need to define who counted as a student.
- A definition based on walking would have to address people who moved in other ways, including wheelchair users.

The example supported the argument that even a seemingly simple, low-stakes data-collection task depends on difficult decisions about identity, behavior, intention, units, and inclusion.

### Example or Case: Apartheid-Era Racial Classification

The lecture used Bowker and Star's account of South Africa's apartheid-era racial classification system as a high-stakes case. The system organized segregation and white supremacy through a racial hierarchy that included intermediate classifications between Black and white. Access to spaces and opportunities depended on the racial category assigned to a person.

The case involved more than categories stored in a database. A complex, supplementary, ad hoc sociotechnical apparatus was required to assign race, enforce classifications, and make the social world conform to the classification system.

The instructor cautioned against reading the case as a problem of insufficiently scientific racial measurement. As presented in the lecture, race was not a natural property that administrators could have measured more accurately. The categories and the attempt to place people within them were themselves part of the problem.

Bowker and Star's concept of "torque" described the twisting produced when a formal classification system failed to match a person's biography, memberships, or location. People could be shifted between categories as life events changed how the system classified them. The case therefore demonstrated both the artificiality and the power of classifications: they did not cleanly fit people's lives, yet they organized social opportunities and caused injury by forcing people into administrative boxes.

## Abstraction and the Removal of Context

Abstraction is intrinsic to representation. Making data from people requires leaving out most of the density, specificity, and qualitative context of their lives. This simplification is not inherently a criticism of data. It makes phenomena tractable for particular purposes and can reveal patterns that would otherwise remain difficult to see.

Abstraction also makes data mobile. People in different places can work with simplified records in approximately the same way. For example, university advisers do not need a student's complete life story to determine whether stated enrollment requirements have been satisfied. They can act on the fields relevant to that institutional purpose.

The lecture described abstraction as the inverse of context. SAT scores and GPA may say something about a student, but they leave out much of the student's educational experience. Even intimate or highly granular personal data remains an abstraction because something is always excluded.

Abstraction can also direct attention away from suffering or other difficult aspects of human life. By turning people into comparable or fungible units, it permits bureaucratic work to proceed through numbers rather than through encounters with complete persons and their problems.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The claim was that data is useful partly because it leaves most of the world out. More data cannot eliminate abstraction because any representation remains selective.

Advising requirements, GPA, SAT scores, graduation rates, and test scores illustrated how institutions use simplified measures to make decisions and evaluate outcomes.

The instructor interpreted this selectivity as both productive and risky. An institution may optimize what is easily counted, such as graduation rates or test scores, even when its deeper concern is the quality of learning. The representation need not be simply false or biased to narrow institutional attention.

## Circulation and Context Collapse

Abstraction enables circulation. Once data has been loosened from the relationships and circumstances in which it originated, it can travel more easily than people, stories, or bulky physical objects. Numbers appear to move weightlessly and frictionlessly from one context to another.

Data brokers provided a familiar example: information produced in one context can be purchased and sold for other purposes. Social media provides another. Material posted for friends may become visible to parents or prospective employers, each of whom interprets it in a different setting. The lecture associated this movement across audiences with "context collapse."

Data never actually exists without context. It can leave one context behind, but it becomes meaningful only within another. It also requires substantial background work and standardization to make data appear to mean the same thing across settings.

### Example or Case: Mental Health Data Across University Contexts

A history of mental health difficulty might lead Disabled Students' Program staff or an academic adviser to see a student as vulnerable and in need of support. The instructor proposed that if the same information were shared with the University of California Police Department, which the instructor said was not known to occur, it might prompt suspicion instead.

The point was not that the underlying record changed. Its institutional destination changed the meaning and response attached to it. The example illustrated why decontextualized data does not speak for itself: different institutions make different meanings and actions from the same representation.

---

## Durability as Circulation Across Time

Data lasts. Although databases require maintenance and records can deteriorate, personal data generally requires less continuing work to remain available than a person's ongoing presence or testimony.

Durability allows data to cross contexts through time as well as space. A record can remain still and later be discovered by a new audience. It may also remain unchanged while the person it represents changes. The data then continues to speak for someone even though that person is no longer who they were when the record was collected.

The lecture described durability as a temporal dimension of circulation. Together, circulation and durability explain how personal data can move beyond the circumstances, purposes, and time in which it originated.

## Relational Data and Collective Identification

Legal and privacy frameworks often tether personal data to an identifiable individual, but the lecture argued that data is always relational. It consists of relationships among people, institutions, technologies, and representations. Data provided by one person can also reveal information about others.

### Example or Case: Direct-to-Consumer Genetic Testing

Direct-to-consumer genetic testing illustrated how a family member's decision can implicate someone who never submitted a sample. Because relatives share kinship relations, one person's genetic data can speak about another person without data having been collected directly from that second person.

The lecture also identified a second form of relationality. Data can place strangers into a common group because they share an attribute, such as having red hair or receiving financial aid. Statistical inference and machine learning can then make the behavior of other group members consequential for an individual. If others sharing an attribute behave in a particular way, systems may attach the corresponding prediction to everyone placed in that pool.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The claim was that "data about me" cannot be fully separated from data about collectives and relationships. Individual identifiability is therefore insufficient as a complete account of personal data.

Kinship in genetic testing and attribute-based grouping in statistical systems supplied the lecture's evidence.

The instructor interpreted relationality as an axis of vulnerability. People may be represented or subjected to predictions because of information supplied by others, including people they have never met. These consequences may escape frameworks focused only on whether data was collected directly from an identifiable individual.

## Data, Exposure, and Vulnerability

What makes data personal is not only that it identifies an individual or knows something intimate. Personal data structures who a person can be and how that person appears to others. Harm can arise from not being known well enough, being known in the wrong way, or being represented contrary to one's self-understanding.

Data exposes people to recognition, respect, and access, all of which require some form of visibility. The same exposure can create possibilities for disrespect, misrecognition, denial, loss, injury, or violence. These effects may be direct, through a representation or decision, or indirect, through the reinforcement of differences and hierarchies.

The lecture drew on Ruha Benjamin's account of "scopic vulnerability" to describe racialization as an experience of being seen in a particular way. In this account, exposure itself can be a source of vulnerability.

### Allocative and Representational Harm

The lecture distinguished two forms of harm:

- Allocative harm concerns inclusion, exclusion, and the distribution of material resources or benefits. A person's representation determines whether that person passes through an institutional process or receives something.
- Representational harm concerns how a person or group is seen. It can include disrespect, inaccuracy, incompleteness, inconsistency with self-understanding, asymmetry, lack of control, or obstruction of mutual recognition.

Representational harm does not depend on a later material consequence. Being exposed and seen in a harmful way may itself constitute the injury described by the lecture.

### Ethical Responsibility

Vulnerability is not a flaw that can or should be eliminated. The lecture presented being exposed, recognized, and acted upon by others as part of the human condition. However, people are not vulnerable in identical ways.

The ethical task is therefore to examine how data creates new vulnerabilities, extends their scope, or embodies existing inequalities. This does not require abandoning representation or classification. It requires asking what data practitioners owe the people represented in their data and recognizing that those people may include more than the apparent subjects of collection.

The instructor concluded by asking practitioners to treat personal data as delegated voices and bodies. Working with such data means taking hold of the vulnerability attached to human representation and assuming responsibility for its care.

## Human Contexts and Ethics Toolkit

### Power

The lecture repeatedly connected data to the capacity of institutions to structure other people's behavior and opportunities. Universities require grades, prerequisites, financial records, Social Security numbers, and other data as obligatory passage points for enrollment, employment, aid, and graduation. The people who define the required fields and categories determine which representations can secure access.

The campus punch-card case made this asymmetry visible by portraying students as inputs processed by an administrative machine. Free Speech Movement protesters contested this arrangement by applying the machine's handling instructions to themselves. Their slogan challenged a university relationship in which students were expected to preserve machine-readable representations in order to navigate the institution.

Classification also exercised power by determining what could be said about people and how they would be treated. The apartheid case was the clearest instance: an enforced racial hierarchy regulated access to spaces and opportunities, while supplementary practices compelled people to fit official categories. The classification system had power even though its categories did not cleanly correspond to people's biographies.

Circulation expands this power because records can reach new institutional settings. Mental health information that prompts support in one university context could prompt suspicion in another. Attribute-based inference similarly allows the recorded behavior of a group to change predictions about an individual who did not control those other people's actions.

### Sociotechnical Systems

The lecture treated personal data as an outcome of intertwined human and technical arrangements. Punch-card holes became student records only through machines, university offices, registration rules, handling instructions, and institutional practices. The technical object could not be separated from the bureaucracy that made it represent students and govern access.

The apartheid classification system also required more than a list of racial categories. Administrators, official rules, supplementary judgments, records, and enforcement practices worked together to impose the classification and make social life conform to it. Torque arose from the interaction between this formal apparatus and people's changing biographies.

The campus-seal exercise showed the same entanglement at a smaller scale. Producing a count required an observer, a location, a definition of "student," a judgment about intentionality, a way of avoiding duplicate observations, and decisions about modes of movement. No isolated technical measurement could resolve those choices.

Data circulation likewise depended on institutional context and standardization. The lecture rejected the idea that data carries a self-sufficient meaning. Advisers, police, platforms, data brokers, and other actors give records meaning through their distinct purposes and practices.

### Identity/Positionality

Classification determines how institutions see people, sometimes regardless of how people understand themselves. The apartheid case tied assigned racial identity to a hierarchy governing spaces and opportunities. The concept of torque captured the injury produced when official categories conflicted with a person's biography, memberships, or location.

The lecture also noted that institutions collect demographic information about gender and ethnicity, while its imagined seal study raised questions about who counts as a student and whether a walking-centered category includes wheelchair users. These examples showed that identity and disability can be built into apparently ordinary decisions about units and categories.

Financial aid, income, mental health history, and housing status positioned people differently within institutional systems. A person might need to demonstrate financial need or a prescribed degree of vulnerability to obtain assistance. The same mental health history could position someone as deserving support in one institutional context and as suspicious in another.

Benjamin's account of racialized scopic vulnerability further connected identity to exposure. Being seen as a particular kind of person can itself create vulnerability, even before a resource is granted or denied. The distinction between allocative and representational harm preserved both the material and identity-related consequences of datafication.

### Narratives

The punch card carried a narrative about the mid-century university as an impersonal machine that reduced students to mechanically processable units. The W.E.B. Du Bois Club cartoon and the protest slogan made that narrative explicit. The instructor complicated it by rejecting the broader conclusion that every data representation is intrinsically dehumanizing and redirecting attention toward what specific institutions do with representations.

Another narrative challenged by the lecture was that data speaks for itself. Data can appear to provide an unmediated account of the world, but the lecture argued that institutions, classifications, standards, and contexts make it meaningful. The social-media and mental-health examples showed how the same information can tell different stories when addressed to different audiences.

The lecture also questioned the narrative that classifications simply discover natural categories. The seal exercise exposed the choices involved in defining intentions and populations, while the apartheid case showed a classification system attempting to make the world fit its racial hierarchy. Denaturalizing categories means recovering the labor, interests, and alternative possibilities hidden by their apparent inevitability.

Finally, the lecture resisted a narrative in which invulnerability is the goal of ethical data practice. It instead described vulnerability as inseparable from being visible and recognized. The resulting ethical narrative centers care and responsibility for the people whose voices and bodies are made to travel through data.
