# L1 - Technology, Politics, and Context

## Executive Summary

This was the opening lecture for Data C104, Human Context and Ethics of Data. The primary lecturer identified himself at the end as Ari Edmondson, although he gave a different name at the opening, and Professor Katherine Carson briefly introduced her background and approach to the course. Students also supplied short answers during an opening discussion about what makes a good bridge.

The lecture began with political opposition to hyperscale data centers and the many competing explanations for it. Rather than settle those questions, Edmondson used them to show that data and AI depend on material infrastructures involving energy, water, labor, and contested social contexts. He then turned to Langdon Winner's essay "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" and Robert Moses' Long Island overpasses to explain how technologies can embody inequality, engineer relationships, endure as normalized parts of the landscape, and exercise power as obligatory passage points.

Four initial tools from the human context and ethics toolkit were introduced: power, socio-technical systems, identity and positionality, and narratives. Course policies emphasized in-person lecture participation, mandatory and screen-free discussion sections, weekly reading-based warm-ups, and several forms of instructional support. Carson and Edmondson framed the course as preparation for acting responsibly in complex situations rather than as a source of fixed ethical rules, with the guiding claim that "there's no ethics without context."

## Data Centers and the Politics of Data Infrastructure

Edmondson opened with hyperscale data centers, including one of the Stargate facilities being built at the time. He characterized the construction effort as one of the largest physical infrastructure build-outs in United States history and one of the most contested. He cited a recent Gallup poll in which about 70% of Americans strongly or somewhat opposed a data center being built in their area, describing this as a nearly 50% shift since the previous fall. He emphasized that this opposition crossed traditional partisan divisions and had become part of recent midterm electoral contests.

He presented several possible interpretations without endorsing one. Opposition might reflect low-information voting, social-media-fueled hysteria, local resistance to nearby development, or concerns about economic growth. It might instead reflect communities' experience with unfulfilled promises of prosperity, concerns about climate change, energy consumption, noise, health risks, or pollution, hostility toward AI as a threat to work and ways of life, or suspicion of big technology companies, inequality, and concentrated political and economic power.

Data centers illustrated how apparently technical objects could become politically and culturally charged. They also made visible the material infrastructure beneath AI and "the cloud," which depended on data alongside energy, water, and labor. The course would return to data centers later, but the lecture moved first to the simpler example of a bridge to establish a method for analyzing technologies in context.

## What Makes a Good Bridge?

Students proposed several criteria for a good bridge: safety, the ability to bear weight without collapsing, effects on surrounding wildlife, effects on nearby property, future maintenance, and successful movement from one point to another. Edmondson noted that functionality and safety often came to mind first because technology was commonly judged by whether it efficiently fulfilled its stated task.

The broader answers already pointed toward social and environmental context. That transition introduced Langdon Winner, an important scholar in Science, Technology, and Society, and his essay "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" Winner was described as having trained at UC Berkeley in political science during the 1960s, experienced the Free Speech Movement, and written the essay around 1980 as a foundational text in the development of Science, Technology, and Society.

## Robert Moses' Overpasses and Political Artifacts

Winner's example concerned a system of roads, parks, and overpasses constructed in the New York metropolitan area under Robert Moses. Moses worked there for about 40 years, from the 1920s to the 1970s, and, despite never holding elected office, decisively shaped the region's built environment. The Long Island parkway connected New York City with Jones Beach, which the lecture described as the crown jewel of Moses' public-park projects.

The overpasses were lower than usual and may have prevented buses from using the roads. The documentary record did not clearly establish Moses' intention, and the extent to which buses could fit remained disputed. The practical concern was that automobile owners could use the routes for recreation or commuting while bus riders could not. Given the socioeconomic composition of New York described in the lecture, this meant that predominantly wealthy white automobile owners could reach places such as Jones Beach more easily, while disproportionately Black, Hispanic, and low-income bus riders could not.

### Embodied and Systematic Inequality

Edmondson closely read Winner's claim that Moses' structures "embody a systematic social inequality." An artifact could materialize, represent, carry, reinforce, and enforce a social relationship whether or not that outcome was its designer's clearly documented intention. The inequality was systematic because its effects followed patterns connected to structures such as class, race, gender, disability, or intersections among them.

The lecture hall supplied a second example. Seats faced the stage, the professor stood at the front, and the room materially encouraged a relationship in which the professor spoke while students listened. That arrangement was not necessarily bad, but it embodied an unequal educational relationship and made alternatives such as broad discussion physically difficult even if ideas about teaching later changed.

### Engineering Relationships

Winner's phrase "engineering relationships among people" distinguished technological politics from politics conducted through speech, persuasion, elections, laws, or cultural norms. Engineering suggested manipulating and optimizing relationships from a position of mastery. It also directed attention to who shaped the arrangement: in Moses' case, an unelected official rather than elected officials, volunteers, or members of the public.

On this reading, Moses did not merely construct objects that happened to produce political effects. He treated society and New York City as technical objects that could be optimized. Material construction could therefore act as politics by other means, settling a conflict through the environment without passing through conventional political processes.

### Durability and Normalization

Winner's statement that these structures became "another part of the landscape" highlighted the durability and inertia of material artifacts. Technologies required work to undo, and people also became accustomed to them. Once incorporated into daily routines and infrastructure, they faded into the background and appeared natural rather than artificial or open to rearrangement.

Stairs illustrated this point. Many people used them without reflection, while a disabled person might encounter them as a disruption or barrier. The everyday invisibility of an artifact contributed to its power because it shaped conduct, habits, and expectations of what was normal, legitimate, and acceptable without repeatedly attracting attention.

### Technologies as World-Making

The lecture drew the broader conclusion that technologies were more than neutral tools for efficiently achieving predetermined ends. They could exert authority, substitute for other forms of action, and build social order through arrangements of concrete, steel, wires, transistors, nuts, and bolts. Politics therefore occurred not only through politicians and voting but also through everyday relationships with technological infrastructure.

Winner distinguished between technologies that settled particular issues within a context and the more radical possibility that some technologies might be inherently political because they required, or were especially compatible with, certain political arrangements. He cited debates over whether nuclear power was inherently authoritarian and solar power inherently democratic but did not resolve them. The methodological task was to look beyond immediate effects toward a technology's wider political context and the limits of the political arrangements it could support.

### Obligatory Passage Points

Technologies could exercise power by becoming obligatory passage points: infrastructures people had to use to obtain something they wanted. Moses' roads offered a literal example because reaching Jones Beach required passing through a road system that accommodated cars but not buses. Smartphones supplied a contemporary analogy because many ordinary activities had become difficult without passing through them.

This concept joined the lecture's other claims about world-making. Technologies integrated with daily life, embodied values and narratives, arranged social relationships, and constrained agency by defining the available route toward desired ends.

## The Human Context and Ethics Toolkit

The human context and ethics toolkit was introduced as a collection of analytical lenses for reasoning about the contexts of data. Its purpose was to denaturalize the social and technical world by asking how familiar arrangements came into being and how they might have been built differently. The tools were not rules, checklists, or algorithms that guaranteed ethical conduct; they oriented inquiry and taught students what questions to ask.

### Power

Power was defined as the asymmetric capacity of a person or technology to structure or alter the behavior of others, with scientific and technological power intertwined with political power. It was presented as a basic feature of human relationships rather than simply a bad condition to eliminate. Power appeared in cooperation and empowerment, conflict management, and domination through force, coercion, or violence.

The analytical questions included who had power, over whom, how it was exercised, who prevailed in conflict, who could arrange circumstances in advance to prevent conflict, which forms of knowledge enabled action, and how science and technology altered power relations. Artifacts could exert distinctive forms of power by becoming obligatory passage points, disappearing into the landscape, extending human capacities, or substituting for labor.

### Socio-Technical Systems

A socio-technical system was defined as an arrangement in which people and technologies were so intertwined that the technical component could not be isolated and handled independently. The concept did not claim that the social was more important or that it determined the technical. Instead, it blurred the boundary and directed attention to the full network of agents, practices, laws, norms, institutions, machines, and artifacts required for a technology to work.

A phone required a network of other phones, supporting organizations, and electricity. Even a hammer became intelligible only in relation to nails, boards, houses, boats, and practices of construction. Similarly, a single overpass acquired political significance through its location in a larger road network designed around cars. If a parallel road without low overpasses had accommodated buses, the original bridge would not have performed the same exclusionary function.

### Identity and Positionality

Identity and positionality referred to life-shaping and socially conditioned aspects of selfhood, including gender, race, class, disability status, immigration, and income. Identity concerned both how people experienced themselves and how society perceived, positioned, and treated them. The framework therefore asked who created data and socio-technical systems, who was represented or shaped by them, who benefited or was harmed, and how power constructed hierarchies around perceived human differences.

The lecture emphasized relationships rather than treating identities as isolated attributes. Class, for example, was not merely a personal label but a position within relationships between labor and capital. Applied to Moses' roads, the tool raised questions about how access shaped the people and spaces on either side of the overpasses, including the identities of those who could inhabit the new parks and those who were prevented from doing so easily.

### Narratives

Narratives were described as stories unfolding through time that expressed what mattered to people, explained who they were and how the world worked, and presented certain futures as possible, desirable, or inevitable. They linked past, present, and future into coherent movement and contained emotional dimensions that oriented and motivated action.

Narratives shaped technological design, purposes, goals, data collection, interpretation, and ideas about which social problems data should solve. Analysis therefore required attention to words, metaphors, origins, beneficiaries, and visions of a good life. Moses' projects expressed narratives about urban improvement, public recreation, and who should shape a city. Car-centered development also reinforced a narrative of modernization organized around individual automobiles and nuclear families, in contrast with communal life structured around public transportation.

## Course Expectations and Participation

Lecture attendance was described as crucial to success and strongly correlated with student performance, although attendance would not be tracked. Lectures were interactive, recordings would not be available, and slides would be posted on the e-courses schedule page. Interactive use of old DB was expected to begin Friday, with instructions already on e-courses and a follow-up announcement forthcoming.

Students were asked to save questions until the end of lecture and not begin leaving during the final minutes, because movement made it difficult for others to hear and concentrate. Students were also told not to sit in aisles because doing so posed a fire hazard.

### Discussion Sections and Absences

Discussion attendance was mandatory. Two absences were permitted without penalty, while a third required a reengagement in the student's GSI office hours within a week. Further absences could not be made up. Students were told to communicate with their GSI about absences.

Attendance at the first discussion section next week was especially important. A student unable to attend because of an emergency needed to contact the GSI because the staff might otherwise interpret the absence as lack of interest and automatically drop the student to make room for someone on the waitlist. Students seeking a section change were instructed to follow the posted process and not simply drop and re-add, which would move them to the back of the waitlist.

### Screen-Free Discussion and Required Readings

Discussion sections would be screen-free to support presence and focused attention. The policy was connected to a broader crisis of attention that the lecture said data-driven technologies had partly produced. Edmondson included himself among those affected and described eliminating classroom distractions as a small effort to cultivate attention. Possible exceptions were to be discussed with the student's GSI.

Careful, patient reading was presented as another form of attention. Students needed to bring the readings to discussion each week. A course reader was available from Copy Central on Telegraph, and students were encouraged to obtain it before the next discussion because participation required access to the key texts.

### Weekly Warm-Ups

Every discussion section would begin with a 10-minute, in-class writing exercise based on readings and lectures from the prior week. These warm-ups were graded but low stakes, intended to demonstrate sincere preparation rather than mastery.

The first warm-up next week would receive full credit if the student made a meaningful effort. Readers would still provide feedback showing how the response would have performed under the normal grading standard.

Katherine Carson said her pronouns were she and they. She was initially trained in theoretical physics and later became a historian of theoretical physics. Her faculty appointment was in the History Department, where she was also department chair. Her work included the history of quantum field theory, physics, politics, and culture in 20th-century Germany and the United States, and subjects shaped by the Second World War, including Nazi weaponry and the thermonuclear arms race.

## Ethics, Context, and Personal Reflection

The final part of the lecture clarified that the course would not prescribe how students should live, define a good life for them, or dictate their politics. Its purpose was to help students understand the world well enough to act within it. The course's guiding slogan was "there's no ethics without context": questions about obligations, mutual responsibilities, and proper action could not be answered adequately without understanding where a data technology came from, whom it served, and what it did.

The course was also intended to be personal rather than merely academic. Students were invited to reflect seriously on their single, irreversible, finite lives: who they were, which stories they told about themselves, how they had reached Berkeley, where they were going, what they cared and worried about, who their role models were, and how their time at Berkeley served the larger lives they envisioned. The aim was to prevent careers and lives from proceeding on autopilot until regret appeared later.

History supported the work of denaturalization by showing that the world had not always been arranged as it was, had arisen for particular reasons, and could be otherwise. The tools introduced in the lecture were therefore meant to help students recognize that their lives did not have only one possible path and that different, potentially better futures remained possible.
