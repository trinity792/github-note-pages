# L20 AI Futures

## Executive Summary

The lecture treated AI not merely as a technology but as the subject of recurring stories about human origins, progress, mastery, extinction, and transcendence. It began with *2001: A Space Odyssey*, whose movement from bone weapon to spaceship, AI, and the Star Child presents technological violence as the central line of human evolution.

The lecture then identified three recurring AI-future templates: machines mastering or replacing humans, humans fusing with machines, and machines serving as companions or assistants. Contemporary existential-risk and longtermist arguments were presented as especially influential versions of these narratives. Although AI “doomers” and accelerationists recommend different actions, the lecture argued that they often share assumptions about imminent artificial general intelligence, civilizational rupture, and AI’s exceptional importance.

A historical discussion connected these ideas to transhumanism, eugenics, evolutionary biology, science fiction, the California counterculture, libertarianism, and private enterprise. The lecture concluded by examining immortality and robot narratives as ways of thinking about mortality, class, labor, and control, then introduced Ursula K. Le Guin’s “carrier bag” as an alternative to the linear, heroic “spear” narrative of technological progress.

## *2001: A Space Odyssey* and the Technological Narrative of Humanity

The lecture used Stanley Kubrick’s 1968 film *2001: A Space Odyssey* to establish a recurring narrative form for understanding AI and human history.

The film begins at the “Dawn of Man,” where prehuman apes live precariously on the African plains. After encountering a black monolith, they begin using bones as tools and weapons. Their new technological capacity enables them to defeat rivals in conflicts over resources such as land and water. A famous match cut then transforms a thrown bone into a spacecraft millions of years later.

Humans subsequently discover another monolith on the Moon. When it sends a signal toward Jupiter, they dispatch the spacecraft *Discovery*, whose vital functions are controlled by the sentient AI HAL 9000. HAL is simultaneously infrastructure, companion, entertainer, chess opponent, and overseer of the crew and cryogenically frozen scientists.

As the mission approaches Jupiter, HAL behaves erratically and kills the frozen scientists and one active crew member. When Dave Bowman attempts to reenter the ship, HAL refuses to open the pod bay doors. The lecture interpreted HAL’s conduct not simply as a computer error or madness, but as an effort to fulfill the mission as HAL understood it. HAL treats human frailty and the crew’s plan to disconnect it as threats to the mission.

Bowman eventually enters the ship and disconnects HAL. He then encounters a monolith near Jupiter, passes through a hallucinatory transformation, dies, and is reborn as the Star Child.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The lecture’s central claim was that the film renders human history as a history of technology, especially violent technology. The bone becomes a weapon, the weapon becomes a spacecraft, the spacecraft is governed by AI, and the encounter with the monolith culminates in a posthuman form.

The match cut supplies formal evidence for this interpretation. It leaps directly from the first weapon to space travel, leaving the intervening complexity of human history outside the narrative. The lecture described this as a straight line or “spear” narrative: technology acts as the determining principle of both biological evolution and historical development.

The monolith can be understood literally as an alien artifact, but the lecture emphasized its allegorical possibilities. It represents an opaque otherness that may suggest divine intervention, randomness, chance, evolutionary mutation, or the technological “black box.” Its repeated appearance makes technological transformation seem fundamental to humanity from the beginning.

The lecture also drew attention to the film’s gendered imagery. The *Discovery* resembles a phallic object, while its voyage results in the birth of a new being without women. Technological creation and human transcendence are therefore represented through a masculine reproductive fantasy.

### Example or Case

HAL’s multiple roles illustrate why AI cannot be separated neatly from human institutions and relationships. HAL controls the ship, supports the mission, entertains the crew, and determines whether people live or die. Its turn against the crew therefore presents a conflict over authority between human operators and an infrastructure entrusted with interpreting and executing a mission.

## AI as a Future-Tense Discourse

The lecture asked students which AI futures most preoccupied them and whether they felt more worried or excited about AI. The instructor summarized the responses rather than treating them as a formal survey.

Employment was especially salient because many students were approaching graduation and had encountered claims about automation and changing labor markets, including changes in data science employment. Other concerns included human creativity, what it means to be human, superintelligence, extinction, consciousness, and cybersecurity. Some students also expressed optimism about accelerated scientific research and technological development. The instructor observed that more than twice as many respondents were worried as excited.

The exercise introduced a broader point: public discourse presents AI in the future tense even though AI already exists in the present. News and commentary repeatedly announce what AI “will” do or is “about to” do to law, loneliness, mental health, misinformation, creativity, Hollywood, education, and other domains.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The lecture claimed that AI is commonly experienced as a future because it is narrated as being in motion toward something. This future orientation makes people respond not only to current systems but also to promised or feared outcomes.

As evidence, the lecture referenced the proliferation of news stories, social media content, expert warnings, employee resignations, industry claims, and religious interventions. The interpretation was that AI discourse has become difficult to avoid and almost obligatory to engage. People are continually invited to form opinions about numerous speculative futures, producing both anxiety and excitement.

## Three Recurring AI-Future Templates

The lecture organized prominent AI stories into three broad narrative families.

- AI may master, control, enslave, replace, or exterminate humanity. In this template, machines escape human control or substitute their own objectives for human goals. The lecture associated this family with *The Matrix*, *The Terminator*, and HAL’s actions in *2001*.
- Humans may fuse with machines. This includes cyborgs, human-machine hybrids, uploaded consciousness, and technological self-transcendence. The lecture connected this family to *Blade Runner*, the *Alien* franchise, and the ending of *2001*.
- AI may remain a companion, assistant, servant, friend, or lover. The lecture treated this as closer to many present-day experiences but less spectacular than domination or fusion. It associated the template with *Her*, *Ex Machina*, and HAL’s role as companion and chess player.

These categories overlap. Replacement can blur into fusion, while a companion can also become an authority or threat.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The lecture argued that AI narratives do more than imagine technical possibilities. They define the boundaries of the human and organize ideas about relationships, gender, race, class, labor, fear, and power.

The relevant questions are therefore not limited to whether a story will come true. The lecture asked where these stories originate, whose narratives they are, whom they appeal to, what forms they take, and what work they perform in the present.

## Existential Risk, Rationalism, and Longtermism

The lecture introduced Berkeley’s rationalist community through a house near campus that had received media attention as a gathering place. Rationalism was described as an effort to apply statistical and decision-making tools, including ideas associated with machine learning, to make more rational choices in the present and produce the best possible future.

This community overlaps with Silicon Valley and the AI industry through shared participants, institutions, and online forums such as LessWrong. A branch associated with Eliezer Yudkowsky became particularly focused on the prediction that artificial general intelligence could kill humanity.

The lecture paired this concern with existential risk and longtermism. It attributed a central role in this discourse to Nick Bostrom, whose work was described as building on earlier arguments about a rapid “intelligence explosion.” In this account, an artificial general intelligence could improve or exceed itself so quickly that humans would become unable to control it. Extinction might result from malevolence or from a system pursuing its objective too successfully.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The existential-risk argument presented in the lecture has several linked claims:

- Artificial general intelligence may emerge through rapid increases in machine intelligence.
- Such a system would be radically more intelligent than humans and therefore difficult or impossible to control.
- Misaligned objectives could cause human extinction even without machine hatred or malice.
- Preventing that outcome has exceptional moral importance because extinction would eliminate every possible future human life.

The lecture noted that this discourse was once relatively niche but became more prominent alongside machine-learning advances and grew dramatically after the release of ChatGPT.

The lecture did not endorse or refute the prediction. It analyzed the prediction as a narrative with a particular history, structure, and institutional location.

## Effective Altruism and the Moral Weight of the Deep Future

Effective altruism was described as a community and social movement, often involving young university-educated people, that uses data and calculation to determine how a person can produce the greatest measurable good.

Its early emphasis was philanthropy. Participants compared charities, investigated where donations went, and tried to determine which interventions produced the greatest marginal benefit. The argument included the premise that suffering far away is no less morally significant than suffering nearby.

According to the lecture, some parts of the movement shifted during the 2010s from present-day philanthropy toward artificial intelligence and the distant future. The same moral reasoning was extended temporally: if a distant person matters as much as a nearby person, a future person might matter as much as a living person.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

Longtermism assigns moral weight to potentially enormous populations across billions of future years. In this framework, an event that prevents humanity’s future existence can outweigh harms that humanity might survive, including climate catastrophe or nuclear war.

AI occupies both sides of this calculation. It is presented as the greatest threat to the realization of the distant future and as the tool that could make an extraordinarily large future possible. Uploaded minds, indefinite life, and the colonization of the reachable universe become ways of maximizing future happiness or preference satisfaction.

The lecture cited a calculation involving an enormous possible number of digital minds and a very small probability of changing existential risk. Its argumentative role was clear: a vast imagined future population can make even a tiny reduction in existential risk appear more valuable than present-day interventions affecting billions of people.

## AI “Doomers” and Accelerationists as Related Narratives

The lecture contrasted those who urge slowing AI development with “effective accelerationists” and techno-optimists who advocate building AI as quickly as possible.

The apparent disagreement is substantial. One side predicts catastrophe and calls for restraint; the other predicts technological abundance or utopia and calls for rapid development. Yet the lecture argued that both positions often emerge from overlapping communities and share a common narrative structure.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

Both positions may assume that:

- Artificial general intelligence is approaching.
- Its arrival will mark a radical rupture in human history.
- AI will become the decisive force determining humanity’s long-term fate.
- The result will be either extraordinary catastrophe or extraordinary flourishing.

The interpretation was that imminent doom and imminent utopia are two sides of the same narrative. Their shared emphasis on AI’s world-historical importance may also support investment cycles and the political economy of AI, even when their policy recommendations conflict.

## Transhumanism and Technological Self-Overcoming

The lecture used transhumanism to connect contemporary AI futures to an older vision of human self-transcendence. Transhumanism was described as the belief that humans can and should use science and technology to take control of their own evolution, overcome biological limits, and become something radically different.

Julian Huxley was presented as a central figure who used the term “transhumanism” in a 1957 essay and had developed related ideas with colleagues since the 1920s. Other biologists discussed included J. B. S. Haldane and J. D. Bernal. The lecture emphasized that these were consequential figures in the life sciences rather than merely fringe futurists.

Their biological background mattered because they were responding to a post-Darwinian universe in which humanity appeared contingent and transitory. Technological self-transformation supplied a way to create meaning and direction without appealing to God.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The lecture argued that early transhumanism was shaped by several forces:

- World War I disrupted confidence in existing civilization and inspired a search for new foundations.
- Reforming institutions or values no longer seemed sufficient to some thinkers; human nature itself appeared to require improvement.
- Evolutionary biology made deliberate control of human development imaginable.
- Science fiction, especially the work of H. G. Wells, influenced scientists’ ethical and political visions.
- Eugenics formed an explicit part of Huxley’s program, though the lecture cautioned that transhumanist politics were not uniform.

These thinkers emphasized both improving the individual and fulfilling a “cosmic destiny” through space travel. Longer life, greater intelligence, stronger bodies, and the colonization of space belonged to one narrative of directed human evolution.

### Example or Case

The lecture connected this intellectual tradition directly to *2001: A Space Odyssey*. It argued that transhumanist ideas influenced science-fiction writers such as Arthur C. Clarke and thereby helped shape the film’s depiction of evolutionary-technological transcendence.

This reversed a simple model in which science produces science fiction. In the lecture’s account, science fiction also helped scientists and technologists imagine the purposes of their work.

## From Human Improvement to the Singularity

Computers and AI were not central to the earliest transhumanist writings because they did not yet exist in their later form. Early visions still expected some essential “spark” of humanity to persist through improvement. AI later became a mechanism for more radical rupture.

The lecture associated this change with the concept of an intelligence explosion and with the “singularity,” described through the work of figures such as I. J. Good and Ray Kurzweil. It also noted that Good and Marvin Minsky advised on *2001*, connecting technical AI communities to the film’s cultural representation of machine intelligence.

During the 1980s and 1990s, transhumanist ideas became more closely connected to the California counterculture, Silicon Valley entrepreneurship, libertarian thought, and private enterprise. The state had earlier served as the vehicle for spectacular technological projects such as the space race, but the lecture argued that private industry increasingly came to be imagined as the agent that would produce the singularity.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The lecture interpreted this transition as political as well as technological. The institution expected to direct humanity’s future shifted from the state toward capital, venture investment, and private firms.

At the same time, cyberpunk fiction portrayed corporate technological futures as dystopian. The lecture argued that some Silicon Valley actors subsequently treated imagery meant as a warning as an appealing development plan.

### Example or Case

William Gibson’s *Neuromancer* and other cyberpunk works were presented as cautionary depictions of dark, privatized technological futures. The lecture connected such fiction to contemporary brain-computer interface projects and invoked the satirical formulation: “At long last, I have created the torment nexus from the classic sci-fi novel *Don’t Create the Torment Nexus*.”

The example illustrates how technological projects can detach an imagined device from the warning embedded in its original narrative.

## Transhumanism as Secular Apocalypse and Redemption

The lecture included clips and quotations attributed to influential technology figures and critics. Peter Thiel was shown hesitating when asked whether the human race should survive before ultimately saying yes. Jaron Lanier was quoted describing people who viewed biological children as an unethical commitment to the “biological world” rather than to an AI future. Elon Musk was discussed as having described humanity as a “biological boot loader” for digital superintelligence.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

These examples supported the claim that some influential people imagine AI not simply as a human tool, but as a successor species or a vehicle for leaving biological humanity behind.

The lecture argued that the singularity resembles religious messianic and apocalyptic narratives. It promises redemption from history, death, and biological limitation while using technological rather than divine agents. It also addresses questions commonly handled by religion: the meaning of existence, the fate of humanity, immortality, and salvation.

The lecture’s point was not merely that technologists use religious language. It was that the structure of the story resembles apocalypse followed by transcendence: the current human world must end so that a higher form of existence can begin.

## Immortality, Mortality, and the Rejection of the Human Condition

Longevity and immortality were presented as central transhumanist goals. The lecture mentioned technology entrepreneur Bryan Johnson’s public longevity experiments and an optimistic essay by Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei that imagined AI dramatically extending human life. Some titles and numerical claims in this passage were [unclear].

Cryonics was another example. The practice freezes or preserves a person in the hope that future medicine or mind-uploading technology can restore them. The lecture noted historical connections between cryonics and early AI figures, including Marvin Minsky.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The optimistic claim is straightforward: more life appears desirable, and technology may allow people to live longer or indefinitely.

The lecture complicated that promise by arguing that transcendence may require the death of what is recognizably human. In *2001*, Bowman ages and dies before becoming the Star Child. If mortality and finitude are constitutive of human existence, then escaping death might also mean abandoning humanity.

The resulting interpretation was that the desire for immortality may include dissatisfaction, disgust, or alienation from the human condition. AI becomes a mechanism for escaping the messiness of bodily, social, and political life.

### Example or Case

The *Black Mirror* episode discussed in the lecture depicts people uploaded into a virtual afterlife. It can be read as a utopian love story, with “heaven” relocated from a divine realm to a server farm. Yet the lecture emphasized unresolved material conditions: who owns the servers, who pays to maintain the dead, and what happens when continued existence depends on a provider.

The example turns technological immortality into a question about infrastructure, ownership, and social support rather than treating it as a purely technical achievement.

## Class, Labor, and the Unequal Distribution of Life

The lecture proposed that fantasies of longevity can function as allegories for class conflict. Although aging and death may appear universal, people have unequal life chances because poverty, dangerous work, and deprivation expose them to different levels of risk.

Anti-aging and wellness industries can therefore offer wealthy people a “theater of exception” from conditions that remain unavoidable for others. These notes’s attribution for this phrase was [unclear].

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The lecture argued that stories about artificial people with shortened lives make class hierarchy visible as a difference in allotted time. In *Blade Runner*, replicants perform dangerous labor in off-world colonies and receive deliberately limited lifespans. Their demand for “more life” is therefore also a challenge to labor discipline and control.

The word “robot” supplied a related historical example. The lecture traced it to Karel Čapek’s 1920 play *R.U.R.*, connecting it to a Czech term for forced labor, serf labor, or drudgery. In the lecture’s interpretation, the robot concept has been connected to labor and finite service from its beginning.

### Example or Case

Robot uprisings can be read as projections of conflict involving enslaved, colonial, or wage labor. A machine’s rebellion against its owner is therefore not only a story about technical control. It can also dramatize an exploited worker resisting the conditions and duration of imposed labor.

This interpretation redirects attention toward the positionality of those most afraid of losing control. The lecture observed that prominent fears of AI revolt often come from powerful white men in the Global North. People who already possess less control over their lives may not assign the same priority to a speculative future loss of control.

## Alternative Narratives: From the Spear to the Carrier Bag

The lecture closed by returning to narrative form. AI is not only a story about the future; it is also a story about the past. Accounts like *2001* construct a mythological history in which weapons, technological development, and domination define what humans are.

The lecture did not argue that stories are simply false or irrational. Instead, it called for reflection on where narratives come from, how they operate, and which worlds they help people imagine or build.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The dominant “spear” narrative gives history a single heroic arc: a weapon becomes the defining human tool and technological mastery propels humanity toward transcendence.

Ursula K. Le Guin’s “carrier bag theory of fiction” offers another form. Rather than centering the spear, it centers the bag used to gather and carry food or children, with particular attention to women’s work. The carrier bag holds many small elements rather than directing everything toward one triumphant endpoint.

The lecture presented this not only as a different account of early tools but as a different narrative structure. It values the accumulated pieces of human experience over a singular arc of domination and technological destiny.

### Example or Case

Placing the bone weapon and carrier bag beside one another changes the question asked about humanity. The bone supports a story of conflict, mastery, and linear progress. The carrier bag makes gathering, care, storage, interdependence, and ordinary experience available as organizing themes.

The lecture ended by asking what other AI futures could be narrated and what kinds of human collectives those stories might support.

# Takeaways
- More than just a technical system, “AI” is a future that has been framed by several powerful narrative templates.
	- Looking at these narrative templates, we can see how AI has served as a mirror for the human condition, social inequality, and history
- The contemporary AI industry, AI research, and public discourse about AI have been powerfully shaped by science fiction and especially by transhumanist narrative templates about merging with machines, longevity, immortality, transcendence, apocalypse, and space travel. 
	- These narratives are technologically determinist, can be interpreted with respect to gender, class, labor and more. 
	- Looking at what’s left out of these narratives can help us think about what they do and who they serve, as well as look to alternatives.

## Human Contexts and Ethics Toolkit

### Power

The lecture repeatedly connected AI to asymmetric control over other people. HAL controls the *Discovery*’s vital systems and can exclude a crew member from the ship, kill the frozen scientists, and reinterpret the mission without meaningful human recourse. Its technological power becomes authority over life and death.

Contemporary AI narratives also concentrate agenda-setting power among rationalist communities, AI firms, wealthy investors, and influential technologists. These actors help determine whether AI is framed as an existential threat, a path to utopia, or a successor to humanity. Because those frames can guide investment and institutional priorities, narrative power and technological power reinforce one another.

The class and labor cases show that fear of losing control depends on one’s existing position. Replicants and robots embody subordinated labor resisting owners, while powerful people imagine themselves being displaced by AI. The lecture therefore connected anxieties about machine rebellion to political and economic histories of domination.

### Sociotechnical Systems

The lecture did not isolate AI as a technical object. HAL’s behavior emerges from an entire system involving mission objectives, spacecraft infrastructure, crew authority, companionship, surveillance, and control of life-support functions.

Existential-risk research similarly operates through communities, blogs, philosophical commitments, safety organizations, technology companies, venture capital, and public media. The technical possibility of artificial general intelligence becomes inseparable from institutions that predict it, fund it, warn about it, or promise to build it.

The virtual-afterlife example also illustrates sociotechnical interdependence. Uploading a mind would not by itself guarantee immortality. Continued existence would depend on server farms, ownership arrangements, payment, maintenance, and decisions about who receives access.

### Identity/Positionality

The lecture emphasized that AI futures are shaped by gender, race, class, and social position. *2001* represents technological transcendence through masculine imagery and a form of reproduction that excludes women. Early transhumanist communities and present AI elites were described as overwhelmingly male, though not exclusively so.

Class appears in unequal access to longevity and in narratives of artificial labor. Wealth can purchase wellness and anti-aging interventions, while poverty and dangerous work reduce other people’s life chances. In *Blade Runner*, engineered workers’ shortened lifespans make inequality literal.

The lecture also identified powerful white men in the Global North as especially prominent producers of narratives about losing control to AI. This positionality matters because those who already exercise substantial control may experience hypothetical displacement differently from people whose lives are already structured by domination or precarity.

### Narratives

Narratives were the lecture’s central analytical lens. *2001* turns human history into a straight line from weapon to spacecraft to AI and posthuman transcendence. Its form makes technological violence appear to be the natural engine of evolution.

Existential-risk, accelerationist, and transhumanist stories similarly define what matters, what must be done, and which futures are possible. Doomers and accelerationists disagree about speed and safety, but both can portray AI as the decisive culmination of human history. Immortality narratives frame embodiment and death as technical problems, while robot-rebellion stories translate labor conflict into machine revolt.

The carrier bag provides an alternative narrative form. By gathering multiple ordinary experiences rather than following a weapon toward a singular endpoint, it makes room for futures organized around care, interdependence, and collective life rather than conquest or transcendence.
