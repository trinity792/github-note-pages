# L22 Making Silicon Valley I

## Executive Summary

The lecture began a multi-class study of Silicon Valley by situating it geographically in the Santa Clara Valley and culturally as a prominent template for imagining technological futures. It presented the region as more than a location: Silicon Valley is also a narrative about who drives technological change, a device for making predictions about the future, and a mechanism that creates wealth and distributes it among winners and losers.

The lecture introduced a standard history centered on materials, technologies, and entrepreneurs, arranging it as successive layers. The first layer connected wireless communication and aviation to local technical communities, suppliers, naval power, and eventually wartime industrialization. The second examined Stanford's transformation through electrical engineering, relationships with local companies, patents, industrial parks, military research, and postwar federal funding. The third followed solid-state electronics from the transistor to Fairchild Semiconductor, integrated circuits, and Intel.

Throughout these layers, the instructor looked beneath individual inventions and celebrated entrepreneurs to identify recurring networks, institutions, markets, political forces, and military demand. The lecture also emphasized that every historical narrative selects a starting point and leaves people out. Beginning with the agricultural "Valley of Heart's Delight," for example, can obscure Indigenous history, colonization, genocide, and the laborers who sustained agriculture.

## Silicon Valley as Place, Narrative, and Future-Making Device

The lecture geographically located Silicon Valley in the Santa Clara Valley, including such places as Palo Alto, Los Altos, Mountain View, Sunnyvale, Santa Clara, Cupertino, and San Jose. The instructor's map also extended the region toward Fremont for personal reasons connected to a family member's first technology job in digital signal processing.

The region's cultural significance exceeds its geographic boundaries. The lecture described Silicon Valley as familiar to students through family ties, career aspirations, local experience, and its status as a cultural touchstone. This familiarity helps make it a powerful model for explaining how technological innovation supposedly happens.

The "silicon" in Silicon Valley does not refer to the area's underlying geology. It refers to the technological and cultural meanings attached to the region. The lecture identified three closely related functions:

- Silicon Valley serves as a device for producing predictions about innovation, technological development, and relationships between humans and technology.
- It carries an embedded causal narrative about how technological change works, who its important actors are, and how development unfolds over time.
- It operates as a machine for creating wealth, allocating that wealth among winners and losers, and repeatedly producing and stabilizing social order.

The instructor described Silicon Valley's future-making as **performative**. Predictions based on the Silicon Valley model do not merely describe a possible future. They help shape conditions so that the future develops in the predicted form. The stories and imagined futures surrounding the region can consequently make a particular historical arrangement appear natural or inevitable.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

**Claim:** Silicon Valley should be understood simultaneously as a place, an imaginative model, a narrative, and a mechanism of wealth and social ordering.

**Evidence:** The lecture connected its geographic concentration of technology companies to repeated stories about innovation, aspirational employment, and the region's influence on expectations about how humans and technologies will relate.

**Interpretation:** These dimensions reinforce one another. The wealth-producing machine gains legitimacy from stories that make its operations appear to be how technological progress naturally works, while those stories draw authority from the region's visible economic and technical power.

## Cycles, Waves, and the Search for Underlying Dynamics

A graph of employment from 1975 to 2013 illustrated Silicon Valley's wave-like development. The lecture associated successive waves with integrated circuits, personal computers, the internet, and social media.

The instructor argued that recurring rises and falls suggest an underlying process rather than a random sequence of inventions. The central historical question is therefore not only which technologies appeared, but why the region repeatedly produces waves of startups that sometimes grow into massive companies.

The lecture did not claim to complete that explanation in this session. Instead, it established historical foundations and asked students to watch for recurring forces, drivers, networks, and relationships across the chronological layers.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

**Claim:** Silicon Valley's repeated technological and employment cycles point toward recurring historical dynamics.

**Evidence:** The lecture used the graph's successive rises and falls and the sequence of dominant technologies as indications of patterned development.

**Interpretation:** Understanding Silicon Valley requires looking beneath the surface chronology of inventions and companies. The standard account of materials, technologies, and entrepreneurs is useful, but it does not by itself explain why similar waves recur.

## The Standard Narrative and Its Omissions

The instructor characterized the day's account as Silicon Valley's "standard narrative." It is a strong starting story and resembles the internal account the region often tells about itself. It selectively emphasizes innovative materials, cutting-edge technologies, savvy entrepreneurs, and chronological progress.

Like all histories, however, this narrative selects a portion of reality. The lecture proposed examining what lies beneath its most visible layers and asking what the story naturalizes, whom it excludes, and what purposes it serves.

The choice of a historical starting point was presented as especially consequential. A familiar prehistory of Silicon Valley begins with the Santa Clara Valley as the agricultural "Valley of Heart's Delight," known for cherries, apricots, prunes, and other stone fruit. Treating agriculture as time zero, however, passes over earlier Indigenous presence, European colonization, and genocide. The instructor specifically noted that Ohlone people were present before colonizers.

The agricultural starting point can also produce nostalgia for an era of white settlement and preindustrial agriculture. Photographs of individual orchard owners or homesteaders under fruit trees foreground property owners while leaving agricultural laborers who picked and processed the fruit outside the frame. The transition to a technological age can then appear as a clean story of takeoff rather than one layer built on earlier histories and social relations.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

**Claim:** Historical narratives are selective constructions whose starting points determine what becomes visible and what disappears.

**Evidence:** Beginning with the agricultural valley highlights white orchard owners and a landscape of fruit production while passing over Indigenous history and obscuring the workers who harvested and processed the crops.

**Interpretation:** The omission is not incidental to the narrative. It allows the later rise of technology to be represented as development from an idyllic agricultural baseline, naturalizing the social conditions already in place.

## Layer One: Wireless, Aviation, and the Foundations of Electronics

The first technological layer began with wireless telegraphy, later called radio. The lecture identified San Jose as the location of the nation's first broadcast radio station in 1909 and noted that Berkeley established a campus station in 1914. These early installations supported a community of amateur radio operators, small businesses, manufacturers, and equipment suppliers.

### Vacuum Tubes and Signal Amplification

The instructor explained the material operation of the vacuum tube. Inside its glass enclosure, a heated filament or cathode releases electrons. The electrons move toward an anode or plate, while a grid controls how easily they pass through the tube. The vacuum prevents collisions with molecules that would disrupt their movement.

This arrangement allows a weak input signal to produce a stronger, measurable output. Vacuum tubes could therefore generate, detect, and amplify electromagnetic signals. Wireless was not merely an electronics technology but a communications technology and, in the lecture's terms, an early twentieth-century information technology.

### Wireless as a Sociotechnical Development

The lecture moved from the device to the social organization around it. Wireless became consequential through the combination of technologies, broadcasting installations, amateur operators, manufacturers, local suppliers, and clients with specific communications needs.

San Francisco's role as a naval center was crucial. Ship-to-shore communication required wireless transmission because a telegraph wire could not follow a moving ship. The lecture used the first ship-to-shore wireless telegraph message received in the United States, transmitted in August 1899 from the San Francisco lightship to announce the fleet's return from the Philippines after the Spanish-American War, to connect wireless technology with the state and the projection of naval power across the Pacific.

### Aviation and Moffett Field

The lecture next connected wireless to aviation. Moffett Field began as a naval air station in the 1930s and included a massive hangar originally built for airships, then considered a leading aviation technology. As airships fell out of favor, the base adapted to other military aircraft.

Around this installation grew a network of amateur aviators and small aviation suppliers. Wireless electronics became integrated with aviation because aircraft, like ships, required communication without fixed wires. The Santa Clara Valley thus developed as one of California's centers for aviation alongside the Los Angeles basin.

### World War II and Industrialization

World War II greatly accelerated the Bay Area's industrial development. The lecture cited the Kaiser shipyards in Richmond and the conversion of an agricultural machinery manufacturer in San Jose to tank production. This industrialization introduced larger, capital-intensive enterprises and helped form a new business elite.

The war also increased demand for electronic manufacturers and made air communication especially important to West Coast defense. Electronics shops, aviation networks, military installations, industrial production, and state demand became mutually reinforcing parts of the region's foundation.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

**Claim:** The early electronics layer cannot be explained as a succession of isolated inventions.

**Evidence:** Wireless depended on vacuum tubes, radio stations, amateur communities, local suppliers, naval communication, aviation facilities, and military demand. World War II then expanded both manufacturing and electronics.

**Interpretation:** The lecture treated the technical device and its social setting as inseparable. The usefulness, financing, manufacture, and spread of wireless emerged through networks connecting technical communities, businesses, the military, and the state.

## Layer Two: Stanford, Industry, and Federal Investment

The lecture presented Stanford as the second layer of the standard Silicon Valley story while cautioning that Stanford's visibility may partly reflect its skill at storytelling rather than sole responsibility for regional development. Berkeley engineers also supplied important technical work throughout this period, even though the standard narrative often assigns more attention to Stanford.

### Stanford's Origins and Early Position

Leland Stanford, a former California governor and railroad magnate associated with completing the first transcontinental railroad, founded the university with his wife as a memorial to their son, Leland Stanford Jr. Its founding president, David Starr Jordan, was described as a natural scientist and theologian as well as a prominent advocate of eugenics, racial segregation, and racial purity.

During the early twentieth century, Stanford remained a financially precarious and relatively provincial institution. It trained predominantly white men and women for roles in the American West and in the United States' sphere of influence abroad. Herbert Hoover, who graduated in 1895 as a mining engineer and later became secretary of commerce and president, helped establish what became the Hoover Institution after World War I. The lecture characterized Stanford during the 1920s and 1930s as a small regional school with a strong conservative orientation.

### Fred Terman and Electrical Engineering

The local radio environment created an opportunity for Stanford to build electrical engineering and physics programs. The university recruited alumnus Fred Terman after his doctoral training. The lecture also linked him to his father, Lewis Terman, whose work in intelligence testing aimed to identify the "best" or genetically gifted individuals.

Fred Terman recruited graduate students and built funding for electrical engineering and physics. His students William Hewlett and David Packard founded a company in 1937 to manufacture precision electronic instruments. Their use of a garage with Terman's support became one of Silicon Valley's iconic origin stories.

Other students, including the Varian brothers, developed microwave transmitting and receiving technology important to radar. The lecture described radar as pervasive across World War II's Atlantic and Pacific theaters and stated that, within histories of science and technology, the atomic bomb is often said to have ended the war while radar won it.

### Patents, Companies, and Institutional Boundaries

Stanford revised its patent policy to claim university ownership over patents emerging from student work in its laboratories, seeking a source of income. It also allowed companies to use university laboratories. This brought revenue but produced disputes over control and secrecy.

After World War II, companies located near Stanford in an early industrial park. Faculty served on company boards, held stock, and consulted for firms. The result was a network with porous boundaries among the university, businesses, students, and faculty.

Fred Terman returned from wartime service as dean of engineering and positioned Stanford to benefit from dramatically expanded federal investment in science and engineering. The lecture presented this postwar national funding, especially defense funding, as central to Stanford's rise from a marginal regional school into the top institutional ranks.

Stanford also promoted the Stanford Research Institute for military work that might not fit the university's traditional role. The university, industrial park, and research institute became connected through people, contracts, students, faculty, and funding.

### Real Estate, Finance, and Regional Leadership

The lecture connected Stanford not only to technology but also to Santa Clara Valley real estate developers, financiers, manufacturers, bankers, and landowners. By the 1950s and 1960s, orchards were being replaced by business and industrial development.

The region acquired a new business-centered leadership while retaining continuities in race, class, and labor relations. Leadership remained overwhelmingly white and hostile to organized labor. Stanford's architecture, modeled on Spanish missions, was interpreted as sustaining a narrative of civilizing the territory and creating continuity across these historical changes.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

**Claim:** Stanford's rise and its place in Silicon Valley resulted from networks connecting academic programs, companies, patents, real estate, federal funding, and military research.

**Evidence:** The lecture traced relationships among Fred Terman's engineering program, Hewlett-Packard, microwave research, revised patent policies, company use of laboratories, the industrial park, the Stanford Research Institute, faculty consulting, and postwar defense investment.

**Interpretation:** Stanford did not simply generate inventions within an isolated university. It developed an institutional model whose boundaries with business and government were deliberately porous, allowing it to capture resources from the postwar political economy.

## Layer Three: Solid-State Electronics and the Semiconductor Industry

The third layer introduced the technology that placed "silicon" in Silicon Valley: solid-state electronics. The transistor was developed at Bell Labs in 1947 as a replacement for the vacuum tube. Transistors amplify and detect electrical signals using semiconductor materials such as silicon or germanium, which the lecture described as neither conductors nor insulators.

William Shockley later left Bell Labs to establish a firm in 1955. Encouraged by Fred Terman and connected to Palo Alto through his childhood and parents, he located the company in Mountain View near Stanford. The lecture also identified Shockley as deeply connected to eugenics.

### Fairchild Semiconductor and Entrepreneurial Networks

Shockley's company failed after technical staff rebelled against his management, but the technology and personnel remained in the region. Eight employees left to form Fairchild Semiconductor, aided by an investment banker who encouraged and financed the venture. The lecture described Fairchild's rapid growth from a small initial workforce to approximately 1,400 employees three years later.

The Fairchild case became a model for employees leaving an existing company to establish another around a promising technology. Its founders also generated later networks, including Eugene Kleiner's connection to the venture capital firm Kleiner Perkins.

The lecture's iconic photograph of Fairchild's founders emphasized a mid-century modern image of white men in suits and ties positioned at the technological frontier. This visual representation was part of the standard entrepreneurial narrative.

### Integrated Circuits and Military Markets

The integrated circuit placed multiple electronic components on a flat semiconductor wafer. Early chips were technically difficult to manufacture, failure-prone, expensive, and dependent on trial and error. Producers might discard many defective devices to obtain a single working unit.

Military customers supplied the market and funding needed to develop this uncertain technology. Integrated circuits replaced fragile vacuum tubes in demanding environments such as jet aircraft and missiles. They were valuable because they could be made small, fast, powerful, and resistant to vibration, pressure, and acceleration.

The lecture used production and price figures from 1962 to 1968 to show output increasing by roughly a factor of 100 while average unit costs fell by roughly a factor of 25. Military purchases initially covered development costs, with other uses emerging as prices declined.

### The Military-Industrial Complex

Aircraft, missiles, defense contractors, and electronics remained central to the semiconductor industry's development. The lecture named firms including Lockheed Martin and described the relationship through President Dwight Eisenhower's warning about the "military-industrial complex."

In the excerpt presented, Eisenhower warned that the conjunction of an immense military establishment with a large arms industry could acquire unwarranted influence, affect cities, state governments, and federal offices, and reshape the structure of society. The instructor stressed that this warning came from a Republican president with extensive military experience rather than from an external critic of the armed forces.

By the late 1960s, the semiconductor industry was established in the Santa Clara Valley. Intel, founded in 1968 by people who had moved on from Fairchild, became a prominent example of the recurring process in which personnel left an existing company to build another around a new product. By the time the term "Silicon Valley" was coined in 1971, the lecture argued, the region's basic dynamics were already operating before computers became the central feature of its public identity.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

**Claim:** Military demand and investment were essential to the emergence of the semiconductor industry in Silicon Valley.

**Evidence:** Early integrated circuits were unreliable, costly, and difficult to manufacture, but military clients required their small size and durability in aircraft and missiles and were willing to pay development costs. Production rose and prices fell before consumer uses expanded.

**Interpretation:** The standard entrepreneurial story of Shockley, Fairchild, and Intel rests on a wider structure joining technical labor, investment finance, military procurement, defense contractors, and state power. The semiconductor industry's initial market was not personal computing but Cold War military technology.

## The Layered Model of Silicon Valley

The instructor organized the lecture as a sequence of layers analogous to those of an integrated circuit:

- Wireless and aviation supplied technical communities, communications needs, manufacturers, suppliers, and connections to naval and air power.
- World War II deepened industrial capacity, state demand, and the region's business leadership.
- Stanford connected research, teaching, patents, companies, real estate, military work, and federal funding.
- Solid-state electronics built on those foundations through semiconductors, investment networks, military markets, and employee-founded successor firms.

The layered metaphor does not suggest that one era simply disappeared when another began. Each layer established people, organizations, infrastructure, markets, and relationships that shaped what could develop next.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

**Claim:** Silicon Valley existed as an operative regional system before computing became its defining public identity.

**Evidence:** By 1971, the region already contained electronics manufacturers, military installations and customers, Stanford's university-industry networks, federal research funding, semiconductor firms, investment relationships, and a recurring pattern of workers leaving firms to found new companies.

**Interpretation:** Computers entered an already structured environment. They did not independently create the region's basic dynamics.

## Human Contexts and Ethics Toolkit

### Power

The lecture repeatedly connected technological power to political and military power. Wireless grew partly through naval requirements and the projection of United States power across the Pacific. Aviation, radar, integrated circuits, aircraft, and missiles developed through relationships among technical communities, defense contractors, universities, and government funding.

Economic and institutional power also shaped the region. Stanford changed patent policies, controlled inventions arising in its laboratories, and built relationships with companies and federal funders. Investment bankers determined which technical teams could establish new firms, while military customers made expensive and uncertain semiconductor production commercially possible.

Silicon Valley's power extends beyond producing technologies. The lecture characterized it as a mechanism that creates wealth, allocates gains among winners and losers, and reproduces social order. Eisenhower's warning about the military-industrial complex supplied the lecture's clearest account of the risk that fused state and industrial power could acquire unwarranted influence throughout government and society.

### Sociotechnical Systems

Every major technological case in the lecture combined artifacts with people, institutions, and political arrangements. A vacuum tube's technical ability to amplify a weak signal mattered because it operated within radio stations, amateur communities, supplier networks, naval communication, and aviation. The lecture explicitly moved from the material device to the community and infrastructure surrounding it.

The semiconductor case followed the same pattern. Integrated circuits were not presented as autonomous inventions that naturally diffused into society. Their development depended on skilled workers, difficult production processes, investment capital, military specifications, government purchasing, defense contractors, and later commercial markets.

Stanford likewise functioned as part of a sociotechnical system. Faculty, students, laboratories, patents, firms, industrial parks, military research institutes, federal contracts, and real estate development formed a network with deliberately porous institutional boundaries. The lecture's central method was therefore to look beneath individual technologies and entrepreneurs for these intertwined systems.

### Identity/Positionality

The lecture tied the region's history to race, class, labor, and social position. The familiar agricultural origin story centers white orchard owners while excluding Ohlone people, the history of colonization and genocide, and the workers who picked and processed fruit. Who tells the story, and who can imagine themselves in its nostalgic landscape, affects what counts as Silicon Valley's beginning.

Stanford's history also connected institutional development to identity and hierarchy. Its founding president promoted eugenics, segregation, and racial purity. Fred Terman's family history linked the engineering program to Lewis Terman's effort to identify supposedly superior or genetically gifted individuals. The university initially trained predominantly white men and women for roles in the American West and abroad.

Later industrialization changed the basis of regional leadership without fully changing who held power. The lecture described the new business elite as still overwhelmingly white, economically powerful, and hostile to organized labor. The Fairchild founders' iconic image similarly presented white men in suits as the visible subjects of technological modernity. These examples show how the standard narrative foregrounds owners, engineers, and entrepreneurs while laborers and other groups remain less visible.

### Narratives

Narratives were a central subject rather than merely a method of presenting history. Silicon Valley supplies a story about how innovation works, which actors matter, what causes technological change, and which futures seem possible or inevitable. Because this story helps organize investment, aspiration, and institutional action, the lecture described its future-making as performative.

The standard narrative centers materials, technologies, and heroic entrepreneurs. Origin stories such as Hewlett and Packard working in a garage, Shockley returning to the region, Fairchild's founders leaving an unsuccessful firm, and Intel emerging from Fairchild provide a chronological account of innovation through exceptional individuals and startups.

The lecture did not discard this narrative, but treated it as incomplete and selective. The agricultural starting point can conceal Indigenous history and labor. Stanford's celebrated role can overshadow Berkeley's technical contribution and minimize the importance of federal defense funding. Entrepreneurial accounts of semiconductors can conceal the military customers and state investment that made risky production viable.

These omissions help a particular arrangement of technology, capital, government, and social hierarchy appear natural. Questioning the narrative therefore means asking where it begins, who appears as an actor, who is absent, which causal forces it emphasizes, and what social order the story makes seem inevitable.
