# L18 Automated Decision Making III - Risk

## Executive Summary

This lecture concludes the sequence on automated decision-making by moving beyond the question of whether individual decisions are accurate or discriminatory. It asks how automated systems organize social life, embody political assumptions, and participate in making social order.

The lecture traces risk assessment from maritime insurance through industrial accident insurance, workers' compensation, the welfare state, private life insurance, actuarial fairness, anti-discrimination disputes, neoliberalism, and contemporary machine learning. Insurance contains two competing tendencies: solidaristic pooling treats risk as collective, while individualized pricing divides people into increasingly narrow risk groups and expects each person to bear their own calculated risk.

The lecture argues that contemporary notions of fairness did not simply predate risk assessment and then get applied to algorithms. Instead, actuarial practices, modern liberalism, legal disputes, and concepts of fairness shaped one another. Automated systems extend this history by sorting people into possible futures, disciplining them to manage their data and scores, and continuously personalizing environments to influence behavior. The lecture ends by asking what is at stake when prediction becomes a dominant model for governance, rational agency, and humanity's relationship to the future.

## From Discrete Harms to the Politics of Automated Systems

The lecture begins by locating itself within the week's broader examination of automated decision-making. Earlier material introduced algorithmic fairness and bias, including the standard narrative that unfairness can be treated as a problem of inaccurate or discriminatory decisions. The course then complicated that narrative by questioning its assumptions and by examining ethical concerns that do not fit neatly under fairness or accuracy.

A technical or evaluative approach can identify a system, list its benefits and harms, assess its risks, and ask how to improve it. The lecture treats these methods as useful but incomplete. Ethical analysis may involve ambiguous, long-term, or world-making effects that cannot be reduced to a list of immediate costs and benefits.

The larger question is therefore not only whether automated systems harm or help particular people. It is how they organize social life, create forms of association, and make social worlds. This shift requires contextual and historical analysis:

- What is the political logic of automated decision-making?
- When did institutions adopt these systems, and for what reasons?
- What political issues does technology appear to settle?
- What forms of human association do automated systems favor or help create?
- What patterns become visible across many otherwise distinct applications?

The lecture invokes the earlier question of whether artifacts have politics. Automated decision-making systems are politically significant not only because they may discriminate but because they participate in producing social order. An answer limited to the statement that algorithms can be biased would therefore omit much of the week's argument.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The central claim is that fairness cannot be adequately understood by inspecting isolated outputs alone. The lecture's evidence consists of the broader institutional history of risk assessment, including insurance, welfare administration, credit, criminal justice, and behavioral technologies.

The lecture interprets these systems as templates for organizing society. Their classifications and predictions do more than produce individual decisions: they distribute responsibility, define deservingness, structure access to resources, and make particular relationships between individuals and collectives appear reasonable.

## The Historical Rise of Fairness and Risk

The lecture identifies a marked increase in discussion of fairness beginning around the 1960s and continuing through the late twentieth century. It argues that a solely nineteenth-century account of liberal rule-following cannot explain this later rise.

One possible explanation comes from intellectual history. The lecture points to John Rawls's *A Theory of Justice* from 1971 as a highly influential account of justice framed as a species of fairness. As presented in the lecture, Rawls attempted to reconcile liberal commitments to freedom with egalitarian commitments to some degree of equality. His argument offered a way to justify a welfare state that redistributes goods while remaining grounded in individual rational choice.

The lecture does not treat the influence of a single book as a sufficient explanation. Ideas become influential within social conditions that make them salient. The civil rights movement forms one important part of that context. Its broad claims about justice were partly channeled through the legal system into anti-discrimination law, where discrimination could be identified as unfair treatment.

The lecture's main addition to these explanations is the role of statistical risk assessment, especially legal disputes involving insurance. It argues that a particular conception of fairness was articulated through disputes over how insurers classified and priced risk.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The claim is that modern algorithmic fairness, quantitative risk assessment, and modern liberalism developed in relation to one another. Fairness was not merely an independent moral vocabulary later applied to algorithms.

As evidence, the lecture juxtaposes the rise in discussion of fairness with the expanding use of risk assessment in public agencies, including a chart showing the rapid growth of actuarial instruments for safety and parole prediction beginning in the late 1970s.

The interpretation is one of co-production: institutions that measured risk helped shape intuitions about fairness, while moral, legal, and political ideas about fairness influenced how risk was measured and governed.

## Maritime Insurance and Risk as a Social Technology

The historical account begins with maritime insurance under early mercantile capitalism. Long-distance shipping was dangerous and expensive, and a shipwreck could devastate an individual investor. Shipping partners responded by pooling resources into a shared fund that could reimburse losses.

Historical data on shipwreck frequency made it possible to estimate future losses. Insurers could calculate a probability of shipwreck, multiply it by the value of the cargo, and use that estimate to determine a premium. This did not predict whether a particular ship would sink. Instead, it made the uncertain future governable by estimating what would happen on average across a population of voyages.

Risk assessment therefore offered a form of control in the present. It enabled investment and planning despite uncertainty, giving participants protection against a single catastrophic event.

The lecture emphasizes that insurance was also a social technology. Individuals voluntarily entered a mutual compact, pooled resources, and secured themselves against a risk they shared. The common risk bound them into a collective even while preserving a liberal ideal of individual choice.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The claim is that risk is simultaneously individual and collective. A person's individual risk can only be estimated by reference to statistical patterns observed across a population.

The maritime case supplies the evidence. Individual investors gained security only because multiple participants pooled their resources and because records of many voyages made average loss calculable.

The lecture interprets this arrangement as a characteristic liberal social form. Voluntary individuals retain a degree of freedom while acquiring collective security through financial interdependence. Insurance thus creates a social order distinct from both traditional personal obligation and rights directly guaranteed by the state.

## Industrial Accidents and the Collectivization of Risk

Risk assessment expanded during industrial capitalism as workers increasingly labored in factories, mines, and railroads around large and dangerous machinery. Workplace injuries could leave workers unable to earn money or support their families.

In the United States, injured workers sought damages from employers through tort law. Courts initially ruled that employers were not liable. Some rulings appealed to a maritime-insurance conception of risk, arguing that the danger of an occupation had already been priced into workers' wages.

At the same time, statistical bureaus documented workplace deaths and injuries. The relative consistency of accident rates made accidents appear predictable at the population level, even though no one could predict which individual would be injured. The lecture connects this insight to the earlier idea of "social physics": if a rate remains stable across a population, the event may appear less like an isolated individual's fault and more like a regular feature of a social arrangement.

Workers responded by pooling resources through what was initially called workman's compensation. Each person contributed, and the fund supported those who later suffered an injury. This arrangement began as a voluntary form of mutual protection because neither courts nor employers guaranteed compensation.

The conception of responsibility subsequently changed. In late nineteenth-century Europe and, more extensively, twentieth-century America, security increasingly came to be treated as a collective or state obligation rather than merely an individual's responsibility to purchase protection.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The lecture claims that statistical regularity helped shift the interpretation of industrial accidents away from individual fault and toward collective risk.

The evidence was the observed stability of accident rates in industrial settings. These regularities made the number of future accidents broadly predictable even when the identity of each injured worker remained unknown.

The interpretation is political as well as statistical. Once accidents were understood as products of working conditions, responsibility could move from the isolated worker toward mutual funds, employers, or the state.

## Social Insurance and the Solidaristic Welfare State

The collectivization of industrial risk helped establish the basis for the welfare state. Organized labor pressed the state to assume responsibility for the population's general welfare. In the United States, the New Deal of the 1930s marked this development, although the lecture notes that American welfare provisions remained comparatively limited.

Social Security exemplifies the solidaristic model. In spirit, even where practice did not fully realize the ideal, it was intended to provide broadly shared protection as a right rather than a benefit that each recipient had to prove they individually deserved. The lecture also acknowledges that such programs could reinforce racial inequalities despite their universal aspirations.

This model treats risks such as economic depression and widespread unemployment as collective conditions beyond any one person's control. People are not individually priced according to their behavior. They contribute as citizens and receive support according to need, with poorer recipients potentially receiving more than they paid in.

The lecture distinguishes this model from arrangements focused on fault or personal responsibility. Benefits are meant to follow automatically from need or qualifying conditions, not from a determination that an individual behaved responsibly.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The claim is that social insurance embodies one pole within the politics of risk: social solidarity. People pool resources because no individual can independently secure themselves against every shared danger.

The welfare-state examples provide evidence that risk pooling can distribute support without demanding that each person's contribution precisely match their expected individual cost.

The lecture interprets this arrangement as neither straightforward socialism nor an entirely individualistic order. It combines personal freedom with collective security, although its egalitarian ambitions may be limited or contradicted in practice.

## Actuarial Fairness and Individualized Risk

Insurance also developed an opposing, more individualizing tendency. Some participants objected to paying the same amount as people who appeared more likely to generate losses. The lecture uses reckless driving as an intuitive example: charging a demonstrably reckless driver more can appear reasonable because the driver has some control over the behavior.

Private, for-profit insurance companies divided broad populations into progressively smaller risk pools. Rather than treat everyone as equally representative of one population-wide average, actuaries used characteristics correlated with outcomes to classify people into different tiers. Each individual was then treated as equivalent to the average person in their assigned group.

This practice supported the concept of actuarial fairness:

- Equal risks should pay equal premiums.
- Unequal risks should pay unequal premiums.
- Each person should pay an amount corresponding to the loss they are expected to generate.
- Requiring someone to pay more or less than their expected risk can be presented as irrational or unfair.

Insurers also argued that excessive pooling creates moral hazard by reducing the incentive to avoid reckless behavior. The lecture notes that insurers had a commercial interest in this position. More precise risk classification could allow a company to charge lower premiums to selected customers without losing money, thereby improving its ability to compete.

The demand for increasingly precise classification encouraged data collection. Life insurance companies were among the institutions driving nineteenth-century quantification outside the state. Insurers required granular information about health and behavior, and doctors were asked to complete standardized, quantified medical reports. People submitted to datafication in exchange for financial security.

### Example or Case

The lecture asks whether individualized pricing remains appealing in other contexts. Should medical insurance costs depend on medical history, genetic information, or personal behavior when those data may predict expense? Should poorer borrowers pay higher interest rates when lenders predict that repayment will be more difficult?

These examples complicate the intuition that everyone should pay only for their own risk. Individualized pricing may appear fair under actuarial logic while also making credit or healthcare more costly for already disadvantaged people and reinforcing class conditions.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The claim is that actuarial fairness relies on a convenient equality within narrowly defined groups. Individuals are treated as equivalent to a group's average, even though the group contains diverse people.

The evidence lies in insurance classification practices and their commercial incentives. Better data allowed insurers to form narrower pools, predict losses more precisely, and compete through differentiated prices.

The lecture interprets this logic as socially consequential. Decisions about which risks belong to individuals and which arise from social or environmental conditions are political decisions, not merely technical classifications.

## Gender, Anti-Discrimination, and the Politics of Classification

The lecture examines disputes over insurers' use of gender as a risk factor in the 1970s and 1980s. Insurers continued to use gender because they argued that it predicted outcomes relevant to life insurance. Under actuarial fairness, using a predictive category could seem justified because unequal predicted risks should receive unequal premiums.

Activists argued that this practice discriminated against women by making an immutable characteristic into a basis for pricing risk. According to the lecture, they maintained that premiums should depend only on attributes over which an individual has some agency. A person should not be penalized for membership in a protected group, even if that group characteristic predicts an outcome.

The lecture stresses the form of this challenge. Activists could have rejected the individualizing logic of risk pricing or demanded a more collective model. Instead, they often appealed to individual rights and individual choice. They asked insurers to classify people more individually rather than treat them through broad group characteristics.

Insurers responded by collecting more data and investing in narrower classifications intended to reproduce predictive distinctions without explicitly using gender. The lecture states that this strategy could reproduce gender effects indirectly.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The lecture's claim is that these anti-discrimination challenges did not necessarily weaken actuarial individualization. By demanding more individualized prediction, they could reinforce it.

The evidence is the insurance industry's response: more data, finer-grained surveillance, and narrower risk pools designed to replace a prohibited group category.

The interpretation attributed to a historian discussed in the lecture is that activists did not sufficiently identify risk measurement itself as a political act. Their challenges addressed the variables used in classification but not the underlying demand that every individual pay for a precisely calculated personal risk.

The broader political question is how society distinguishes outcomes produced by personal choice from those produced by social and environmental conditions. The lecture treats that distinction as contested and political rather than simply statistical.

## Neoliberalism, Individual Responsibility, and Welfare Automation

The lecture situates individualized risk assessment within the late twentieth-century political development it calls neoliberalism. As presented, this included:

- Privatizing public functions such as education, prisons, healthcare, and welfare.
- Deregulating industry.
- Weakening organized labor.
- Expanding markets and financialization.
- Treating market logic as a standard of rationality and efficiency.
- Moving away from ideals of social solidarity and egalitarianism.
- Making individuals responsible for bearing and managing risk.

Under this political orientation, the state is less responsible for guaranteeing social equality or material sufficiency. It is increasingly tasked with protecting and extending markets in which individuals must determine how to prosper. People are addressed less as citizens entitled to collective guarantees and more as consumers making choices.

Automated decision-making contributed to the dismantling of welfare provision. Beginning in the 1970s, welfare agencies adopted computerized systems partly as administrative modernization and partly as a way to reduce spending by identifying fraud or mismanagement and denying benefits. Rules involving missed appointments or paperwork could be encoded and applied at scale. Removing human decision-makers also made denials more difficult to contest.

These systems helped transform welfare into a test of individual deservingness. Data were used to determine whether a person deserved assistance rather than treating assistance as a universal provision.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The lecture does not claim that insurance risk assessment simply caused neoliberal politics. It claims that actuarial systems both reflected and helped advance broader cultural and political changes.

The evidence includes privatization, changing expectations of the state, the expansion of individualized risk management, and automated benefit administration.

The lecture describes this relationship as co-production. Individual risk assessment became a template for neoliberal governance, while actuarial fairness supplied part of its moral framework. A well-ordered society was increasingly imagined as one in which each person bore the cost of their own risk as calculated by automated systems.

## Automated Sorting, Credit, and the Production of Class

Automated systems make social order by sorting people into different possible life paths. Decisions about credit, hiring, insurance, and university admission affect which futures become available.

The lecture uses credit scoring to explain how predictive technology participates in producing class differences. Access to financial capital shapes people's opportunities, and credit scores make financing more or less available by sorting individuals into risk categories.

The process is not limited to an external decision imposed on someone. People learn that recorded data and scores influence their futures, so they become responsible for managing those representations. The lecture describes these scores as a form of "ordinal capital" [unclear]. Individuals are encouraged to monitor and improve the data-based standing that helps determine their place within society.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The claim is that automated classification does not merely detect a preexisting class position. It participates in making class differences by controlling access to resources and possible futures.

Credit scores are the central evidence. A prediction of repayment risk influences whether credit is available and at what price.

The interpretation is disciplinary. Once people internalize the importance of scores, they govern their own conduct in anticipation of future classification. Individual responsibility expands from managing behavior to managing the data through which institutions perceive and rank them.

## Choice Architecture, Nudging, and Behavioral Modulation

The lecture distinguishes disciplinary sorting from another mode of algorithmic power associated with choice architecture and hypernudges. This technique is connected to the political philosophy of libertarian paternalism, which governs through individual choices while lightly directing those choices from outside.

Unlike discipline, behavioral modulation does not require people to know they are being watched or explicitly internalize a norm. A platform predicts behavior, changes the user's environment, observes the response, and updates its prediction. Continuous feedback and A/B testing allow the system to influence behavior at population scale.

Google Maps is used as an example of a system that predicts behavior, observes real-time conduct, and revises its predictions. The individual encounters a changing environment and reacts to it, while the system continuously anticipates and adjusts to those reactions.

The lecture presents this as a distinct articulation of power. The goal is not necessarily to make every individual behave in exactly the same way. It is enough to shift the behavior of a population, on average, in a desired direction.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The claim is that automated systems govern not only by classifying people but also by dynamically modifying the environments within which they choose.

The evidence is the continuous cycle of prediction, intervention, observation, and revision in systems such as mapping platforms and A/B-tested digital environments.

The interpretation is that personalization can extend neoliberal governance. Individuals still appear to choose freely, but their choice environments are continually arranged in light of predictions about their future behavior.

## Personalization and the Fulfillment of Actuarial Individualism

Contemporary machine learning makes risk assessment more granular, continuous, and individualized than older actuarial classifications. Traditional actuarial systems grouped a person by legible attributes and equated them with the average member of a risk pool. Machine-learning systems can instead combine vast numbers of data points to produce an actionable prediction about one person's propensity to behave in a particular way.

The lecture connects this development to personalized recommendations, personalized medicine, surveillance-based pricing, and dynamic airline prices. In the airline example, tracking technologies may be used to predict how much a particular customer is willing to pay and to maximize the offered price.

Personalization appears to fulfill the ideal behind actuarial fairness: each person receives treatment supposedly matched to their own risk, behavior, or predicted willingness to act. Yet the person's individuality is operationalized through data points and predictions rather than through a richer account of who that person is.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The claim is that machine-learning personalization moves beyond visible demographic grouping while extending the older drive to narrow risk pools.

The evidence is the use of large quantities of behavioral data for recommendations, pricing, medicine, and other individualized interventions.

The interpretation is that the relevant individual becomes a predictive object - a collection of data from which the system estimates a future propensity. Personalization therefore intensifies individualization while still relying on patterns extracted from populations.

## Prediction as a Model of Governance and Agency

The lecture concludes by arguing that prediction has become more than a technical operation. It is increasingly a model for rational decision-making, governance, intelligence, and agency.

The ubiquity of prediction appears in predictive policing, prediction markets, estimates of catastrophic artificial-intelligence risks, and machine learning itself. The lecture notes that machine learning is fundamentally associated here with statistical prediction. Some philosophical accounts even describe intelligence as prediction.

This orientation changes how people relate to the future. Prediction competes with or reorganizes other future-oriented practices such as hoping, projecting, waiting, planning, expecting, and fearing. A predicted future becomes a trajectory constructed from a selective reading of past data.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The claim is that prediction is becoming a dominant template for organizing decisions and imagining rational agency.

The evidence is its use across governance, markets, technology, and arguments about intelligence and future risk.

The lecture interprets prediction as constraining the future to patterns selected from the past. It closes with an unresolved question: what is at stake when the capacity to create or choose futures is increasingly captured by prediction?

## Human Contexts and Ethics Toolkit

### Power

The lecture presents risk technologies as forms of power because they structure people's opportunities and behavior. Insurers determine which characteristics count as risk and translate those classifications into premiums. Credit-scoring systems affect access to capital and therefore the futures people can pursue. Welfare systems encode eligibility rules, deny benefits at scale, and can make those denials harder to contest when human decision-makers are removed.

Choice architecture and hypernudges demonstrate another form of technological power. Platforms alter a person's decision environment in response to continuously updated predictions. This power can operate without an explicit command or the person's awareness that their conduct is being directed.

Scientific, technological, and political power remain intertwined throughout the lecture. Statistical descriptions of accident rates contributed to shifting responsibility from injured individuals toward workplaces and collective institutions. Later, actuarial classifications supported a political order that placed more responsibility back on individuals. Risk measurement therefore participates in decisions about who must bear costs, who deserves support, and which institutions are responsible for security.

### Sociotechnical Systems

The lecture treats risk assessment as inseparable from the institutions and social relations in which it operates. Maritime probabilities depended on investors, shared funds, historical shipping data, contracts, and commercial goals. Industrial accident statistics acquired political significance through factories, courts, labor organization, compensation systems, and changing accounts of responsibility.

Actuarial fairness similarly cannot be isolated as a neutral mathematical technique. Risk pools reflect judgments about relevant categories, insurers' competitive interests, legal restrictions, available data, and cultural ideas about personal responsibility. Removing gender as an explicit category did not end the surrounding classificatory system; it prompted insurers to gather additional data and construct narrower proxies.

Contemporary automation continues this interdependence. A welfare eligibility system joins technical rules to administrative cost cutting and political judgments about deservingness. A credit score links data infrastructure to lenders, individual self-management, and class differentiation. A personalized platform combines prediction, interface design, observation, and user behavior in a continuous feedback loop.

### Identity/Positionality

Gender is central to the insurance case. Women were treated through a group classification that insurers considered predictive, while activists argued that an immutable and socially consequential identity should not determine individual premiums. The dispute illustrates positionality because institutional treatment depended on how insurers categorized a person, not simply on the person's self-understanding.

Class is also produced and reinforced through risk systems. Poorer borrowers may be charged more because they are predicted to have greater difficulty repaying, but the higher cost of credit can itself reinforce their economic position. Credit scores shape access to resources while encouraging individuals to manage the data that institutions use to place them within a social hierarchy.

The lecture also notes that welfare programs with universal aspirations could reinforce racial inequalities. It does not provide a detailed case explaining those mechanisms, so that point cannot be developed further from these notes. More broadly, the lecture shows that identity categories and economic position can become inputs into systems that distribute prices, opportunities, and obligations.

### Narratives

Several competing narratives organize the lecture. The solidaristic narrative treats risks such as industrial accidents, unemployment, or economic depression as shared conditions that no individual fully controls. It supports pooling resources and providing security according to need.

The actuarial narrative says that fairness requires each person to bear the cost of their own predicted risk. It depicts individualized pricing as rational, discourages allegedly reckless behavior, and can make unequal treatment appear justified when predicted risks differ.

The neoliberal narrative extends this logic by presenting individuals as responsible for managing their behavior, data, scores, and future prospects within markets. Automated systems can then appear to distribute only what each person has earned or deserves, while the social conditions producing risk receive less attention.

Finally, the narrative of prediction presents the future as a continuation of patterns extracted from past data. Its growing prominence can make predictive governance and predictive accounts of intelligence appear natural or inevitable. The lecture asks readers to question what becomes less visible when hoping, planning, projecting, and creating futures are subordinated to statistical prediction.
