# L15 Making Decisions

## Executive Summary

The lecture examined the familiar idea that a life can be described as a sequence of decisions. It contrasted deliberate choices with events, relationships, preferences, and forms of growth that are not experienced as discrete decisions, introducing agency as the capacity of individuals or collectives to act and influence their environments.

The central historical argument was that decision making became a culturally prominent model of agency after World War II. Earlier histories of legal and political decisions and economic or electoral choices converged with cybernetics, behaviorism, game theory, rational choice theory, administrative science, cognitive science, and behavioral economics. These fields formalized rational action as selecting among structured alternatives, often according to calculable rules.

The lecture then argued that models of decision making are performative: institutions, policies, and technologies build structured choices into the world and thereby help produce people as decision makers. Choice architecture, social media experiments, navigation systems, and personalized digital platforms illustrated how actors can preserve nominal choice while shaping the conditions under which others act. The concluding concept of co-production described technical models of agency and forms of social order as historically making and remaking one another.

## Life as a Sequence of Decisions

The lecture began with the proposition that describing a life as a sequence of decisions distinguishes a person who actively takes charge from one who passively reacts. In this view, important moments appear as junctions at which alternatives are available: a person chooses one path, that decision establishes the conditions for later decisions, and the resulting sequence comes to define the life.

The instructor also synthesized a challenge to this account. Much of a life consists of circumstances and events over which a person has little or no control. Decisions suggest agency, choice, and the possibility of doing otherwise, but what happens to a person may be just as important to having a life as what that person deliberately chooses.

A further complication concerned identity across possible choices. The lecture discussed the idea that if a person remains identifiable across possible worlds in which different decisions are made, identity cannot be exhausted by the particular decisions taken. This challenges the image of life as a choose-your-own-adventure story or video game made primarily from branching junctions.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

- The initial claim was that contemporary people commonly understand action and identity through decisive moments in which an agent selects among alternatives.
- The lecture complicated that claim with experiences that do not fit discrete choice, including learning a first language, acquiring preferences, growing into expectations, and being affected by uncontrolled events.
- The instructor interpreted these examples as reasons to distinguish agency from a simple sequence of explicit decisions. People may participate actively in a process without having chosen it at a single identifiable moment.

### Examples

A person may identify attending community college or transferring to Berkeley as a defining choice because one decision establishes later possibilities. By contrast, a person may never have explicitly decided on a favorite movie until being asked to name one. The question itself can organize an unsettled preference into a stated choice.

Learning a first language also involves activity and agency, but the lecture presented it as something a person grows into rather than something selected from a menu at one discrete moment. College attendance can work similarly: some students may remember deciding to attend, while others may have experienced college as an established expectation before encountering any explicit alternative.

## Agency and the Decision-Making Model

The lecture defined agency as the ability of an individual or collective to act, exert influence over an environment, initiate something new, or bring about a new state of affairs. Agency is not restricted to individual humans. Institutions can have agency, and the emergence of AI agents may be changing what the term itself means.

The lecture emphasized that agency has a history. This does not mean only that different people possess different amounts of agency in different settings. It also means that the recognized meaning and form of agency change over time.

The contemporary decision-making model describes human beings as agents who:

- Encounter several available options at discrete moments.
- Possess preferences relevant to those options.
- Freely select the best, optimal, or most rational option.
- Follow rules that can identify what the best decision would be.
- Build a life or course of action from a sequence of such selections.

According to the lecture, this framework has become so familiar that it can seem universally applicable. Almost any action can be redescribed as a decision, including the behavior of animals or plants. That apparent universality is precisely why the lecture sought to historicize and denaturalize it.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The lecture's main claim was not that decision making is false, arbitrary, or useless. It was that decision making should be treated as a historically made tool or artifact rather than as the self-evident substance of all action.

As evidence for its historical specificity, the instructor referred to a graph showing that the English phrase "decision making" was rare or effectively absent before a sharp rise beginning in the 1940s and accelerating in the 1950s and 1960s.

The instructor interpreted this linguistic pattern as an invitation to ask how people understood action before decision making became culturally salient. The graph alone did not establish the causes of the change. It instead oriented the lecture toward World War II, the Cold War, scientific models of choice, and the institutions that made those models operative.

## Earlier Histories of Decisions and Choices

The lecture separated two histories that preceded the postwar concept of decision making.

"Decision" emerged from legal and political contexts. It referred especially to what judges, politicians, experts, bureaucrats, or sovereigns did. A decision could result from experienced judgment or prudence rather than from following a formal rule. In some accounts, a true decision went beyond what reasons and calculations could fully determine. It could resemble a leap of faith or an exercise of spontaneous will.

The lecture also emphasized finality. The Latin etymology presented in the lecture connected decision with cutting. A decision cuts across time by establishing that what follows will differ from what came before.

"Choice" developed more strongly in economic and, later, electoral settings. It described consumers selecting among goods in a marketplace and voters selecting a candidate from a slate. The lecture connected the growing importance of choice to the expanding role of capitalist markets in everyday life, to marginalist economics' emphasis on consumption, and to representative democratic politics. It also noted the historical importance of the language of choice within feminism.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The lecture argued that the postwar model joined these partially distinct histories. The finality and authoritative force of decision became connected to the structured selection among alternatives associated with markets and voting. Their convergence helped produce a formal and mathematical account of rational decision making that could be applied across domains.

## World War II, the Cold War, and the Cyborg Sciences

The lecture placed the rise of decision science in the institutional context of World War II and the Cold War. Wartime mobilization created problems of logistics, resource allocation, weapons supply, and optimization. Nuclear weapons subsequently intensified concern that frail or irrational human judgment could produce catastrophic outcomes.

The political struggle against Nazism and Soviet communism also encouraged ideological emphasis on the free individual and the market. According to the lecture, these institutional and political conditions helped make the freely choosing individual central to emerging sciences of choice.

The instructor grouped several related fields under the broad description of the "cyborg sciences." These fields developed close to military institutions and blurred boundaries among humans, animals, machines, minds, and organizations. They searched for general principles that could describe purposive, self-regulating, and organizing systems of many kinds.

### Cybernetics

Cybernetics emerged in the 1940s as an interdisciplinary project seeking a universal science of organization and purposive behavior. Its participants came from fields including physics, biology, political science, and anthropology.

The lecture described feedback as a basic cybernetic mechanism. A system acts, receives information about the consequences of that action, and changes its subsequent behavior. A thermostat illustrated this pattern: it measures temperature and activates heating or cooling in response, maintaining a homeostatic balance.

Mathematical information theory supported the possibility of treating what matters in reality as flows of information and viewing reality as fundamentally computational. This allowed living beings, machines, and social systems to be compared through their organization and information processing rather than treated as categorically separate.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The instructor interpreted cybernetics as part of the intellectual setting in which purpose could be explained mechanically. A goal-directed action no longer had to be explained by appealing to a future end as a cause. It could instead be modeled through feedback, information, and self-regulation.

The lecture also connected cybernetics to the history of artificial intelligence, while noting that the relationship was complicated and would receive later attention.

## Behaviorism and Reinforcement

Behaviorism described organisms as stimulus-response systems whose actions change according to their consequences. The lecture associated this approach especially with B. F. Skinner and operant conditioning.

Skinner boxes were presented as environments for training rats and mice by reinforcing desired behavior and punishing undesired behavior. The instructor connected this mechanism to what is now called reinforcement learning: actions associated with rewards become more likely, while punished actions become less likely.

The point was not merely to observe animals neutrally. Experimental environments made behavior intelligible and predictable by actively producing repeatable relationships between interventions and responses.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

Behaviorism did not necessarily posit an interior mind consciously selecting among possibilities. Nevertheless, the lecture connected it to decision making because behavior could be modeled as the selection of an action from a space of possible actions according to rules established through reinforcement.

The instructor also emphasized behaviorism's political ambition. Skinner's 1948 utopian novel *Walden Two* imagined a society whose environments were scientifically designed to train people into good behavior. The lecture described the novel as strikingly dystopian to many readers and noted that it appeared in the same year as George Orwell's *1984*.

Behaviorist mechanisms subsequently entered systems such as gamification, where rewards shape user behavior. They also became central to reinforcement learning in machine learning and AI.

## Game Theory and Rational Choice

Game theory and rational choice theory attempted to formalize what decision making involves and, especially, what the best decision would be under given circumstances. The lecture traced game theory to mathematics and economics and associated its familiar formulation with John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern.

Game theory defined rationality through optimal strategic decision making. Logical and mathematical rules could specify the best strategy, making rationality calculable, computable, and potentially algorithmic. This promised a way to bypass human fallibility at a time when strategic mistakes involving nuclear weapons appeared capable of ending civilization.

The model treated the agent as an isolated decision maker who takes in information, surveys a system from above, selects an action, observes what happens, and chooses again. Rational choice economics similarly modeled agents as selecting the course of action that promised the greatest payoff or expected utility.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The instructor distinguished normative from descriptive purposes. Rational choice was not necessarily offered as an empirical description of how every person actually behaves. It established what an ideally rational decision would look like.

This model linked rationality to rule following. If the rules for an optimal choice could be made explicit, a machine as well as a human could carry them out. The lecture treated this as an important step toward automated decision making.

Bayesian decision theory and statistical decision theory were mentioned as related approaches in which uncertainty and available data are incorporated into choosing an action. The detailed connection to a referenced data course was [unclear] in these notes.

## Herbert Simon, Bounded Rationality, and Cognitive Models

Herbert Simon served as a connecting figure across administrative science, cognitive science, artificial intelligence, and behavioral economics. His concept of bounded rationality modified the ideal of fully informed optimization without abandoning the framework of rational decision making.

Bounded rationality describes agents as making the best decision they can under constraints of limited time, limited information, and a complex environment. This abstraction could apply to people, organizations, bureaucracies, and artificial systems.

Simon modeled bureaucracies as information-processing, problem-solving, and decision-making machines. A decision became the output of a process in which information was organized and a problem was rendered manageable. His work was both descriptive and normative: it asked how organizations actually decide and how their decisions might be improved.

Cognitive science similarly compared the mind to a computer. Because the world contains more complexity and information than a person can process, the mind must use simplifications, schemas, and heuristics. These devices divide the world into more manageable and decidable parts.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The lecture argued that analogizing thought to computation encouraged machine-like accounts of human cognition. Human thought became legible insofar as it could be represented as information processing.

The instructor also stressed that simplification makes the world actionable. A complex environment must be cut into digestible units before an agent or organization can decide what to do. This process does not merely discover ready-made decisions; it helps construct the alternatives and problems to which decision procedures can be applied.

## Behavioral Economics and Human Departures from Optimization

Behavioral economics examined how actual human decisions depart from the ideals specified by rational choice theory. The lecture linked its development to work in psychology and economics during the 1970s and to Simon's account of bounded rationality.

The field investigated heuristics, biases, and the ways uncertainty affects judgment. As one example, the lecture stated that people do not respond symmetrically to potential losses and gains. They may prefer security and a lower reward over a larger but riskier possible reward.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

Behavioral economics supplied a more descriptive account of human decision making, but the lecture argued that it retained the same basic conception of rationality as optimization. Rather than replacing the optimal decision with another definition of rational action, it explained why human beings frequently fail to reach the established optimum.

The instructor noted that behavioral economics experienced a resurgence in the twenty-first century and also referred to scandals involving research quality, non-replicable studies, and fabricated data. These points were presented briefly and without detailed cases.

More importantly for the lecture's argument, behavioral economics became part of the intellectual infrastructure of institutions. Its models were used not just to explain behavior but to design settings that shape behavior.

## Performativity and Choice Architecture

The lecture described theories of decision making as performative. They do not merely observe a preexisting activity called decision making. When incorporated into bureaucracies, policies, media platforms, and AI systems, they help produce the form of agency they claim to describe.

Choice architecture was the main example. Associated in the lecture with Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein's book *Nudge*, it refers to arranging material or digital environments so that they direct attention and make some actions easier or more likely than others. It was presented as influential in the Obama administration.

A nudge does not remove choice altogether. Instead, a designer specifies a limited set of alternatives and organizes the setting to increase the probability of the preferred outcome. The person therefore retains the experience of deciding while acting within conditions established by someone else.

### Examples

A parent may ask a child whether to brush their teeth before or after another bedtime activity. The choice concerns timing rather than whether tooth brushing will occur. The child receives alternatives, but the parent defines their scope.

In a school lunch line, healthier food can be placed where it is more visible and accessible than less healthy food. Students can still select either, but the arrangement is intended to increase the frequency of the preferred selection.

Default settings in digital services similarly make the designer's preferred action easiest to accept. Social media feeds, search engines, recommendations, and interface layouts can structure what users notice and which options appear available.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The lecture interpreted choice architecture as both a political theory and a political technology. It organizes governance through what Thaler and Sunstein called "libertarian paternalism":

- It is paternalistic because an authority defines an outcome as being in another person's interest and designs the environment to encourage it.
- It is libertarian because the person formally retains the ability to choose otherwise.

This arrangement raises questions about who defines the good outcome, designs the utility function, establishes the available options, and governs the people operating within the architecture. The lecture described this as a form of second-order decision making: some actors do not directly make another person's choice, but they decide the conditions and scope within which that person chooses.

## Cybernetic Choice Architecture and Hypernudges

Digital platforms intensify choice architecture by making it dynamic, personalized, continuous, and scalable. The lecture called this cybernetic choice architecture and also used the term "hypernudges."

The process operates as a feedback loop:

1. A user acts within a designed environment.
2. The platform observes the action and converts it into data.
3. The institution assesses whether the behavior matches its preferred outcome.
4. The platform modifies the choice environment.
5. The user encounters a new set or arrangement of choices, and the cycle continues.

The lecture contrasted a speed bump with a navigation application. A speed bump encourages drivers to slow down but remains fixed and works similarly for everyone. A navigation application monitors location, responds when a user ignores a direction, and continually produces an updated route toward the destination.

### Facebook Voting Experiment

The instructor referred to an experiment conducted by Facebook and academic collaborators more than fifteen years earlier. By changing messages shown to users, the experiment reportedly demonstrated with statistical significance that platform design could affect whether people went out to vote. The lecture clarified that the experiment concerned turnout rather than the selection of a particular candidate.

This case supported the claim that platforms can affect political behavior by changing the information and prompts users encounter, even without directly commanding them to act.

### Data-Driven Behavior Modification

The lecture quoted an anonymous chief data scientist at a large Silicon Valley education company, as cited by Shoshana Zuboff in *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism*. The quoted vision treated behavior change at scale as the goal of data-driven systems. An application captures behavior, classifies actions as good or bad, develops interventions intended to elicit preferred conduct, observes the response, and adjusts the environment again.

The precise wording of parts of the quotation was [unclear], but its stated role in the lecture was to illustrate continuous surveillance and adjustment of choice architecture.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The instructor argued that these systems preserve a formal space for individual choice while giving platform designers substantial influence over the probability of different actions. Power therefore becomes less visibly coercive, more personalized, and increasingly dependent on data.

## From Human Decision Makers to Automated Decisions

The lecture connected the history of decision making to automated decision systems. Its central chronological argument was that machines could not be understood as making decisions until human beings had first been reconstructed as entities whose agency consists in decision making.

Human beings also had to be represented in machine-like terms. Models of information processing, optimization, rules, and feedback made the actions of people, organizations, animals, and machines comparable. Once rationality was defined as applying rules to information in order to select an optimal option, decision making could be delegated to or reproduced by machines.

The instructor linked this argument to the preceding lecture's discussion of quantification, objectivity, and impersonal rules. Institutions can become more powerful by restricting the discretion and subjectivity of individual workers. Decisions then appear to result from procedures rather than from a particular person's judgment.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The lecture did not claim that automated decision making began with contemporary AI. It presented rule-based and algorithmic systems as preceding the current AI era by decades. The history of postwar decision sciences established concepts and institutional practices that made automation conceivable.

The instructor indicated that the following lecture would focus more directly on algorithmic and automated decision making, including forms that could be described as pre-AI.

## Co-Production of Agency and Social Order

Co-production was introduced as a Human Contexts and Ethics tool for examining how human and technical elements make one another historically. It rejects an account in which either "the social" fully explains technology or technology independently determines society.

Technical models of rational choice, choice architecture, and artificial intelligence develop alongside particular ideas about the human agent and social order. The lecture connected an individualistic conception of agency to a managerial conception of society. From a view above the system, social order appears as the aggregate of many individual decisions coordinated through technological interventions that nominally preserve freedom.

Under this model, better social outcomes can be pursued by making individual choices more informed, predictable, and rational. Automated decision systems can then appear as ways to augment or replace fallible human judgment.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The instructor argued that models of agency and forms of governance mutually shape one another. Scientific concepts become embedded in institutions, while institutional needs help determine which scientific concepts are developed and treated as useful.

The lecture therefore proposed evaluating decision making as an artifact. Historicizing it reveals how it was made, what work it performs, what social arrangements it supports, and which forms of a good life it promotes. This approach does not require discarding decision making. It makes the model available for critical examination.

The lecture concluded by asking what the decision-making framework leaves out. Literature and other accounts of human life may disclose dynamics, passions, interiority, growth, and forms of action that cannot be captured fully as discrete selections among alternatives.

## Human Contexts and Ethics Toolkit

### Power

The lecture explicitly connected decision making to the capacity to influence other people's conduct. Choice architects exercise asymmetric power by defining available alternatives, arranging their visibility, establishing defaults, and deciding which outcome should be encouraged. The person subject to the architecture may retain a formal ability to choose, but the designer governs the conditions under which that choice occurs.

The school lunch counter illustrates this distinction. Students make the immediate selection, while another actor determines the arrangement intended to make one selection more likely. The parent who offers limited bedtime alternatives similarly decides the scope of the child's decision before the child chooses within it.

Digital platforms intensify this power because they can surveil behavior, turn it into data, classify outcomes, and continually adjust the environment. The Facebook voting experiment supported the lecture's claim that control over messages and interfaces can influence political participation. The quoted Silicon Valley data scientist made behavior modification itself the stated objective of a data-driven platform.

The lecture also related scientific and technological power to political power. Wartime logistics, nuclear strategy, Cold War ideology, public policy, and platform governance were not external applications of neutral decision science. They were institutional contexts that shaped the development and use of its concepts.

### Sociotechnical Systems

The lecture treated decision making as a sociotechnical formation rather than an isolated mental operation or technical procedure. Scientific models, military problems, bureaucratic organizations, economic theories, political ideals, interfaces, data collection, and users' actions all participated in producing decision-making systems.

Cybernetic choice architecture provides the clearest example. A user's action cannot be isolated from the platform that presents options, records behavior, evaluates it, and changes what the user sees next. The technical components operate through institutional goals and classifications, while those goals become effective through software, interfaces, and data.

Co-production extended this analysis historically. Models of machine-like rationality helped constitute a society organized around structured choices, while institutions built around administration, markets, and governance created demand for calculable decision models. Human and technical agency therefore emerged through their interaction rather than as independently fixed categories.

### Identity/Positionality

The lecture discussed identity primarily through the question of whether a person is defined by a sequence of decisions. Its examples suggested that selfhood also develops through uncontrolled events, language learning, inherited expectations, relationships, and preferences that may only become explicit when a question or institution demands a choice.

The discussion of college illustrated socially conditioned agency. For some students, attending college may appear as a discrete personal decision. For others, it was described as something always expected, which complicates the narrative of an autonomous individual selecting freely from equivalent paths.

These notes briefly named feminism as a historical domain in which the language of choice became especially powerful. It did not develop a specific analysis of gender, race, class, disability, immigration, or income, so the lecture does not provide enough material for a more detailed application of those dimensions of positionality.

### Narratives

The lecture's main target was the narrative that a human life is fundamentally a sequence of choices made by an autonomous decision maker. This narrative explains who people are, how action works, and what rational conduct requires. Because almost any behavior can be redescribed as a decision, the account can come to seem natural and universal.

Historicizing decision making challenged that inevitability. The rise of the phrase after World War II, the earlier separation of decisions from choices, and the institutional development of the cyborg sciences showed how the narrative emerged under particular conditions.

Behaviorism, rational choice, bounded rationality, and behavioral economics supplied different narratives of human action. They variously described people as stimulus-response systems, optimizers, constrained information processors, or predictably biased choosers. These accounts did not remain only theories. Through bureaucracies, policies, platforms, and interfaces, they helped create environments in which people increasingly encountered themselves as decision makers.

The lecture's alternative narrative remained open rather than fully specified. It pointed toward lives shaped by growth, passions, interiority, relationships, and events that do not take the form of discrete choices. This opening supported the broader argument that decision making is one powerful model of agency, not the only imaginable account of what it means to act.
