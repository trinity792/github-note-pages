# L27 Environmental Contexts and Consequences of Data

## Executive Summary

The lecture challenged the narrative that computing is a clean, immaterial, post-industrial activity. It first traced the material history of Silicon Valley through land development, semiconductor fabrication, toxic chemical exposure, groundwater contamination, and environmental activism. It then introduced externalities and invisibilization as concepts for understanding why computing’s costs are often excluded from products’ public presentation and shifted onto workers and communities.

The lecture extended this analysis to the Amazon Echo and other AI-enabled devices, whose apparent convenience depends on planetary networks of mineral extraction, manufacturing, labor, data, transportation, energy, and waste disposal. It concluded with data centers as the increasingly visible material infrastructure of AI, emphasizing their demands for land, electricity, water, construction, and public subsidies. Local opposition to data centers was presented not merely as a reaction to aggregate resource consumption, but as politics shaped by specific places, histories of industrial betrayal, unequal bargaining power, and distrust of corporations and government.

## Changing Awareness of Computing’s Environmental Effects

The lecture opened by describing a substantial recent change in student responses to a recurring survey statement about computing and environmental impact. In spring 2024, the class was comparatively divided: roughly 54 percent disagreed or strongly disagreed with the statement, while about 46 percent agreed [the statement itself is unclear]. In more recent semesters, the proportion disagreeing reportedly moved closer to 90 percent, with one figure given as approximately 88 percent.

The instructor interpreted this movement as part of a broader change in public awareness. Environmental concerns about mining, resource development, and technological production had existed for years, but the public narrative around AI shifted sharply as data centers became more visible. Their electricity, water, land, and other resource requirements made the material consequences of computing harder to ignore.

The introductory discussion identified batteries as an accessible example. Computing-related devices, electric vehicles, phones, and laptops rely on materials such as lithium and cobalt. The lecture connected cobalt extraction in the Democratic Republic of the Congo to child labor, exploitation, dangerous working conditions, environmental damage, and displacement.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

- **Claim:** Public awareness of computing’s environmental materiality has changed dramatically in the last few years.
- **Evidence:** The instructor cited changing responses to a recurring class poll and the increasing prominence of public stories about AI data centers.
- **Interpretation:** The instructor associated the change less with the sudden appearance of environmental effects than with their growing visibility through the rapid expansion of data-center infrastructure.

## The Clean and Immaterial Narrative of Silicon Valley

An image from the Stanford Research Park website served as an entry point into an alternative history of Silicon Valley. The image foregrounded trees, open vistas, hills, water, quiet, and a broad natural landscape. Buildings and industrial infrastructure were barely visible. The instructor interpreted this composition as communicating serenity, spaciousness, and harmony between technology and nature.

The Stanford Industrial Park, later called Stanford Research Park, was established in the 1950s through a partnership between Stanford University and Palo Alto. Despite its presentation through park-like imagery, it was an industrial workplace and an early center of what came to be described as the knowledge or post-industrial economy. This economy was defined in contrast to smokestacks, coal, noise, cars, and the visible pollution associated with earlier heavy industry.

The lecture quoted a May 1982 description of the San Jose horizon as “unmarred by smokestacks,” with local pride attached to the idea that the computer-electronics industry made Silicon Valley an unpolluted place to live. This supported a larger narrative in which technology appeared clean, natural, vaporous, cloud-like, and focused on information rather than material production.

The instructor argued that this narrative was reinforced by structural changes in the United States economy. Manufacturing regions experienced recession and decline while Silicon Valley represented growth during the 1970s. Yet the new economy still depended on land, water, energy, industrial facilities, and political decisions about development.

### Land, Annexation, and Growth

In the early twentieth century, the San Jose area was primarily agricultural. Beginning around the 1940s, local growth advocates influenced city government and sought to diversify the economy by attracting high-technology firms and expanding the tax base. Large-scale annexations during the 1950s and 1960s converted land for industrial and technological development.

The lecture presented this process as political rather than natural. Government zoning and development served particular economic goals. The effects included the displacement of immigrant and low-income communities to make room for technology parks. Silicon Valley’s knowledge economy therefore rested on material changes to land and on decisions about whose homes and communities could be displaced.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

- **Claim:** Silicon Valley’s public identity as a clean, post-industrial economy obscured its dependence on material resources and industrial development.
- **Evidence:** The lecture contrasted the Stanford Research Park’s nature-centered imagery and the “unmarred by smokestacks” description with the region’s workplaces, annexations, resource requirements, and semiconductor manufacturing.
- **Interpretation:** The absence of conventional industrial symbols did not mean the absence of industry. Instead, it helped distinguish high technology from older manufacturing and made its environmental costs less visible.

## Semiconductor Manufacturing and the Meaning of “Clean”

Microelectronics fabrication is resource-intensive. According to the lecture, it requires complex processes, substantial energy, greenhouse-gas-intensive activity, hazardous chemicals, and the release of pollution into the environment.

Production facilities nevertheless appear exceptionally clean because semiconductor manufacturing requires highly controlled conditions. Tiny impurities can damage products, so “clean rooms” must protect fabrication processes from contamination. The instructor emphasized that the cleanliness of the interior production space should not be confused with the environmental cleanliness of the process as a whole.

A 1964 photograph from Fairchild Semiconductor illustrated this distinction. Workers were separated from the materials by a glass enclosure. The enclosure prevented breath and other contaminants from affecting production, while also offering some separation between workers and hazardous chemicals. The image also showed that nearly all the workers were women. The lecture stated that women, immigrants, or people occupying both categories made up much of the semiconductor production workforce in Silicon Valley.

These workers often lived near the factories. Their jobs and homes could therefore expose them to toxic substances. The lecture characterized semiconductor manufacturing positions as among the twentieth century’s more hazardous manufacturing jobs, despite the clean appearance of the facilities and products.

The production of an eight-inch silicon wafer was used to illustrate the magnitude of material inputs, wastewater, and solid waste involved. The precise figures shown in the lecture were not recoverable from these notes, but the example’s purpose was to demonstrate that a small, precise technological component required a much larger material process.

## Chemical Pollution, Fairchild Semiconductor, and Superfund Sites

Waste chemicals from electronics production were sometimes dumped directly into soil. In other cases, they were placed in poorly designed storage tanks that spilled or leaked into groundwater. The instructor stated that early Silicon Valley pioneers had described simply dumping chemicals outside a back door. Regulation was limited, and toxic waste did not fit the dominant story of a clean, futuristic technology economy.

Awareness began to change during the environmental movements of the 1970s and, more specifically in Silicon Valley, during the early 1980s. The lecture focused on trichloroethane, or TCA, a petroleum-based solvent used to degrease microchips and printed circuit boards. It had been adopted as a substitute for TCE, which the industry had begun phasing out. The lecture associated workplace exposure to TCA with dizziness, headaches, and fainting, and discussed evidence connecting TCE exposure to cancer, disease, reproductive effects, and birth defects. Some chemical names and distinctions in this portion of these notes are difficult to recover precisely.

When chemicals enter groundwater, the lecture explained, they can also move through cracks and building foundations into homes and classrooms. Thus, contamination originating in an industrial process can become an exposure problem across workplaces and residential spaces.

### The Fairchild Leak

In 1981, TCA contamination was discovered in drinking-water wells serving approximately 16,500 people in South San Jose. The lecture stated that 14,000 gallons of TCA and another 44,000 gallons of toxic waste had leaked from an underground storage tank for at least a year and a half. Fairchild Semiconductor was identified as the responsible company.

Gloria Ross, described as the mother of an infant born with heart defects, became an activist in response. Residents presented evidence to San Jose officials concerning miscarriages and birth defects in the neighborhood. Their organizing helped galvanize a local movement, produced new organizations, and contributed to the site’s listing under the federal Superfund program.

The lecture explained a Superfund site as an area recognized by the Environmental Protection Agency as contaminated by hazardous material and requiring significant long-term remediation. In 2002, Santa Clara County reportedly had 29 Superfund sites, the highest number of any county in the country, and 24 were attributed to electronics firms. In 2022, 23 of those sites reportedly remained, with the instructor estimating that approximately 21 were still active at the time of the lecture.

Removal from the Superfund list does not necessarily mean that all chemicals have been physically eliminated. The lecture explained that remediation can instead mean containing contaminated soil or separating it from groundwater through barriers such as concrete.

### A Pattern Rather Than an Isolated Actor

The instructor cautioned against reducing the history to a simple story about one evil company. Similar contamination occurred around IBM, Teledyne, Intel, and Raytheon facilities. One cited finding stated that 80 percent of relevant firms had hazardous chemicals in the ground near their plants [the exact scope of the finding is unclear].

The important issue was the set of historical and institutional dynamics producing the harms:

- Semiconductor production technically depended on toxic materials, intensive cleaning, and extremely exacting specifications.
- Petrochemical solvents developed after 1945 were both effective and hazardous.
- Pressure to scale production quickly discouraged slower or less resource-intensive approaches.
- Military production specifications contributed to demanding manufacturing processes.
- Former agricultural wells created pathways through which chemicals could travel into groundwater.
- Facilities were located in communities with comparatively little political power.
- Workplace health effects were inadequately studied, and regulatory protections were weak.

### Unequal Distribution

Superfund sites were not evenly distributed throughout the region. The lecture described their concentration in comparatively poor and immigrant communities, including Mountain View and Sunnyvale. Those receiving the greatest economic benefits from the technology industry were often the most insulated from its environmental costs, while those receiving fewer benefits experienced greater exposure.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

- **Claim:** The environmental hazards of Silicon Valley were systemic consequences of production processes, growth pressures, land history, weak regulation, and unequal political power.
- **Evidence:** The Fairchild leak, related contamination around other electronics firms, the number and location of Superfund sites, former agricultural wells, and the demographic composition of manufacturing labor all supported this account.
- **Interpretation:** The contamination was not merely an accidental departure from an otherwise clean industry. It revealed the material basis of an industry whose dominant narrative had excluded waste, exposure, and affected communities.

## Externalities and Invisibility

The lecture introduced two concepts for generalizing from Silicon Valley’s history.

### Negative Externalities

A negative externality is a harm or indirect cost not paid by the people who benefit from a product, service, or outcome. Instead, the cost is transferred to others. Environmental pollution is a central example.

In Silicon Valley, firms and consumers benefited from electronics production while workers, nearby residents, and public institutions bore costs associated with illness, contaminated water, and long-term cleanup. Because those costs were excluded from the ordinary cost of doing business, they were externalized.

### Invisibility and Invisibilization

Invisibilization is the exclusion of people, labor, harms, problems, or effects from what society sees, hears, and discusses. The instructor emphasized that invisibility does not simply happen. It is produced through active choices about where attention is directed.

A product launch may display a new phone through clean surfaces, polished design, and carefully controlled presentation while omitting overseas manufacturing plants, poorly paid workers, mineral extraction, pollution, and disposal. The smooth surface of a product or service directs attention toward convenience and away from the wider network that makes it possible.

The lecture connected invisibilization to the earlier concept of invisible labor. Computing systems can appear immaterial because the labor, infrastructure, resources, and pollution underlying them remain outside the user’s immediate field of view.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

- **Claim:** Environmental and labor costs can be simultaneously externalized and made invisible.
- **Evidence:** Semiconductor companies did not incorporate the full consequences of chemical pollution into products’ visible costs, while public representations emphasized clean facilities, natural landscapes, and polished technological objects.
- **Interpretation:** Externalization explains who carries the cost, while invisibilization explains why that allocation may remain absent from dominant understandings of technology.

## The Amazon Echo and the Planetary Anatomy of AI

The lecture turned from semiconductor production to the Amazon Echo as a way to examine the infrastructure behind a seemingly small act of technological convenience. An Echo can answer a question, play a song, control lighting or air conditioning, change a temperature, or order groceries. The interaction may appear immediate and immaterial, but it activates a much larger network.

Kate Crawford and Vladan Joler’s “Anatomy of an AI System” was presented as both an argument and a visualization. It maps the birth, life, and death of a single Amazon Echo and the infrastructure activated when a user makes a request. The lecture quoted its claim that each small moment of convenience requires a vast planetary network fueled by the extraction of nonrenewable materials, labor, and data.

The visualization organizes that network around three kinds of extraction:

- Material resources are extracted to produce the device and its supporting infrastructure.
- Human labor is extracted across mining, manufacturing, assembly, shipping, data labeling, engineering, and corporate management.
- Data is extracted through user interaction and the processes involved in building and operating AI systems.

Read from left to right, the diagram follows the object from raw-material extraction through manufacturing and transportation, then through the infrastructure and human activity required to operate it, and finally to disposal. Its story begins and ends with the Earth and with geological processes operating over deep time.

### Material Resources and Waste

The Echo depends on minerals used in batteries, screens, electronic components, and servers. The lecture mentioned lithium, cobalt, neodymium, and europium. Mining can require substantial water, while smelting and refining can produce pollution. These processes can damage communities and ecosystems, often far from the users who benefit from the finished products.

The device and its battery have limited lifespans. Amazon discourages users from opening and repairing an Echo because doing so voids the warranty. Once the device or battery is no longer usable, it may enter an electronic-waste stream.

The instructor drew attention to contrasting timescales. Materials formed through geological processes over millions of years can be consumed and converted into waste within a few years to deliver convenience measured in seconds or microseconds.

### Labor and Geographic Distribution

Human labor appears throughout the system: mining, refining, shipping, manufacturing, assembly, data labeling, data science, electrical engineering, and executive management. These forms of labor are geographically and economically unequal.

The lecture stated that the lowest wages and greatest exposure to danger often fall on workers in the Global South, including people in conflict-affected regions where extraction is intertwined with local violence and political instability. The environmental costs once borne by manufacturing workers in Silicon Valley have, in significant part, been moved abroad as electronics manufacturing shifted away from the Bay Area.

### Cobalt in the Democratic Republic of the Congo

Cobalt was used as the principal example. The lecture described growing demand as having severe consequences in southern Congo, a region said to contain approximately 3.4 million metric tons of cobalt, nearly half of known reserves. Artisanal mines were associated with child labor, forced or slave labor, dangerous conditions, and the displacement of people whose villages were bulldozed after mining concessions were granted to corporations.

The case supported the larger point that the environmental and human costs of computing have not disappeared. They have been distributed through global supply chains and placed at a distance from many consumers.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

- **Claim:** A smart device is not an isolated consumer object but the visible endpoint of a planetary system of extraction, labor, data, infrastructure, and disposal.
- **Evidence:** The Echo case linked raw materials, manufacturing, global transportation, data collection, model development, server operation, and electronic waste.
- **Interpretation:** The object’s compact and polished appearance hides a network many orders of magnitude larger than the action it performs for the user.

## Data Centers as the Material Infrastructure of AI

Data centers were presented as the most visible physical manifestations of contemporary AI. They predate generative AI and previously supported cloud computing, platform companies, and cryptocurrency. Before the release of ChatGPT, however, their scale was smaller and their effects were more successfully invisibilized. The rapid expansion of AI changed both their scale and public awareness.

The prevailing model of AI development depends on increasing amounts of data and computation for pre-training, post-training, and inference. Every user query activates this infrastructure. This has produced demand for more and larger data centers, especially in the United States.

The lecture described data-center development through the classic factors of production:

- Land is required for buildings and large campuses.
- Material resources are required for servers, chips, batteries, and electrical infrastructure.
- Labor is required to construct and operate facilities.
- Energy is required to power computation and cooling.
- Water may be required both for cooling and for energy generation.

### Land and Real Estate

Data centers occupy large physical spaces. The instructor discussed Meta’s proposed campus in Louisiana, noting that common visualizations could misleadingly suggest one very tall building when the project was instead a vast cluster of facilities. Online mapping tools that overlaid the site on San Francisco or Golden Gate Park helped communicate its geographic scale.

Digital Realty, founded in the 1990s to support web infrastructure, illustrated the industry’s real-estate model. It develops facilities and leases them to major cloud providers such as Google, Amazon, and Microsoft, or offers colocation centers in which firms rent rack space for their own servers. The lecture quoted a former executive’s description of the business as leasing access to power. Data-center development is therefore both a computing business and a business of land, buildings, infrastructure, and electricity access.

Northern Virginia, near Washington, DC, was identified as the largest United States cluster and an early center of this development.

### Electricity and Emissions

The lecture cautioned that reliable estimates of electricity consumption are difficult to obtain, but all cited studies showed rapid growth. An updated Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory study was described as estimating 192 terawatt-hours of United States data-center electricity use, approximately 4.7 percent of national electricity consumption, compared with 176 terawatt-hours a year earlier. Other estimates cited figures of 312 terawatt-hours for 2025 and 415 terawatt-hours for 2024. These notes did not reconcile these differing figures.

The lecture identified several downstream effects:

- Residential consumers may pay higher electricity rates as local demand grows.
- Communities may be asked to subsidize modernization of electrical grids built to accommodate data centers.
- Data-center companies may receive reduced electricity rates, tax incentives, or other subsidies as municipalities compete for investment.
- Electricity demand can increase carbon emissions.
- Where the grid cannot meet demand, companies may use natural-gas turbines.

Microsoft was used as an example of a firm whose earlier commitments to reduce emissions became harder to maintain amid AI expansion. The lecture also discussed xAI’s Colossus facility in Memphis, Tennessee, alleging that gas turbines had been used in ways that evaded local emissions regulation and that the number of turbines had been misrepresented.

### Water and Local Context

Data-center cooling creates significant water demands, although closed-loop systems can reduce direct use. The lecture argued that aggregate comparisons, such as contrasting all data-center consumption with golf-course water use, can obscure the local meaning of water demand.

Water is geographically constrained and cannot be transported everywhere without cost. The place, source, and timing of consumption therefore matter. The instructor cited a suggestion that two-thirds of data centers developed between 2022 and 2025 were in regions experiencing water stress. Estimates focused only on cooling may also omit the water used to generate electricity.

The instructor stated that direct water cooling may be preferred over air conditioning when companies can obtain inexpensive water because air-based cooling is highly energy-intensive. The relevant environmental question is therefore not simply whether water or electricity is used, but how resource choices shift burdens between connected systems.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

- **Claim:** AI depends on data centers whose demands for land, electricity, water, infrastructure, materials, and labor create substantial local and planetary consequences.
- **Evidence:** The lecture cited construction spending, the scale of proposed campuses, several estimates of electricity consumption, grid modernization, natural-gas turbines, water-stressed locations, and government incentives.
- **Interpretation:** Data centers make the materiality of AI difficult to deny, but aggregate measurements alone remain insufficient because consequences depend on particular grids, water systems, regulatory arrangements, and communities.

## Local Politics, Distrust, and AI Populism

The lecture examined data-center politics “from below,” beginning with the experiences of affected communities rather than relying only on industry-wide comparisons. In Silicon Valley discourse, local complaints are sometimes met with condescension and aggregate studies suggesting that data centers have not significantly raised electricity prices or that their water use is small compared with other activities. The instructor argued that attending to local specificity produces a different picture.

Many proposed data centers are located in rural communities or former industrial regions. These places may have histories in which large industries promised employment and prosperity, later closed, and left communities worse off. New data-center proposals are interpreted through those histories of industrial betrayal and distrust.

Data centers promise substantial construction work, particularly for building trades, and local governments seek investment and tax revenue. At the same time, companies may demand nondisclosure agreements, negotiate privately, receive tax and electricity incentives, and move environmental costs away from their primary user bases.

The lecture drew on Jasmine Sun’s account of “AI populism.” Local activists may interpret data-center politics as a familiar story in which billionaires and corporations purchase influence over politicians and impose costs on ordinary residents. Sun was presented as reporting this perception rather than necessarily endorsing it. The political resistance arises from an experience of AI as serving powerful interests.

The instructor connected these local conflicts to the broader structure of the economy. Unprecedented public and private investment in AI creates institutional pressure to continue building rapidly. Venture capital is not the only money involved; pension funds and retirement investments may also depend on the sector’s success. The resulting commitment can feel beyond any individual actor’s control, especially when the principle of moving quickly suggests that slowing down risks losing a competitive race.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

- **Claim:** Opposition to data centers is shaped by local histories, bargaining arrangements, and distrust, not only by abstract disagreement over technological progress.
- **Evidence:** The lecture pointed to rural and deindustrialized locations, promises of jobs, subsidies, nondisclosure agreements, rising utility costs, environmental burdens, and perceptions of political capture.
- **Interpretation:** Data-center conflict becomes a form of AI populism when communities interpret development as an alliance between government and concentrated corporate wealth against local interests.

## Environmentalism, Consumption, and Ethics

The lecture framed its cases as preparation for a broader ethical discussion. Computing’s environmental consequences require attention to industrial production and consumption together. A user’s request to an AI system, the purchase of a smart device, and the construction of a data center belong to the same material networks even when they occur in different places and at different scales.

The historical and contemporary cases shared recurring patterns:

- Clean technological products depend on resource-intensive production.
- Environmental harms can be displaced from beneficiaries to workers and communities.
- The people most exposed to risks often receive fewer economic benefits.
- Infrastructure and labor can be hidden behind language emphasizing information, clouds, convenience, and innovation.
- Local land, water, and political histories shape how a supposedly global technology is experienced.
- Technological expansion is driven not only by technical capacity but also by government planning, corporate competition, finance, real estate, labor markets, and narratives about economic necessity.

The lecture did not offer a resolved ethical framework for balancing these demands. Instead, it positioned the material history of computing as necessary context for subsequent discussion of ethics.

## Human Contexts and Ethics Toolkit

### Power

The cases show technological power operating together with political and economic power. Palo Alto, Stanford, San Jose officials, and growth advocates helped determine how land would be annexed and developed for the technology industry. These decisions displaced immigrant and low-income communities while making new industrial growth possible.

Semiconductor companies exercised power over workers and neighboring communities by controlling production processes while transferring pollution and health risks beyond the firms’ boundaries. Weak regulation and limited workplace research reduced the capacity of workers and residents to prevent exposure. Grassroots organizing around the Fairchild leak altered that balance by making contamination publicly visible and using local, state, and federal institutions to pursue remediation.

Contemporary data-center companies exercise power through their ability to select locations, negotiate incentives, require nondisclosure agreements, obtain electricity concessions, and induce governments or utility customers to support new infrastructure. Communities may formally participate in local politics but possess less bargaining power than firms able to move investment elsewhere. The lecture’s discussion of AI populism reflects a perception that corporate wealth can shape political decisions and impose environmental costs on people who receive fewer benefits.

### Sociotechnical Systems

The lecture consistently treated computing as sociotechnical rather than isolating hardware or algorithms from social arrangements. Semiconductor contamination resulted from the interaction of fabrication requirements, petrochemical solvents, military specifications, production speed, storage practices, agricultural wells, land-use decisions, weak regulation, workplace organization, and residential geography. No single “technical” component accounts for the outcome.

The Amazon Echo likewise combines a physical device, minerals, batteries, factories, transportation, labor, user data, AI models, servers, energy systems, warranty rules, and waste disposal. The apparent technical interaction of asking a question depends on the entire network.

Data centers connect models and servers to real estate, electrical grids, water systems, construction labor, tax policy, finance, corporate competition, and local political history. Choices between water cooling and air cooling can shift burdens between water and electricity systems. The lecture therefore presented environmental consequences as properties of interconnected systems, not merely of individual machines or user choices.

### Identity/Positionality

Identity and positionality shaped who performed hazardous labor and who experienced pollution. The lecture identified women and immigrants as much of Silicon Valley’s semiconductor manufacturing workforce. Their occupational roles and nearby residences exposed them to risks that were less visible to engineers, executives, consumers, and wealthier residents.

Class and immigration status also mattered geographically. Superfund sites were concentrated in poorer and more immigrant communities, while those benefiting most from Silicon Valley’s economic growth were often more insulated from environmental harm. Gloria Ross’s position as a mother of a child born with heart defects informed her activism, connecting household experience to public claims about industrial contamination.

In global supply chains, workers in the Global South were described as receiving lower wages while facing greater risks in mining, refining, and manufacturing. The Congo cobalt case connected dangerous labor, child labor, displacement, and conflict to consumer technologies used elsewhere. Rural and deindustrialized communities proposed as data-center sites also encounter AI from a different position than distant users: they experience its land, utility, employment, and pollution consequences directly.

### Narratives

The lecture centered on competing narratives about computing. One narrative presents Silicon Valley as natural, clean, quiet, post-industrial, and devoted to information rather than material production. Stanford Research Park’s landscape imagery, the absence of smokestacks, clean-room imagery, the language of “the cloud,” and polished consumer products all reinforce this account.

The historical evidence supplied a counter-narrative in which computing depends on annexed land, displaced communities, hazardous production, contaminated groundwater, gendered and immigrant labor, and long-term cleanup. The clean room exemplifies the difference: the production environment is clean for the chip, but the production system is not necessarily clean for workers or surrounding communities.

“Anatomy of an AI System” offers another counter-narrative by placing geological time, extraction, labor, data, and waste around a moment of consumer convenience. Contemporary data centers further weaken the immaterial narrative because their campuses, power demand, water use, and grid effects are increasingly visible.

AI populism supplies a political narrative in which powerful corporations and wealthy investors influence governments and transfer costs to ordinary communities. The lecture did not endorse that narrative as a complete account, but treated it as important for understanding why data-center development is interpreted through distrust, previous industrial betrayal, and perceived oligarchic power.
