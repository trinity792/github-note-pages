# L19 Data Futures

## Executive Summary

The lecture developed two connected tools for studying data futures: narrative and futuring. Narratives organize selected events into meaningful accounts of who people are, how the world works, what should be done, and which futures appear possible, desirable, or inevitable. Futuring projects people from the present toward an uncertain horizon through more than prediction, including speculative fiction, scenario planning, design exercises, smart-city environments, risk assessment, and predictive systems.

Cases involving the sewing machine, electronic health records, surveillance, social media, smart cities, *The Matrix*, *Black Mirror*, *1984*, and AI creation imagery showed how technological narratives can attract support, direct attention, naturalize assumptions, and obscure the social relations in which technologies operate. The lecture concluded that narratives of the future come from particular historical contexts and positionalities, so critically examining them can reveal excluded perspectives and open alternatives.

---

## Narratives as Stories in Time

The lecture defined a narrative as a story in time that provides meaning and explanation. Narratives address descriptive questions such as who “we” are, what the world is like, and how things work. They also address directive questions about what must be done and which outcomes are possible, desirable, natural, or inevitable.

A narrative is not simply everything that happens in temporal order. It selects events from the larger flow of experience and gives those events meaning. This resembles the lecture’s earlier account of data: just as arbitrary observations do not become data without representing something, an arbitrary sequence does not become a narrative without meaning.

Narratives may concern historical or fictional time, but the lecture argued that both forms use time and meaning to structure experience. Narratives can become especially powerful when their selectivity disappears from view and they come to seem natural or given.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

- **Claim:** Narratives are pervasive and do work in the world.
- **Evidence:** The lecture described family and religious stories that transmit identities, moral lessons, and values. It also examined technological narratives that organize expectations about what a new technology will accomplish.
- **Interpretation:** Because narratives help people form coherent accounts of identity, causation, continuity, and change, they can shape present actions even when they describe imagined futures.

The lecture explicitly rejected two possible conclusions: that narratives are inherently untrustworthy and that narrative is an essentially feminine way of knowing. Narratives are often socially coded as feminine, but the lecture attributed that coding to social relations rather than to anything intrinsic to narrative. Since there is no simple escape from narratives, the proposed response was careful critical engagement.

### Narrative, Time, and Human Experience

Narrative addresses duration by creating continuity amid change. It helps turn a flow of effects and events into a coherent life story rather than a random collection of experiences. This relationship to time supports personal identity because identity depends partly on continuity across time.

The lecture contrasted narrative with data while presenting both as ways of working with reality. Data does not address time unless time is made into a variable. A sequence of points such as \(T=1\), \(T=2\), and \(T=3\) does not capture the lecture’s account of lived time, in which time can feel fast or slow, memories of the past remain active in the present, and the path already taken changes which futures are available. Narrative can synthesize these temporal relationships and pursue meaning in ways that a sequence of data points does not accomplish on its own.

## Technological Narratives and Social Change

Narratives commonly accompany new technologies. They often promise that introducing a technology will improve individual lives, create better social relations, or harmonize society. The lecture stressed that such outcomes do not follow automatically from a technical object.

### The Sewing Machine

The sewing machine served as the historical example. Narratives surrounding its introduction presented it as a household appliance that would free women from the drudgery of hand sewing and create time for other pursuits. Another prediction claimed that it would eventually eliminate “ragged and unclad humanity” by making enough clothing available to everyone.

The lecture interpreted these accounts as narratives of social uplift, equal access, and liberation from labor required for basic existence. The promised outcomes, however, did not occur simply because the machine increased the speed and efficiency of sewing.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

- **Claim:** A technology cannot be separated from the social relations and systems in which it is embedded.
- **Evidence:** The sewing machine’s promised liberation depended on questions about factory organization, ownership, control, and who benefited from industrial clothing production.
- **Interpretation:** These surrounding relationships affected actual outcomes more than a narrative in which the technology independently transformed society.

Drawing on a chapter from Ursula Franklin’s *The Real World of Technology*, the lecture said that those who create and disseminate technologies want others to accept them. Consequently, technological narratives often emphasize liberating or democratic possibilities. Feminist histories of gendered household technologies, including sewing machines, washing machines, and cake mixes, were presented as showing that the social relations surrounding a technology rarely change exactly as its creators promised.

This pattern means that narratives are neither natural nor neutral. They can shape public perceptions and orient people toward outcomes desired by their creators while avoiding the complicated contexts into which technologies must enter.

### Contemporary Datafied Examples

The instructor synthesized several examples of similar narratives in the datafied world:

- Electronic health records promise pervasive availability of personal medical information and simpler, better care. The instructor contrasted this promise with a lengthy vaccine visit during which basic information about the instructor’s child still had to be communicated.
- Mass surveillance can be narrated as a way to prevent crime. A counter-narrative emphasizes sacrifices of liberty and the possibility of abuse.
- Social media data collection is justified through promises of personalized feeds, recommendations, and advertising. The same arrangement can be narrated as imposing a cost that users may reject.

These paired accounts show that narratives give technologies causal and moral significance. Competing narratives also matter because a dominant story can constrain perceived agency, while recognizing alternatives can reopen possibilities for judgment and action.

## Performativity and the Alignment of Action

Technological narratives do not merely describe where the world may be going. They can align people around a particular future and help bring it into being. The lecture connected this capacity to performativity.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

- **Claim:** Narratives about technology have a performative dimension.
- **Evidence:** Their promises attract support, organize expectations, and encourage people to act as though a particular future is desirable or approaching.
- **Interpretation:** A narrative can therefore function as a steering force in the present rather than as a detached description of the future.

The lecture did not treat narrative power as unlimited. People retain freedom to respond, social relations can resist alignment, and the material world may not conform to the narrative. Performativity names the work of attempting to produce alignment, not a guarantee that the promised future will occur.

## Futuring Beyond Prediction

The lecture introduced “futuring” as the process of connecting the present to something over the horizon of time. Human beings live in the present but project themselves toward futures to which they do not yet have access.

The future is uncertain, not yet experienced, and possibly not a settled object waiting to be discovered. People nevertheless try to make it more tangible through imaginative projection. Prediction is only one limited form of this activity.

### Projection Rather Than Prediction Alone

Prediction describes what is expected at a later time. Projection is also directive: it casts people toward an imagined horizon and helps them see, feel, and act in relation to it. Futuring can therefore include speculation, scenario planning, design, risk assessment, predictive analytics, and artistic narrative.

The lecture’s visual model of futuring combined three ideas:

- A horizon represented what lies at a distance.
- A lens represented the attempt to see from the present into an unclear future.
- The lens also reflected or gathered the world around it, suggesting that an image of the future is partly a transformed image of the present.

This model supported the claim that futures disclose how narrators understand their present circumstances. Even when a story points forward, it remains an account produced in the present.

### Technological Progress and the Suppression of Endings

The lecture connected modern Western ideas of technological progress to an indefinitely extending future. It suggested that this orientation suppresses death and endings and may quietly entangle technological thought with fantasies of escaping human mortality. These notes presented this as a tension between projected technological continuation and the finite time of individual human lives.

## Modes of Data Futuring

The lecture distinguished several ways that data and technology can reorganize relationships among past, present, and future.

- Predictive systems can recycle and recombine past data to shape decisions about later outcomes.
- Design exercises and product scenarios can imagine technologies and uses that do not yet exist, helping participants explore what might be built.
- Smart environments can begin materially enacting a future by giving people an experience of how ordinary datafied life could operate.
- Preemptive technologies can reduce human agency by attempting to prevent particular futures from occurring.

In each case, data does more than reveal knowledge already present in a sequence from one time point to another. These systems direct attention, feeling, and conduct toward particular possible futures.

### Speculative Product Scenarios

Companies may conduct speculative use-case exercises to explore possible products. Such scenarios remain connected enough to ordinary life to seem plausible while making not-yet-existing arrangements imaginable. Even when these exercises are not presented as literary stories, they perform narrative work by organizing actors, events, causal relationships, and anticipated outcomes.

### Smart Cities

Smart cities offered an example of futuring through environments rather than through written stories. Instrumented, datafied urban settings allow people to experience an account of present and future daily life by moving through space.

The lecture associated “smartness” with positive affect and a utopian tendency. A utopia was described as an imagined society with highly desirable features, approaching perfection on Earth. Smart-city exhibits and systems can therefore make a preferred future feel tangible and ordinary while beginning to enact it.

### A Legal-Philosophical Scenario

The assigned reading opened with a character named Diana awakening in a technologically saturated home. Her alarm, heating, coffee, assignments, and communications were managed by an environment that knew about her, acted for her, and made decisions on the basis of available data.

The lecture said this story opened a book about law rather than a work of science fiction. Its author’s name is unclear in these notes. The scenario was presented as analytical and as related to planning practices used by governments and businesses.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

- **Claim:** Imaginative scenarios can conduct analysis by making an abstract future concrete.
- **Evidence:** Diana’s ordinary morning depicts technologies that anticipate needs and regulate conduct without always consulting their user.
- **Interpretation:** The scenario asks readers to internalize a possible datafied environment, become sensitive to its regulatory effects, and begin considering consequences for the legal system.

## Utopian and Dystopian Data Futures

Future narratives can organize positive or negative affect. Smart-city narratives often lean toward utopia, while *Black Mirror*, *The Matrix*, and *1984* provide dystopian approaches.

### The Matrix

The lecture began with *The Matrix*, described as a 1999 film about an imagined future in which people experience ordinary lives inside a computer simulation while AI overlords keep their bodies immobilized and extract their energy.

The film illustrated how fiction can change how audiences think and feel about both future and present. Its artistry does not depend on being a straightforward prediction. Instead, it invites reflection on whether people remain trapped in systems and illusions or attempt to see realities that are difficult or inconvenient.

The lecture used *The Matrix* to establish that imagined futures are necessarily fictional because the future has not happened. “Fiction” was treated as an act of making a possible future imaginable, not as evidence that the account lacks influence.

### Black Mirror

Episodes of *Black Mirror* illustrated dystopian futures close to ordinary experience. The examples included a social-credit system in which ratings from other people determine opportunities and a virtual-reality game that blurs the distinction between what is real and unreal.

The lecture argued that dystopian fiction can be effective when its world is only slightly different from the present. Familiarity helps audiences imagine themselves within the scenario and feel its implications.

### Utopia and Dystopia as Present Interventions

Both utopian and dystopian narratives work in the present. They select features of contemporary life, extend or rearrange those features, and invite emotional and practical responses. Their significance therefore lies not only in whether they accurately forecast later events, but also in how they frame present technologies, risks, and choices.

## Narrative Templates and Selective Attention

Individual narratives often draw on shared cultural templates. The lecture compared a template to a cookie cutter: a standard form is pressed onto reality and then given individual decoration. These shared patterns make stories recognizable but also direct attention toward some dangers and away from others.

### 1984 and Centralized Surveillance

George Orwell’s *1984* was presented as a major template for thinking about surveillance. The lecture described it as a novel written after the Second World War and set 35 years ahead of its author’s time. Phrases such as “Big Brother is watching you” now function as cultural resources whenever pervasive data collection generates fear.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

- **Claim:** A narrative template can reveal some aspects of technology while concealing others.
- **Evidence:** The *1984* template directs concern toward centralized, dictatorial, totalitarian state surveillance and demands for complete subservience.
- **Interpretation:** Because the novel arose from a particular postwar context, using it as the dominant template for contemporary data collection may make other forms of power harder to recognize.

The lecture advised asking where a template came from, what assumptions it carries, what it makes visible, and what it fails to capture.

### AI Creation Imagery

An image of a human hand and robotic hand reaching toward one another remixed Michelangelo’s creation scene from the Sistine Chapel. The lecture treated its meaning as ambiguous. It might suggest that humans act like God by creating AI in their own image, or that a spark of life is being passed to a technological creation that will gain independent life and knowledge.

The image was not presented as a neutral or universal depiction of humanity. Its racial coding and relationship to whiteness illustrated how a particular idea of the human can be projected into an imagined technological future while appearing unmarked.

## Positionality and Alternative Futures

Narratives come from specific contexts and points of view. The lecture argued that universalized stories of “the human” should be opened to scrutiny because they may conceal assumptions about whose experiences count as normal.

AI narratives were described as strongly gender-coded, even when a particular image appeared gender-ambiguous. The creation image also made whiteness appear universal. Once these patterns become visible, the lecture suggested that they can be recognized across recurring depictions of technological futures.

Alternative speculative and fantasy traditions were introduced as resources for imagining futures from different positionalities. These notes attributes one example to “Octavia Spencer,” though this reference may be unclear. These alternatives were described as envisioning Black technological thriving rather than reproducing erasure or conventional AI stories.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

- **Claim:** Narrating from a different positionality can produce a fundamentally different future.
- **Evidence:** Alternative speculative traditions offer different accounts of technological relationships, human identity, improvement, and flourishing.
- **Interpretation:** Identity and positionality affect what a narrator considers desirable, which people are included, and whether a particular experience is presented as universal.

The lecture recommended examining who is absent from a future narrative and seeking alternatives that do not erase differences through claims of universality.

- Examine how futuring works

- Recognize and analyze narratives
	- Look for them everywhere
	- Ask where they come from
	- Ask what work they do
	- Recognize how certain narratives are templates
- Denaturalize narratives that you’re handed
	- Look for whose narratives these are
		- How they’re located in context
		- How they universalize a particular future or vision of what’s human
	- And whose narratives they’re not

## Human Contexts and Ethics Toolkit

### Power

Technological narratives exercise power by structuring what people perceive as possible, desirable, inevitable, or necessary. Creators and disseminators of technologies can use liberating or democratic promises to generate support and align others with their preferred futures. The lecture’s examples included the sewing machine’s promised liberation, surveillance framed as crime prevention, social media personalization, and smart-city utopianism.

Power also appears in technologies that act on people. Diana’s automated home makes decisions based on data, while social-credit systems and preemptive technologies shape or restrict future opportunities. The lecture nevertheless emphasized that narrative power is not absolute: people, social relations, and material conditions can resist the attempted alignment.

### Sociotechnical Systems

The sewing-machine case directly challenged the idea that a technical object transforms society on its own. Its consequences depended on factories, ownership, control, labor relations, and the people being served. Similarly, electronic health records could not be assessed only by the existence of digitized information because the instructor’s experience also involved queues, institutional procedures, and failures to make basic information readily usable.

Smart cities, surveillance systems, social media personalization, and automated homes likewise combine data infrastructures with designers, institutions, users, policies, and social expectations. The lecture’s central sociotechnical point was that projected benefits and harms depend on these relationships rather than being intrinsic properties of the technology.

### Identity/Positionality

The lecture connected identity and positionality to both technological promises and representations of the future. Narratives about sewing machines and domestic technologies targeted gendered household labor, but the promised liberation did not automatically alter the surrounding social relations. AI images and narratives may encode gender even when they seem ambiguous.

The remixed creation image showed how whiteness could be presented as a universal image of humanity. Alternative speculative traditions centered different experiences and forms of technological thriving. These examples supported the lecture’s argument that imagined futures reflect their narrators’ locations and assumptions, including who is treated as representative and who is omitted.

### Narratives

Narratives were the lecture’s primary analytical tool. They select events, arrange them through time, and assign meaning to identities, causal relations, obligations, and possible futures. They can describe how the world supposedly works while directing audiences toward particular choices and outcomes.

The sewing machine, electronic health records, surveillance, personalization, smart cities, automated homes, and fictional dystopias each illustrated narrative work. Shared templates such as *1984* make certain risks readily visible but can crowd out other interpretations. Recognizing that narratives are selective and situated makes it possible to question their assumptions, compare counter-narratives, and recover agency by imagining alternatives.
