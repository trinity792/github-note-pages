# L8 Population and States

## Executive Summary

The lecture opened a week on how the world became datafied by looking backward to the emergence of population as an object of knowledge and government. It connected the rise of statistics - described as the science of the state - to early modern state-building, warfare, taxation, military recruitment, colonial expansion, resource management, and biopower. English political arithmetic and German Statistik supplied different traditions of population knowledge, while administrative grids and standardized categories made information comparable across territories. John Graunt's mortality tables, the 1790 United States census, and Adolphe Quetelet's social physics illustrated the movement from individual records to aggregates, distributions, and norms. Throughout this history, counting and classification did not merely describe populations: they helped produce governable realities and encoded judgments about whose lives mattered, how much they counted, and what qualified as normal.

---

## Population, the State, and the Datafied World

The lecture situated the history of populations within four previously introduced layers of the datafied world: ubiquitous data, reliable analytics, a culture of algorithms, and autonomous agents. This week of the course focuses on the historical development of ubiquitous data and its branching into reliable analytics. Rather than presenting this development as purely technical or deterministic, the lecture offered a sociotechnical account in which social institutions and statistical techniques shaped one another.

The lecture defined a modern state through several connected features:

- A state has a geographical territory.
- It has a permanent population.
- Its territory and population are subject to the sovereign control of a government.
- It has the capacity to relate laterally to other states, although the lecture concentrated on the vertical relationship between sovereign authority and population.

Thomas Hobbes's *Leviathan* supplied the lecture's visual model of European sovereignty. The sovereign appears above the territory, while his body is composed of the people. From the sovereign's position, government involves looking down on the territory and population, producing knowledge about them, and putting them in order.

The population was described as the collective body of people residing within a state's borders, governed and administered by the state, and nominally protected or supported by it. The lecture emphasized the distinction between the state's structure of power and the population within which social life unfolds.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The central claim was that populations are not simply natural objects waiting to be discovered. States help bring them into being as objects that can be known, administered, cultivated, and managed.

The historical rise of the word "population" was presented as evidence that the concept should not be taken for granted. An Ngram shown in class indicated an initial rise between 1700 and 1800, an early nineteenth-century peak, another rise beginning around 1900, and a twentieth-century peak.

The lecture interpreted the modern state, population, and statistics as mutually productive. States developed administrative ways to know people, these practices constituted people as populations, and statistics emerged as a discipline organized around that state-produced knowledge.

## Knowledge, State Capacity, and Biopower

The lecture summarized the state's motivation for collecting population data with the phrase "knowledge is power." Between roughly the fifteenth and eighteenth centuries, European states emerged from prolonged warfare, developed ideas of sovereignty over bounded territories, expanded colonial empires, and adopted increasingly systematic forms of accounting and administration. In this setting, the state, science, and capitalism were described as making and remaking one another.

Rulers sought knowledge about resources because effective management could increase state power. Forestry practices, for example, counted timber that could support maritime fleets. Population data served comparable purposes:

- Taxes required knowledge of how many people and how much taxable production existed.
- Armies required knowledge of how many young men could be conscripted.
- Assessing the health and growth of a country required information about births, deaths, war, and peace.
- Colonial power required information about overseas land, natural resources, Indigenous peoples, and enslaved people.

The lecture connected these practices to Michel Foucault's concepts of biopower or biopolitics. In this form of state practice, power and legitimacy depended on managing the positive conditions of a population's life and flourishing. The population became a resource to cultivate, rather than solely a collection of subjects over whom rulers exercised violence, punishment, or death.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The lecture did not claim that earlier empires or non-European states lacked population counts, or that states alone kept records. Rulers had long counted people for taxation, while merchants and insurers also maintained records.

The claimed historical change was an intensification and sharpening of population measurement: standards were extended across territories, more phenomena were counted, and counting became an organized body of knowledge concerned with reliability. The interpretation was that these changes transformed enumeration into a science of population and state administration.

### Example or Case

Forestry illustrated how knowing a resource and managing it were connected. Counting timber served state wealth and military projection, while standardized forest management reshaped forests to make them more orderly and administratively useful. The lecture later used this example as a model for understanding how populations could also be classified and cultivated as resources.

## Statistics as the Science of the State

The lecture described the name "statistics" as meaning the science of the state. Modern mathematical statistics was presented as emerging from administrative practices through which states collected and analyzed information about populations.

Two early modern traditions contributed to this development:

- English political arithmetic emphasized quantitative counting, tabulation, and visualization.
- German Statistik emphasized qualitative and descriptive knowledge about territories, populations, and resources.

Both traditions produced knowledge by and for states. Their different practices contributed to a way of seeing from the state's elevated perspective, treating society and territory as wholes that could be described and administered.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The lecture distinguished the later image of a statistician as a mathematical data analyst from an older meaning: a person who collected and managed statistics as information about the state.

The evidence for this genealogy came from the two traditions' subjects and institutional purposes. English political arithmetic counted matters relevant to decision-making and state power, while German Statistik advised rulers about competing territories and their resources.

The lecture interpreted statistics, population, and the modern state as developing together. Administrative data collection created the conditions from which later mathematical statistics became possible.

## English Political Arithmetic and John Graunt's Mortality Tables

Political arithmetic was the specifically English tradition of quantitative population knowledge. It involved counting, tabulation, and visualization by practical actors extending existing recordkeeping practices. William Petty's *Political Arithmetick* was presented as an important statement of this approach. Its subjects included land, people, buildings, manufacturing, commerce, fisheries, artisans, sailors, soldiers, public revenue, interest, and taxes. The lecture also linked Petty's importance to the histories of capitalism and empire, although it did not develop those connections in detail.

John Graunt's 1662 *Natural and Political Observations Made upon the Bills of Mortality* supplied the lecture's principal example of vital statistics. London parishes had recorded christenings as a proxy for births, burials as a proxy for deaths, and reported causes of death. Graunt compiled mortality bills from across London and used accounting methods to search for patterns.

Graunt's purposes included estimating London's population, its age and gender ratios, the relationship between births and deaths, migration from the countryside, and the behavior of diseases. His work transformed locally generated church records into population-level information.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

Each parish record referred to a unique birth or death, but political arithmetic moved above the individual record to an abstract aggregate. It counted how many people appeared in categories rather than preserving who they were or how they were socially related.

Graunt's large table organized years into columns and causes of death into rows. Even without reading every number, a viewer could identify visually dense and sparse areas and scan for patterns in causes such as consumption, cough, flux, smallpox, and plague.

The lecture interpreted the table as a new visual form of population thinking. It presented the population as a coherent whole with a substance that could be perceived through aggregated records.

### Example or Case

The mortality table also exposed the limits of early data collection. Its cause-of-death categories were irregular, overlapping, and not standardized. These notes lists examples including "abortive," "stillborn," "aged," "apoplexy," "bloody flux," cancer, measles, murder, and other partially unclear categories. Some years were missing, including a long gap within the displayed series.

Because no centralized authority imposed a common questionnaire or classification system, parish observers reported what they saw using inconsistent categories. The table could still support pattern recognition, but the lecture used it to explain why state standardization later became important for producing dependable data across contexts.

## German Statistik and the State's Administrative Grid

In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, the territory later associated with Germany consisted of many competing German-speaking states. Scholars and princely advisers called cameralists developed expertise intended to help rulers manage territories and resources after warfare and depopulation.

By the mid-eighteenth century, Statistik named a state-serving body of knowledge that was primarily qualitative and descriptive rather than mathematical. It gathered observations and narratives about populations and other resources relevant to state power and to management practices such as forestry.

The lecture connected these traditions to James Scott's idea of the state's administrative grid. Like geometrical plans imposed on cities, an administrative grid fits complex realities into standardized boxes useful to central authorities. Standard weights and measures were another example of consolidated administrative infrastructure.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

For numbers to travel across contexts and remain useful to authorities, they must be reliable and comparable. The lecture argued that centralized procedures were needed to ensure that people throughout a territory counted the same things in the same ways.

This required abstraction and generalization. Administrators stripped away local particulars so that diverse people and circumstances could be fitted into common categories. Individuals became administratively equivalent units that could be counted and combined into larger aggregates.

The lecture interpreted this simplification as a service to state management and power, not merely as a scientific search for accuracy. It also associated standardization with distrust of local practices considered too variable or complex for central administration.

### Example or Case

Forestry again illustrated this administrative logic. Knowledge practices designed to estimate available lumber also changed forests, making them more regimented and manageable. Scott's forest therefore functioned as a metaphor for society: the grid used to observe a complex reality could also reconstruct that reality in its own image.

## Performativity: Measurement Makes Governable Realities

The lecture returned to the course concept of performativity. Performativity describes how practices that claim to represent the world can shape or bring into being the phenomena they describe.

When a state establishes categories, defines collection procedures, and trains people to use them, it does more than record an independent reality. It encourages social reality to conform to an administrative grid. The state's categories become part of the practices through which people and things are recognized, managed, and reproduced.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The claim was that state measurement is performative: instructions to "count this way" transform the reality they purport to observe.

The evidence came from standardized administrative practices and the forestry example. Categories made information more legible to central authority, while resource-management procedures altered forests to fit those categories and administrative needs.

The lecture preserved Scott's qualification that reality never conforms perfectly to the grid. Administrative categories simplify and restructure the world, but the complexity of reality continues to exceed them.

## The Modern Census and Political Representation

The lecture presented the modern census as a late eighteenth-century development based on the ambition to obtain specific, reliable knowledge about every individual and collect those individuals into a larger population. Earlier enumerations existed, but the modern relationship among individual records, standardized categories, and an aggregate population was described as new.

The 1790 United States census served as the main case. Its constitutional purpose was to determine representation in the House of Representatives and to apportion financial obligations. The lecture connected the rise of censuses in liberal and relatively democratic societies to the use of population counts for political representation.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The census categories showed what mattered to the state and exposed the limits of the democracy that used them. The displayed categories included:

- Free white males aged sixteen and older.
- Free white males under sixteen.
- Free white females without an age division.
- Other free persons.
- Enslaved people.

The lecture explained that the first age distinction identified men old enough for military conscription. It connected the count of enslaved people to the three-fifths compromise, under which enslaved people were counted as three-fifths of a person for representation. Indigenous people were described as absent from the displayed count.

The interpretation was that data made political allocation possible while embodying decisions about whose lives mattered, how much they counted, who belonged to the body politic, and who remained unseen. Counting was therefore not detached from power: population data were collected for purposes that shaped their categories and effects.

### Example or Case

The lecture used the three-fifths calculation to show how transforming people into census numbers enabled their administrative devaluation. The census did not merely describe an existing political community. Its classifications and calculations participated in producing representation and defining differentiated membership in that community.

## Administrative Statistics, Society, and Social Order

Population measurement intensified during the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries as states and scholars confronted the instability of the age of European revolutions. Administrative statistics were published on an unprecedented scale, producing what one scholar called an "avalanche of printed numbers."

A table comparing the birth rates of boys and girls in different European countries illustrated the scale of this collection. Questions about why one country might have more boys per one hundred girls than the European average depended on extensive records of births and populations.

These statistics were still primarily descriptive tables rather than mathematical statistics. Nevertheless, they supplied the empirical foundation for a new way of seeing regularities in populations.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The lecture connected this expansion of administrative data to the emergence of "society" as a structured social whole encompassing the population and the relations, rules, and dynamics governing it. An Ngram comparison showed a sharp rise in the word "society" around 1800 alongside the already emerging concept of population.

The interpretation was that people facing revolutionary social transformation sought an underlying order beneath visible instability. Population statistics made it possible to imagine society as a substantial object with regular patterns that could be studied and potentially used to stabilize government.

## Adolphe Quetelet, Social Physics, and the Average Man

Adolphe Quetelet, introduced as a Belgian astronomer and mathematically trained scholar, studied administrative statistics for patterns and regularities. He noted, for example, that births and deaths could follow seasonal patterns and that murder rates could remain relatively consistent from year to year.

This created a puzzle: individuals appeared to exercise free will and experience highly varied circumstances, yet population-level behavior displayed regularities. Quetelet called the study of these patterns "social physics," borrowing the term from another scholar named Auguste [unclear]. The aim was to identify laws that abstracted from individuals and described regularities at the population level.

The lecture explained that this perspective could relocate responsibility. Crime, in Quetelet's account as presented, was not merely an individual phenomenon but also a function of society.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

Quetelet moved beyond tabulating totals by arranging human characteristics along a scale and counting how many cases appeared at each point. A graph of the heights of French military recruits illustrated this approach.

His astronomical background supplied a model in the Gaussian curve of errors. He connected the distribution of observations in astronomy to the distribution of human characteristics, contributing to the application of what became the normal distribution to administrative statistics.

The lecture interpreted the graphical distribution as a nineteenth-century invention and a new way of seeing. Rather than treating a population only as a total aggregate, it represented differences across the population and searched for underlying laws.

### Example or Case

Quetelet's "average man" represented the population as a whole. According to the lecture, he treated this figure not simply as a statistical abstraction but as something real, with other individuals understood as deviations from it.

This move joined statistical and moral meanings. The average became normal not only in a descriptive sense but also as an ideal against which differences at the ends of a distribution could appear deficient or wrong. The lecture connected this moralization of measurement to Quetelet's creation of the body mass index, presented as an example of assigning population measures to individuals and linking measures to assumptions about food, variety, and morality.

The lecture described this work as foundational to nineteenth-century mathematical statistics. It marked a transition from aggregating individuals into population totals to analyzing populations as distributions and seeking laws of human difference.

## Human Contexts and Ethics Toolkit

### Power

The lecture directly connected scientific and technological power to political power. States collected information about taxes, conscription, mortality, resources, colonies, and political representation because knowledge increased their capacity to administer populations and project power. The sovereign perspective in *Leviathan* expressed this asymmetry: government looked down on people and territory, defined what should be known, and used that knowledge to put them in order.

The census showed how measurement could distribute political power unevenly. Its categories determined representation while differentiating free white males, free white females, other free persons, enslaved people, and people omitted from the count. The lecture's interpretation of the three-fifths compromise demonstrated that technical operations on data could enact political devaluation.

The administrative grid further located power in control over categories and procedures. Central authorities determined what counted, trained others to classify accordingly, and displaced more varied local practices. Power therefore operated not only through overt coercion but also through infrastructures of observation, standardization, and comparison.

### Sociotechnical Systems

The lecture explicitly framed the rise of data collection as a sociotechnical history rather than a purely technical sequence. Statistical techniques developed together with state institutions, warfare, taxation, military recruitment, colonialism, capitalism, churches, administrative bureaucracies, and changing ideas about society.

Graunt's mortality tables illustrate this interdependence. Parish routines generated records of christenings, burials, and causes of death; Graunt compiled and tabulated those records; visual tables enabled population-level pattern recognition; and the lack of a central classification system limited comparability. The resulting knowledge cannot be separated into a purely technical table and an independent social setting because institutions, purposes, observers, categories, and representations jointly produced the data.

Quetelet's distributions likewise combined administrative records, astronomical mathematics, graphical representation, theories of society, and moral ideas about normality. The lecture treated mathematical statistics as emerging from this network of practices rather than as an isolated technical invention.

### Identity/Positionality

The census case showed that state data systems perceived and treated people according to socially conditioned positions including race, gender, age, freedom, enslavement, and Indigenous status. Free white men were divided by military age, while white women were counted without that distinction. Enslaved people were counted through a reduced numerical value for representation, and Indigenous people were described as absent from the displayed categories.

These classifications affected more than how individuals saw themselves. They expressed how the state positioned them within military obligations, citizenship, political representation, and the body politic. The case therefore demonstrates the toolkit's distinction between identity and positionality: census categories encoded how institutions recognized and valued different people.

Quetelet's average man introduced another mechanism of positionality. Once a population mean became an image of the normal or ideal person, individuals could be positioned as deviations. The lecture stressed that this relationship attached moral significance to statistical location rather than treating variation as merely numerical.

### Narratives

The lecture challenged a narrative in which population is a natural, self-evident object and statistics is simply a neutral mathematical technique. Its historical narrative instead described populations, statistics, and modern states as mutually constitutive.

Several governing narratives organized the practices discussed:

- "Knowledge is power" made enumeration appear necessary for effective rule.
- Biopower presented the population as a resource whose life and flourishing should be cultivated.
- Administrative standardization presented centralized categories as necessary for reliable knowledge across a territory.
- Social physics presented apparent individual disorder as compatible with population-level laws.
- The average man transformed a mathematical center into a narrative of what was normal, ideal, and potentially morally desirable.

The forestry and census cases also showed how narratives become performative. A state narrative about what a manageable forest or countable population should look like informed categories and procedures that then reshaped the reality being described. The lecture retained the qualification that reality always exceeded these simplifying narratives and grids.

## Key Terms

**State** — Defined by four features: a geographical territory, a permanent population, sovereign government control over both, and the capacity to relate to other states. The lecture used Hobbes's *Leviathan* as the visual model: the sovereign sits above the territory, body composed of the people below, and governing means looking down, producing knowledge, and putting things in order.

**Population** — The collective body of people residing within a state's borders, governed/administered by it, and nominally protected by it. The lecture's central claim: population isn't a natural, pre-existing object — states *produce* it as something knowable and governable through administrative practices (censuses, records, categories).

**Biopower** (Foucault) — A mode of state power focused on managing the *positive* conditions of a population's life and flourishing (health, births, growth), rather than power that operates only through violence or punishment. The population becomes a resource to cultivate — the forestry analogy (counting timber to manage it better) is the model for this.

**Political arithmetic(k)** — The English tradition of population knowledge (William Petty), emphasizing quantitative counting, tabulation, and visualization of practical matters: land, people, trade, taxes, soldiers, sailors, revenue. John Graunt's mortality tables are the flagship example.

**Statistik** — The German tradition (cameralists, mid-18th century), by contrast primarily *qualitative and descriptive* rather than mathematical — narrative knowledge about a territory's population and resources meant to advise rulers, especially useful when German-speaking states were competing with each other. Both traditions together are why "statistics" originally meant "the science of the state," not math.

**Normal curve** — The Gaussian/bell-shaped distribution Quetelet imported from astronomy (the "curve of errors") to describe how human characteristics (like height) are distributed across a population. It's the technical vehicle that let statisticians move from *totals* (how many people) to *distributions* (how people vary) and from there to claims about what's "normal."

## Case Studies

**John Graunt's tables (1662, *Bills of Mortality*)**
Graunt compiled London parish records of christenings (proxy for births) and burials with cause of death (proxy for deaths) into a single large table — years as columns, causes of death as rows. This is the earliest worked example of political arithmetic in the lecture:
- It moved knowledge *above* the individual record — no single parish entry mattered on its own; what mattered was the aggregate pattern (dense vs. sparse areas of the table, trends in causes like plague, smallpox, consumption).
- It let Graunt estimate London's population, age/gender ratios, migration, and disease behavior — new knowledge that didn't exist at the level of any single record.
- Its flaw is just as important as its innovation: categories were inconsistent and non-standardized ("abortive," "aged," "bloody flux," etc.), and there was no central authority forcing common definitions across parishes. This is the lecture's setup for *why* later state standardization (the "administrative grid") became necessary — Graunt showed what aggregation could do, but also its limits without centralized categories.

**Adolphe Quetelet's "Average Man"**
Quetelet (a Belgian astronomer) applied the normal curve to human populations. He noticed that population-level behaviors (birth seasonality, roughly stable murder rates) showed *regularity* even though individuals seemed to act with free will — he called studying this "social physics." Rather than just totaling people up, he plotted characteristics (e.g., heights of French military recruits) along a scale and counted frequency at each point, producing a distribution instead of a single sum.
- From that distribution, he constructed the "average man" — not just a statistical mean, but treated as something *real*, with actual individuals understood as deviations from it.
- This is the case the lecture uses to show statistics becoming **moralized**: "average" slides into "normal" in the evaluative sense, so people at the tails of the distribution could be read as deficient or deviant, not just numerically different. His invention of the BMI is the lecture's example of this move — a population-level statistical measure applied back onto individuals, carrying implicit moral judgment (about diet, body, "normal" weight).
- Historically, this is presented as the pivot point from descriptive administrative tallying (Graunt's era) toward modern mathematical statistics built on distributions and laws of variation.

**How the two cases connect:** Graunt shows the *aggregation* move (individual records → population totals) and exposes the standardization problem. Quetelet shows the next move (population totals → distributions and norms) and exposes how measurement gets moralized.
