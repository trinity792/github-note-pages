# L5 Making People Out of Data

## Executive Summary

The lecture examined how classifications and data relate to the realities they claim to represent. Its central tool was performativity: descriptions, categories, and measurements do not merely depict the world but help shape how the world appears, how institutions act, and what kinds of people can exist within a culture.

The lecture developed this argument through the history of multiple personality disorder, the shift from character to personality, apartheid racial classification, gender performativity, units of measurement, air quality indices, and GDP. It then focused on quantified selfhood, connecting contemporary digital self-tracking to Benjamin Franklin’s virtue tables, the Quantified Self movement, online self-optimization, and personality testing. These practices make people legible to themselves and others while also establishing standards by which people manage themselves or are managed by institutions.

The concluding concept of the cyborg described people as deeply intertwined with their data, technologies, norms, and categories. This raised unresolved questions about what people gain through data-driven optimization and what other, less measurable dimensions of a good life might be obscured.

## Representation, Classification, and Reality

The lecture began with a common account of knowledge: concepts and classifications are tools for mapping an underlying reality. On this account, knowledge improves as its categories more accurately correspond to naturally distinct phenomena in the world. Questions about data and representation then become questions of accuracy, bias, and whether a representation gets reality right or wrong.

The lecture argued that this account misses the capacity of classifications to change both perception and the world being classified. Categories are selective. They bring some features to the foreground while obscuring others, operating like lenses through which experience is mediated. The causal relationship therefore runs in both directions: people revise concepts in response to the world, but concepts also transform how the world appears and how people act within it.

### Character and Personality

The lecture used the history of the word “personality” to show that familiar categories of selfhood are historically variable. A Google Ngram indicated that “personality” did not become widely used until around the turn of the twentieth century, although the word existed earlier in a more marginal and different sense. The lecture described the modern concept as approximately 175 years old.

Before personality became common, people could be described in terms of “character.” Character was closely connected to ethical conduct, virtue, respectability, and conformity to social norms. The lecture also linked this usage to the idea of characters in fiction, where different figures could represent different levels of morality and respectability.

The change in vocabulary did not mean that durable behavioral patterns suddenly appeared. People still responded differently to social situations and other stimuli. The lecture’s point was that “character” and “personality” organize those patterns differently. Character frames individuality through ethics and sociability, while personality frames it as a bundle of traits that can be identified and measured without the same explicit normative foundation.

### Apartheid Racial Classification

The lecture recalled apartheid South Africa’s categories of white, Indian, colored, and Black. These classifications could appear natural because people were assigned to them from birth and often acted within the resulting social order. The lecture contrasted these rigid boxes with an underlying gradient of physical features.

This case illustrated the two-way causal relationship between classification and reality. The categories trained people to sort those they encountered into apartheid’s boxes, while the social system organized lives around those assignments. The instructor also emphasized positionality: not everyone experienced the classifications or their apparent naturalness in the same way.

## Performativity

Performativity was introduced as a tool for asking what data, words, and concepts do rather than only what they represent. Descriptions and categories shape the way reality appears by supplying the grids or filters through which people perceive it. Because these classifications are learned through culture and change over time, people do not encounter the world from an entirely unmediated position.

As people learn to perceive through these categories, the world may appear to conform to them or may be materially reorganized to fit them. Performativity therefore includes changes in perception, conduct, institutions, relationships, and material arrangements.

### Performative Language

The lecture traced the concept to examples in the philosophy of language. Saying “I do” in the appropriate wedding context does not merely describe something. When witnessed by the relevant people, the words establish a new social relationship with concrete implications.

Similarly, saying “I promise” creates a soft contract and an expectation that the speaker will act accordingly. A person can break the promise, but doing so may bring consequences. These examples show why the literal representational content of a phrase does not exhaust its social function.

### Performativity Is Not Simply Faking

The lecture distinguished its academic use of performativity from contemporary references to a “performative male.” In the latter usage, someone is accused of presenting sensitive or intellectual interests for strategic and possibly dishonest reasons. Performance here implies a false exterior covering a more authentic self.

The lecture’s account of performativity was not primarily about deception or self-conscious acting. Repeated performances can shape how people see themselves, how others see them, and what identities and norms become possible. There need not be a deeper, unaffected self beneath those performances.

## Multiple Personality Disorder and the Making of Kinds of People

The lecture’s central historical case came from Ian Hacking’s work on multiple personality disorder, or MPD. The American Psychiatric Association included MPD in the third edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders in 1980. By 1990, tens or possibly hundreds of thousands of Americans had reportedly received the diagnosis. The lecture also described an increase in the average number of diagnosed personalities, from two or three in 1970 to 70 by 1980.

This rapid increase followed a period in which the number of known cases was so small that the cases in recorded history could be counted individually. Clinics, hospital units, and entire hospitals devoted to MPD emerged in response to the apparent epidemic.

### Competing Claims

One professional faction argued that MPD was not a real disorder. The lecture reported several features cited in support of that claim: MPD did not interact meaningfully with known psychiatric drugs, did not develop in children, and was diagnosed almost exclusively in adults over 30.

Other experts within the American Psychiatric Association maintained that MPD was real and demanded immediate attention. The conflict became part of the prolonged professional controversy known as the Memory Wars. The lecture noted that the issue remains controversial among mental health professionals.

### Performative Interpretation

The performativity framework moved the case beyond a choice between saying that MPD was a naturally existing disorder whose sudden rise required explanation and saying that it was fake and the treatment response was a farce.

Once MPD became an official classification, psychologists began looking for it and reported finding cases resembling it. Professional awareness spread, clinics and diagnostic practices developed, and members of the public began looking for symptoms in themselves. Experiences such as holding conversations in one’s head could then be interpreted through the new classification, prompting some people to seek diagnosis and treatment.

Quoting Hacking, the lecture stated that MPD was not “a way to be a person” in 1955 but had become one by 1985. The classification supplied a new way to experience oneself and live in society. It did not merely name a stable type of person already waiting to be discovered; its institutional and cultural use participated in making a new kind of person possible.

## Gender, Identity, and Repeated Performance

The lecture presented gender as a classic application of performativity. Gender scholars argue that gender appears binary partly because people are trained from birth to perceive and reproduce a binary division. The lecture used the example of children receiving different toys, with boys receiving airplanes and cars and girls receiving dolls and miniature houses. These practices train children for culturally expected divisions of social roles.

Judith Butler’s account was presented as challenging the idea that an inner, fixed gender identity simply expresses itself outwardly. Instead, gender is performed through repeated acts, including clothing, tone of voice, and conduct. On this account, gender identity is an effect rather than the original cause of gender performance: performance comes first, and identity follows.

The lecture connected this argument to a broader social account of selfhood. A person raised in total isolation might not develop the same concept of being a distinct kind of self with a particular personality. Selfhood emerges through interactions with others and through demands to give an account of oneself.

This account does not reduce gender performance to dishonesty. Repeated actions shape a person “to the core,” as well as shaping how that person is interpreted by others. The lecture suggested that even a contemporary, initially self-conscious performance of masculinity could, if repeated widely and over time, alter masculine norms and become a more ordinary way of expressing masculinity.

## The Performativity of Measurement

Measurements are invented conventions, but they become materially consequential as institutions, tools, and practices are organized around them. Their effects extend beyond describing preexisting conditions.

### Inches and the Metric System

An inch is not presented as a naturally occurring phenomenon. Once inches, feet, meters, and millimeters are incorporated into architecture, engineering, and manufacturing, however, they become embedded in machines, tools, interchangeable parts, and production standards.

The lecture argued that the United States could not switch measurement systems instantly without substantial logistical disruption. Existing machines and tools are designed around specified units and intervals. Ford’s gradual transition to metric units illustrated how manufacturing standards were changed to support participation in global markets. The example showed that a descriptive convention can reorganize material infrastructure and constrain later choices.

### Air Quality Indices

The air quality index was described as an assemblage of measurements with particular historical origins rather than as a naturally occurring object. Harmful substances existed in the air before AQI measurements, but describing air through AQI and embedding that measure in policies made it a consequential institutional reality.

The lecture noted that the American AQI draws on five particular gases. A harmful sixth or seventh pollutant would not register in that index. If it remained outside the measurement, public health officials and school administrators might not incorporate it into decisions. The index therefore makes certain pollutants visible and actionable while leaving others institutionally obscure.

### GDP and “the Economy”

Gross domestic product began as a measure of economic activity but became a policy target. Governments design policies to make GDP rise, so the metric helps define what economic prosperity means and what actions appear appropriate.

The lecture further stated that “the economy,” understood as a discrete and manageable entity, is only about 80 years old and emerged from the post-World War II period. GDP helped make this entity visible to administration. The UN’s Beyond GDP initiative was presented as a response to the blind spots created when GDP becomes the dominant definition of prosperity. The example illustrated how a descriptive measure can become a goal that constrains understanding and action.

## Categories and Data as Techniques of Self-Making

People commonly describe themselves through socially available boxes such as boy or girl, introvert or extrovert, logical or creative, gamer, jock, preppy, or artsy. The lecture argued that it would be difficult to give an account of oneself without using such typified categories.

These labels function as personal data. They do not merely record who someone already is; they participate in constituting that person’s sense of self. The lecture presented self-making as a basic feature of social life and then asked what changes when selfhood is increasingly made through quantitative data collected at scale by algorithmically driven platforms.

Quantification has distinctive force because it reduces complex experiences to comparable data points. It can make a person visible as an object to themselves and others, enable tracking over time, and establish seemingly clear criteria of success. The resulting lack of ambiguity can make numbers appear objective and actionable even when a measurement, such as sleep tracking, does not correspond to a person’s own experience.

## Self-Tracking from Virtue Tables to Smartphones

Digital devices can track sleep, heart rate, calories, macronutrients, steps, running distance, productivity, and screen time. The instructor’s synthesis of the class discussion emphasized that students commonly used these measures to take action: to improve performance, regulate device use, or move a number in a desired direction. The apparent clarity and objectivity of the numbers could make them feel trustworthy enough to guide behavior.

The lecture argued that contemporary devices make tracking especially ubiquitous and sometimes track by default without a deliberate choice. However, counting oneself in order to improve oneself is not new.

### Benjamin Franklin’s Virtue Tables

Benjamin Franklin’s 1791 Book of Virtues used tables to track moral failures. Franklin recorded marks when he violated virtues such as sincerity, cleanliness, humility, frugality, and [unclear: one virtue was written as “comforts”]. His goal was to keep the table as empty as possible.

This system converted the complexity of a week into a display visible at a glance. A diary could also externalize the self and allow someone to view their life from the outside, but Franklin’s table supplied a faster and less ambiguous assessment of how morally he had lived.

The lecture described Franklin’s approach as an adaptation of self-tracking associated with the sixteenth-century physician Santorio Santorio, whose tracking focused more on biomedical information. Franklin’s innovation was to make counting explicitly serve moral improvement.

### The Quantified Self

The Quantified Self movement, created by Gary Wolf and Kevin Kelly in 2009, redirected this self-counting impulse toward biometric and productivity measures. Participants tracked sleep, calories, exercise, work, and the ability to remain focused. At the time, this was presented as a relatively novel and deliberate community practice, whereas similar tracking had become a more ordinary background feature of smartphone use by the time of the lecture.

The movement’s practices treat a person as an object that can be observed, managed, disciplined, and improved through data. Quantification makes progress easier to identify because a metric can move visibly upward or downward.

### Gamified Optimization and Bodily Modification

More recent online self-improvement cultures sometimes encourage people to imagine themselves as role-playing game characters with attributes such as books read, body-fat percentage, resting heart rate, and portfolio value. Life becomes a quest to “level up” according to explicit and measurable criteria.

The same logic appeared in online body-optimization culture, where physique and facial features are measured against desired mathematical parameters. Training, therapy, surgery, and even striking one’s jaw with a hammer were cited as practices intended to move the body toward those metrics. The example showed the performative force of data in an immediate material sense: a measurement can prompt a person to reshape or injure the body in pursuit of a quantified standard.

### From Self-Knowledge to Self-Discipline

The lecture connected these practices to the maxim “know thyself.” Recording and measuring oneself can turn the self into a manageable object. Quantification is not the only technique for doing so, but it simplifies standards of success and makes them legible both to the individual and to others.

This process can alter what counts as important to selfhood. When measurable improvement becomes central, being a good person may be redefined as becoming stronger, faster, healthier, more productive, or otherwise optimized. The issue is therefore not only whether data improves an already existing self, but how quantitative practices change the meaning of having a self.

## Personality Tests and the Quantification of Personality

The lecture connected the modern concept of personality to attempts to measure people. Contemporary examples included the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator, the Big Five, and recreational quizzes such as sorting people into Harry Potter houses. Although some quizzes are treated as leisure activities, others can influence how people understand themselves and their relationships.

The instructor synthesized one student contribution by noting that a test may become informative relationally: seeing that one’s result differs from a family member’s can supply an explanation for how the two interact. More generally, receiving a label such as “introvert” can affect later decisions and conduct. The test result may therefore participate in producing the behavior it appears merely to describe.

### The Woodworth Personal Data Sheet

Robert Sessions Woodworth’s 1918 Personal Data Sheet was presented as the first personality test. It included questions such as whether a person was troubled by dreams about work, liked outdoor life, or had ever fainted.

The test was developed to help the United States military screen recruits for their likelihood of developing shell shock, now called post-traumatic stress disorder in the lecture. Its purpose was institutional rather than recreational: identifying recruits thought more likely to be mentally resilient would guide selection and decisions about where to invest training resources.

The lecture argued that practices like Woodworth’s contributed to the broader shift from defining people through character to defining them through personality. Personality became a way of being a self partly through the statistical practice of measuring people as personalities.

## Data, Institutional Management, and Social Order

Personal data is not used only for private self-understanding. Governments, militaries, mental health professionals, universities, workplaces, and platforms use it to sort and manage people. Data can help individuals discipline themselves into the kinds of people institutions need.

The military personality test illustrated this connection directly: measurement helped determine which recruits merited investment. The lecture also recalled a cartoon that framed university datafication as producing homogenized students for the workforce and capital. Self-making was therefore described as a process of making social order, not merely an isolated personal project.

The lecture linked quantified self-optimization to Silicon Valley work culture. In that setting, an ideal person may be described as “high-agency”: someone able to work continuously, produce extensively, and mobilize others. Those who do not fit that ideal may be dismissed as non-playing characters who merely populate the environment for supposedly more active people.

This culture makes productivity and influence into the criteria of a successful life. Quantification supports that order by making people comparable, manageable, and oriented toward continual investment in themselves as human capital. The lecture did not characterize datafication as necessarily good or bad; it used these examples to show that data powerfully shapes people and the social purposes they are expected to serve.

## Cyborgs and Data-Extended Selfhood

The lecture concluded with the cyborg as a metaphor for human existence amid technology. It quoted Colin Koopman’s argument that data is not merely external material that can be detached from a true self. Data is constitutive of who people can become, making people “cyborgs extended into our data.”

The conventional image of a cyborg joins flesh to technological components that augment limbs, bones, or brains. The lecture presented a broader account associated with Donna Haraway, in which the cyborg captures the multiple and relational nature of being human. People are intertwined not only with bodily technologies but also with personal data, norms, concepts, and practices outside their bodies.

This framing produced the lecture’s concluding questions. Data might help people become stronger, faster, healthier, more productive, more introspective, or more capable of action. At the same time, relentless optimization might weaken contact with dimensions of a good life that are fuzzy, ambiguous, or resistant to measurement. The lecture left the evaluation of these forms of datafied selfhood open.

## Human Contexts and Ethics Toolkit

### Power

The lecture repeatedly connected classification and measurement to the asymmetric capacity to structure other people’s behavior. The American Psychiatric Association’s authority to add MPD to the DSM influenced what clinicians searched for, what patients recognized in themselves, and which clinics and treatment systems were built. The classification’s power operated through professional authority, institutional adoption, and self-interpretation rather than through a label alone.

The military’s use of the Woodworth Personal Data Sheet connected scientific measurement to decisions about who would be selected and who would receive training resources. AQI and GDP similarly gained power because officials used them to determine school, public-health, and economic policy. What the measures included became actionable, while what they excluded could disappear from institutional attention.

Digital self-tracking shows power operating through self-discipline. A platform or device supplies the metric, but users may alter their own conduct to improve it. Silicon Valley’s ideal of the high-agency, optimized worker likewise demonstrates how workplace values can be internalized as personal goals. In these cases, external management and self-management reinforce one another.

### Sociotechnical Systems

The lecture treated categories, technologies, people, institutions, and material infrastructures as intertwined. MPD’s history involved the DSM, psychiatric professionals, patients, public awareness, clinics, and diagnostic practices. The apparent epidemic cannot be separated into a purely medical condition on one side and a purely social response on the other within the lecture’s performative account.

Measurement standards also became sociotechnical systems. Inches were embedded in manufacturing machines, tools, parts, engineering practices, and global market decisions. AQI joined sensors and pollutant measurements to public-health categories and administrative decisions. GDP linked economic statistics to governmental definitions of prosperity and policy goals.

Self-tracking similarly depends on relationships among phones, smartwatches, apps, algorithms, users, cultural ideals, and institutional demands. The technology records data, the user interprets and acts on it, and social norms determine which metrics appear worth improving. The lecture’s cyborg metaphor summarizes this interdependence by presenting people as extended into their data and technological practices.

### Identity/Positionality

The lecture directly treated identities as socially conditioned and shaped by classification. Apartheid’s racial boxes affected how South Africans perceived and treated one another, although people in different positions did not experience the system’s meaning or naturalness in the same way. The case showed that a classification can simultaneously shape self-understanding and determine how institutions and other people treat someone.

Gender performativity supplied a second example. Gendered toys and social expectations train people into roles, while repeated clothing, speech, and conduct shape identities and how others interpret them. The lecture presented gender as relational and socially produced rather than as a purely private identity expressed from within.

MPD and personality testing showed classifications producing additional ways of being a person. A diagnostic category could reorganize how someone understood internal experiences, while a personality label such as “introvert” could influence conduct and relationships. Institutional uses of personality tests added positional consequences because classification could affect military selection and the allocation of training resources.

### Narratives

The lecture challenged the narrative that knowledge progresses simply by finding categories that mirror natural divisions more precisely. Performativity supplied an alternative account in which categories also participate in producing the realities they describe.

The MPD controversy contained competing narratives: one treated rising diagnoses as evidence of a real epidemic requiring treatment, while another treated MPD as an unreal disorder. Hacking’s performative interpretation introduced a third account in which official classification, professional attention, public awareness, and self-understanding made MPD into a newly available way to be a person.

GDP supports a narrative in which national prosperity is represented by growth in a primary economic number. Once governments organize policy around that story, increasing GDP appears to be a natural goal while other meanings of prosperity can recede. The Beyond GDP initiative was presented as challenging that narrowed account.

Quantified selfhood also relies on narratives about what a person is and what a good life requires. The role-playing-game metaphor frames life as a quest to improve statistics, while Silicon Valley’s high-agency ideal presents productivity and optimization as markers of a real or successful person. The lecture’s concluding cyborg narrative offers another account: people are not isolated selves who merely possess data but relational beings partly constituted through it.
