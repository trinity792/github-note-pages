# L10 Taking the Global View

## Executive Summary

The lecture developed the idea of a "global view" by connecting contemporary indicators to the historical rise of the administrative state, national statistical infrastructure, and post-World War II international institutions. Measures such as GDP, unemployment, inflation, inequality, and the Sustainable Development Goal indicators make large populations and abstract objects legible from above, but they depend on institutional authority, standardized categories, technical methods, and territorial control.

The lecture then examined Gapminder's visualization of fertility and child mortality. Although the visualization promises a factual view that can overcome bias and show worldwide improvement, its organization by countries, world regions, and development trajectories also carries assumptions about progress and population management. Because fertility data aggregate intimate reproductive experiences, the lecture argued that global population statistics must be examined alongside positionality, historical accounts of family planning and population control, and narratives from people whose lives become data.

## Indicators and the View from Above

National indicators offer policymakers a dashboard-like view of society. The lecture introduced this view through an image of Janet Yellen and an imagined economic control board containing levers, meters, and dials. This image represented the aspiration to stand above a complex world, monitor it through indicators, and adjust policy to improve outcomes.

Examples discussed in the lecture included:

- Gross domestic product represents aggregate economic activity.
- The Gini coefficient represents the distribution of economic outcomes rather than only their overall level.
- Unemployment measures labor-market conditions.
- The Consumer Price Index and Producer Price Index measure changes in prices.
- The Air Quality Index turns a dispersed environmental condition into a summary measure.
- The Sustainable Development Goals establish 17 goals and 210 indicators for tracking global progress.

These indicators are not simply discovered in the world. The lecture described goals, metrics, and methods as products of complicated processes involving scientists, politicians, governments, and sometimes consultation with individuals about what matters in their lives. Indicators therefore express decisions about what should count as well-being, progress, or a social problem.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

**Claim:** A view from above allows institutions to summarize and manage large populations, but it is constructed through selected measures rather than being a complete or neutral picture.

**Evidence:** National governments collect economic, demographic, labor, health, and environmental indicators, while international institutions use extensive indicator systems such as the Sustainable Development Goals.

**Interpretation:** A dashboard makes society appear measurable and adjustable. What appears on that dashboard reflects prior choices about valuable outcomes, relevant categories, and the scale at which social life should be understood.

## Statistics, State Knowledge, and the Administrative State

The lecture revisited the rise of statistics as part of state formation. Beginning in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, sovereigns increasingly treated knowledge of their populations as a source of power. Thomas Hobbes's image of the European sovereign surveying his territory served as a visual touchstone for imagining knowledge produced from the ruler's position and in the ruler's interests.

Statistics was presented as historically connected to the state. The state produced knowledge about populations while also developing new ways to govern them. Across the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, these practices contributed to the administrative state - a state that cultivates power over and through its population by collecting and using data about people and their activities.

Institutionalization makes statistical collection regular rather than exceptional. It creates agencies, infrastructures, recurring procedures, standardized forms, and coordinated systems for aggregating information. The lecture described government statisticians as central operators of this system.

### The 1940 United States Census

A census form from Los Angeles in 1940 illustrated how state interests become administrative categories. The form collected personal description, sex, race, age, education, previous residence, and employment status.

The lecture connected these categories to contemporary state concerns. Employment questions followed the mass unemployment of the Great Depression, while questions about origin and movement were connected to earlier immigration and integration concerns. The form thus provided a way to read the history of state priorities through the information the state chose to collect.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

**Claim:** State knowledge and state power develop together through standardized population data.

**Evidence:** Census forms translate people into categories that can be processed and aggregated. Specialized agencies collect demographic, labor, economic, and health data, while coordinating bodies organize these activities into a larger national statistical infrastructure.

**Interpretation:** Administrative categories do not merely describe society. By stabilizing classifications and making them usable in governance, they help reproduce a particular social order and a particular understanding of the population.

## Statistical Infrastructure and Political Contestation

The administrative state's capacity to know populations depends on durable infrastructure. Statistical agencies coordinate the collection, protection, aggregation, and publication of information across different domains. The lecture referred to the United States statistical system's stated role in operating seamlessly and safely stewarding sensitive data resources.

This infrastructure can become politically contested. The lecture pointed to disputes over whether institutions release important statistics and used public-health information concerning vaccination and measles as an example of data whose availability is connected to political power. Practices that may once have seemed routine or taken for granted can become visible sites of political struggle.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

**Claim:** Statistical infrastructure is part of government power and can become a focus of political contestation.

**Evidence:** Government institutions decide which statistics to collect, coordinate, protect, and release. The lecture identified the availability of public-health data as one contemporary area in which these decisions carry political stakes.

**Interpretation:** Statistical production cannot be separated from institutional authority. Decisions about whether data exist publicly affect what governments and members of the public can know and act upon.

## Constructing and Measuring “the Economy”

Economic indicators were used to show that measurement does not merely record an already settled object. Measures such as inflation and GDP abstract from individual activities such as buying food, exchanging goods, looking for work, and trying to meet household needs. They make it possible to view those activities collectively as "the economy."

The lecture presented changes in the use of the phrase "the economy" as evidence that this object became widely recognizable at the same time states sought to measure and manage it, particularly around the late 1930s and early 1940s as the United States prepared for World War II. Economics as a discipline preceded the widespread treatment of "the economy" as a single managed object.

A 1992 issue of the United States Department of Commerce's *Survey of Current Business* illustrated the labor and infrastructure involved in producing economic abstractions. Its subjects included corporate profits, national income and product accounts, and alternative measures of changes in real output and prices. The instructor also connected this infrastructure to her mother's work in the Bureau of Economic Analysis, including years devoted to revisions from gross national product to gross domestic product, and later work at the International Monetary Fund.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

**Claim:** Objects such as "the economy" become governable through the same processes that make them measurable.

**Evidence:** Government agencies devote sustained technical and institutional labor to defining, revising, and publishing national economic accounts and indicators.

**Interpretation:** GDP is an abstraction that enables a national overview. Its usefulness depends on infrastructure and repeated methodological work, while its revisions also indicate that such measures have limitations and are not fixed representations.

## Data Practices, Methods, and Territorial Authority

The view from above requires more than indicators. The lecture identified surveys, sampling, estimators, and other developments in quantitative social science as parts of the technical instrumentation that made large-scale measurement possible.

Underneath these practices lies territorial control. A state or comparable authority must be able to standardize methods and enforce their use across an area before the resulting measurements can be treated as comparable. The lecture therefore presented the datafying world as a sociotechnical history involving tools, methodologies, institutions, territory, and governance rather than as the natural accumulation of data.

These methods also moved beyond the state. Insurance companies were early users of population-based approaches, and political polling, survey research, and market research later adopted similar techniques. The tools retained their capacity to produce an overview across large populations even when used by nonstate actors.

## The Postwar Emergence of the Global View

The lecture distinguished the global view from a purely national one. It argued that the term "global" came into common use after World War II because it described a particular historical process of globalization. Four related developments were emphasized: the Cold War, decolonization, economic globalization and development, and international institutions.

### Cold War Ordering

The Cold War was described as a globe-spanning political order in which every part of Earth was understood as having a place within a standoff between rival blocs. The first world referred to the United States, Western Europe, and their military and economic allies. The second world referred to the communist bloc, including the Soviet Union, China, and Cuba. States aligned with neither bloc were classified as the third world.

The lecture stressed that this three-world schema was not a complete account of international dynamics. It reflected the perspective of the first- and second-world powers, which treated their own conflict as dominant and often viewed other countries as proxies. The struggle concerned political allegiance, economic dominance, and influence as well as military power.

### Decolonization

European colonial empires were dismantled during the twentieth century, especially after World War II, through both treaties and violence. New states and governing institutions pursued independence from colonial extraction and exploitation, though the lecture noted that these efforts did not always succeed.

In the Cold War context, first-world powers treated decolonizing regions, especially the area described in the lecture as the global South and South Asia, as central terrain in the conflict between economic and military blocs. This framing could obscure the agency of newly independent countries seeking to determine their own futures.

### Economic Globalization and Development

Globalization included the movement of goods, services, technology, capital, jobs, people, and cultural products across national borders. The lecture gave multinational firms based in Western Europe and the United States, job relocation to lower-wage regions, and movement toward areas with particular regulations as examples.

"Development" also changed meaning. Under colonial rule, development referred to colonial powers developing resources, agriculture, and plantations for extraction. After World War II, global institutions reframed development as helping "developing countries" move along a linear path toward economic prosperity modeled on the first world's claimed success.

### International Institutions

Institutions created after World War II attempted to stabilize and manage the new global order. The lecture named the United Nations, World Bank, International Monetary Fund, and international courts as examples. Although these institutions did not possess the same sovereignty as nation-states, they exercised substantial power by setting standards and creating administrative grids for collecting and comparing information.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

**Claim:** The contemporary global view emerged from a specific postwar order rather than from an inevitable expansion of knowledge.

**Evidence:** Cold War blocs, decolonization, cross-border economic flows, development programs, and new international organizations all created reasons and mechanisms for representing the world as one governable field.

**Interpretation:** The global view carries the priorities and power relations of the institutions and historical processes that produced it. Its classifications can privilege the perspectives of dominant actors while making other states and populations appear as objects of management.

## Global Data Institutions and Standardization

International institutions bring national data together in pursuit of global goals. The United Nations Statistics Division's phrase "Better data, better lives" was presented as a causal narrative: collecting better data is expected to produce better conditions.

The Sustainable Development Goals illustrate how global institutions define desirable outcomes and establish indicators for monitoring them. Goals concerning quality education, health and well-being, gender equality, poverty, and hunger can appear positive or uncontroversial, but the lecture emphasized that selecting goals and measures has political implications. Once institutions define indicators and track them, the world becomes visible through the things chosen as targets.

The International Monetary Fund was presented as another institution that aggregates and visualizes standardized national quantities such as GDP. Its animated displays allow viewers to track geographic and historical changes, including changes associated with COVID and variations among countries.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

**Claim:** Global data depends on extensive coordination and on the assumption that countries measure the same things in the same ways.

**Evidence:** The United Nations and International Monetary Fund compile national statistics into comparable global indicators and visualizations.

**Interpretation:** Standardization enables international governance without requiring a single world sovereign. At the same time, global institutions gain power to define what counts as improvement, identify problems, and steer action toward their specified goals.

The lecture used this point to pose questions rather than provide settled answers: Who occupies the view from above? Who is doing the governing? Whose definition of "better" determines the direction in which global data is supposed to steer the world?

## Gapminder, Factfulness, and Population Visualization

Gapminder was introduced as a platform that does not primarily collect global data but brings data together and displays it, especially for comparison across health and welfare measures. The lecture associated it with a narrative of "factfulness": factual knowledge is presented as a way to move beyond ideological commitments, fear, and historically conditioned bias. Its visualizations often support the claim that the world has improved over time.

A prominent Gapminder visualization places babies per woman on the x-axis and child mortality on the y-axis. Each circle represents a country, its size represents population, and its color represents a world region. The lecture noted that Asia and Africa were especially visually prominent in the displayed version. Animation shows how country positions change from the past through the present and into projected futures.

### Representation and Completeness

The lecture used class polling to distinguish two questions:

- Does the visualization make viewers feel capable of seeing the world from above?
- Do viewers feel personally represented in the data?

In synthesizing the responses, the instructor identified two different concerns. First, data may be incomplete or inferred, especially for historical periods or places without strong state collection capacity. Second, the world and individual experience may contain more than any data point can represent.

The instructor also discussed a contribution suggesting that people in countries with extensive, systematic data collection may feel more represented than people in places with weaker collection capacity. A question about countries that do not collaborate with international institutions, including North Korea and Turkmenistan, remained unresolved. The instructor's tentative response was that such countries were probably represented in Gapminder, but not necessarily in the same way as countries with different data systems and institutional relationships.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

**Claim:** A compelling global visualization can make worldwide comparison feel direct while concealing uncertainty, aggregation, and uneven representation.

**Evidence:** Gapminder turns national fertility, mortality, population, and regional classifications into an animated visual field. The lecture noted missing historical measurements, possible inference, unequal collection capacity, and the abstraction of national averages from personal experience.

**Interpretation:** Visual clarity is not the same as complete representation. The visualization creates a particular global perspective by selecting countries as units, arranging them along two axes, assigning regional colors, and presenting movement over time as an intelligible trajectory.

## Fertility, Child Mortality, and the Development Narrative

The Gapminder animation presents a recognizable story: over time, babies per woman decline while more children survive. Countries move toward the region of the chart occupied by Europe and North America, and Africa is implicitly expected to follow as economies develop and access to family planning, contraception, and abortion expands.

The lecture connected this trajectory to a model of economic development. Poor countries are expected to limit population growth, participate in the economy, manage themselves in approved ways, and become part of the global consumer market. Women's reproduction becomes part of this model.

Demography, described as the study of population growth, developed within this global frame. Its concern with regulating reproduction and promoting family planning was linked to postwar advocacy that moved from the global North toward the Third World, where population growth exceeded that of Europe and North America. The lecture presented these connections as the historical background that makes an apparently straightforward visualization politically and ethically significant.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

**Claim:** The axes and animation do not only display population change; they also encode a narrative about development and the expected direction of history.

**Evidence:** Countries are shown moving toward lower fertility and lower child mortality, with Europe and North America occupying the implied destination. Family planning and economic participation appear as mechanisms through which other regions are expected to follow.

**Interpretation:** The visualization treats one historical path as a general developmental trajectory. The view from above positions someone as if they could observe, manage, intervene in, and steer world populations.

## Intimate Experience Within Aggregate Population Data

The lecture shifted from national averages to the reproductive experiences underneath fertility statistics. A poll asked about experiences including having or wanting children, trying to conceive, pregnancy, contraception, abortion, family size, sterilization, and hysterectomy. The purpose was not to turn individual responses into a class position, but to demonstrate that fertility data aggregate experiences with different meanings for different people.

"Babies per woman" is therefore both a population statistic and a representation of intimate lives. Reproductive experience can involve personal decisions, social circumstances, medical interventions, and political regulation. The instructor explicitly located herself within the dataset as someone who had two children while also declining to disclose the rest of her reproductive history.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

**Claim:** Global fertility measures compress highly personal and differently situated experiences into national and regional abstractions.

**Evidence:** The lecture named conception, childbirth, contraception, abortion, sterilization, family size, and other reproductive experiences as parts of the lived background of fertility data.

**Interpretation:** A person's relationship to the visualization depends partly on identity, experience, and whether institutions have recorded that person. Positionality changes what the same aggregate statistic means and whether it feels representative.

## Population Management and Alternative Historical Narratives

The lecture argued that population and reproduction should not be treated as natural, perspective-free subjects. Viewing world population by region can encode racialized distinctions, even when represented through seemingly neutral geographic categories.

The history behind population statistics includes narratives about a global "population bomb" and concerns about supposedly unlimited population growth in the global South while fertility declined in Europe and North America. The lecture connected these concerns to eugenics after World War II and to Bangladesh's use as an experimental site for Western contraceptive interventions intended to control what was framed as an exploding South Asian population.

These histories introduce perspectives that a chart of declining fertility does not itself show. A woman in Bangladesh who objected to sterilization might appear only as a value in a national statistic. Her account could change the story from one of inevitable development to one involving intervention, refusal, coercion, and unequal authority.

The lecture therefore proposed supplementing statistical views with narratives and contextual evidence. Rather than asking only whether fertility declined, analysis should also ask how it declined, who defined high fertility as a problem, who selected the interventions, and how people living through those interventions understood them.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

**Claim:** Historical and first-person narratives can reveal power relations omitted by aggregate population statistics.

**Evidence:** The lecture contrasted the smooth developmental narrative of Gapminder with histories of population control, contraceptive experimentation, sterilization, and objections from affected women.

**Interpretation:** Knowing the history does not make current programs identical to earlier ones. It changes which questions appear relevant and which additional forms of information should be collected when evaluating population programs.

## Human Contexts and Ethics Toolkit

### Power

The lecture repeatedly connected knowledge to the capacity to govern. Early sovereigns sought population knowledge because it increased state power, while the administrative state used censuses, agencies, classifications, and standardized procedures to structure populations. International institutions exercise a related form of power by establishing indicators and standards even without the sovereignty of nation-states.

The Gapminder case extends this issue to reproductive governance. A global view can position institutions as managers of fertility, family planning, and development. The lecture's questions - who defines the problem, who decides what counts, and whose good is pursued - identify asymmetric authority over how populations are represented and which interventions appear necessary. Histories of contraceptive experimentation and sterilization make the stakes concrete by showing that scientific and technological practices can be intertwined with political power over people's bodies.

### Sociotechnical Systems

The lecture treated indicators as products of intertwined social and technical arrangements. A census requires forms, administrative categories, collection systems, processing technologies, agencies, legal or territorial authority, and people whose lives are classified. GDP similarly depends on conceptual definitions, professional labor, methodological revision, government institutions, and the activities being aggregated.

Global data systems add coordination across countries, international organizations, standards, visualizations, and development goals. Gapminder's chart is therefore not an isolated technical display. Its axes, national units, regional colors, historical estimates, institutional data sources, and development assumptions jointly produce the global view. The lecture's account makes it impossible to separate the visualization's technical design from the institutions and narratives that give its data meaning.

### Identity/Positionality

The lecture explicitly used reproductive experience to show how positionality affects encounters with data. A viewer who has had children, used contraception, experienced abortion or sterilization, or lived within a particular family structure may understand "babies per woman" differently from someone with another history. The instructor also noted her own inclusion in the dataset while maintaining boundaries around the details of her reproductive life.

Geographic and institutional position also matters. People living in a state with extensive and systematic data collection may feel more visible in a global dataset than people in rural settings or countries with limited capacity or participation. Gender, race, colonial history, region, and economic position shape both how populations are classified and how reproductive interventions are experienced. The Bangladesh example shows why the perspective of a woman subject to or refusing sterilization cannot be replaced by her country's aggregate fertility value.

### Narratives

Several narratives organize the lecture's cases. "Better data, better lives" presents improved measurement as a route to improved conditions. Gapminder's factfulness narrative presents data as a means of escaping ideology and fear, while its animation tells a story in which countries move toward lower fertility, lower child mortality, and the developmental position associated with Europe and North America.

The lecture questioned the apparent naturalness of this path. The same statistical change can be narrated as progress through family planning or as a history involving population control, experimentation, sterilization, and resistance. The Cold War's first-, second-, and third-world schema likewise supplied a story that centered rival powers and could reduce other countries to proxies. By introducing alternative accounts from affected people, the lecture showed that narratives determine who appears as an agent, what counts as a problem, and which futures seem desirable or inevitable.
