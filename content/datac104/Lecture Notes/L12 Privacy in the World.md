# L12 Privacy in the World

## Executive Summary

The lecture examined why legal and institutional privacy frameworks inherited from the 1970s struggle to address contemporary data practices. These frameworks emphasize individual control over personal information through notice, consent, security, access, and enforcement, but they place responsibility on individuals, offer largely all-or-nothing choices, and focus narrowly on personally identifiable information.

Several cases illustrated what this framework leaves out. Research using Havasupai blood samples implicated tribal sovereignty, collective identity, history, and relationships rather than consent alone. Crisis Text Line's transfer of anonymized conversation data to a for-profit spinoff raised questions about changed purposes and expectations despite formal consent and de-identification. Berkeley classroom recordings and a Canvas breach showed how sensitive information can move beyond its original context without necessarily triggering protections tied to personally identifiable information. Transportation Security Administration body scanners connected privacy to bodily dignity, compulsory participation, gender norms, and differential exposure.

The lecture introduced contextual integrity as an alternative framework centered on context-specific relationships, expectations, and information flows. It presented this framework as an advance over legal compliance alone, while leaving open whether it goes far enough. The lecture ultimately asked whether privacy protection sometimes requires collecting less data, obstructing continued datafication, or deciding that some matters should not be datafied at all.

## Privacy Law Inherited from the 1970s

The lecture began from the claim that legal concepts developed in the 1970s continue to govern contemporary data systems even though those systems have changed dramatically. It connected this problem to an earlier discussion of rent-pricing algorithms, where concerns about shifts in power were addressed through inherited concepts such as business collusion and claims about an algorithm's free speech. According to the lecture, such concepts repeatedly prove inadequate for the problems produced by a datafied world.

The same mismatch appears when data collected for one purpose is fed into machine-learning and artificial-intelligence models without anything resembling meaningful consent. The lecture cited the scraping of websites and social media simply because the material is available. It also referred to information disclosed in a lawsuit in which Microsoft executives reportedly characterized AI scraping as an extraordinarily large theft of labor, linking model-training data to the labor embodied in what enters a training set.

The dominant privacy paradigm treats privacy as individual control over personal information. Its structure was described through five fair-information principles, transcribed variably as "FIPPs" or "five FIPs," grouped by the lecture into three broad areas:

- Individual consent includes notice, awareness, choice, participation, and control.
- Security concerns the protection and management of collected information.
- Enforcement concerns whether governments can punish important violations of organizational promises.

The lecture emphasized that this template appears even where private companies are not legally required to follow it. It is attractive because it is easy to explain and does not greatly restrict what companies can do with supplied data.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

**Claim:** A privacy framework created around the concerns of the 1970s does not adequately govern contemporary systems built on extensive data collection, combination, and machine learning.

**Evidence:** The lecture pointed to web scraping, social-media scraping, AI training sets, rent-pricing algorithms, and the continued reliance on notice-and-consent procedures. These practices involve flows and uses of information that extend beyond the circumstances in which individuals originally supplied it.

**Interpretation:** Because the inherited framework asks whether an individual received notice, consented, or retained limited control, it can classify a practice as acceptable without confronting changed purposes, collective consequences, relationships, dignity, or underlying concentrations of power.

## Terms of Service and the Limits of Individual Consent

Terms of service and privacy policies were presented as the basic legal structure through which privacy is managed in the United States. A person signs up for a service, accepts its terms, and becomes legally bound in relation to the company. Government enforcement, particularly by the Federal Trade Commission in the lecture's account, is then generally directed toward violations of promises made in those documents.

The lecture described this arrangement as an exchange: users obtain access to a service while supplying information that has commercial value. On a social-media platform, this information may include:

- Preferences that can support profiling and targeted links or advertisements.
- Connections to other people and patterns shared with groups categorized as similar.
- Examples of how people write and communicate, which can become training data for machine-learning and AI systems.

Terms of service are often long and difficult to understand, which helps explain why few people read them. The lecture treated this low readership as compatible with the business model: companies can depend on most people accepting the terms while data collection continues.

The lecturer's former Twitter account illustrated the temporal reach of this agreement. Although the lecturer no longer actively used the platform and no longer agreed with aspects of the company, old posts remained public because authentication procedures had prevented their removal. The example showed how an agreement made at signup can continue to govern information into the future, including after a user's relationship with a company has changed.

### Personal Privacy Practices

A class poll asked which privacy-related actions students performed regularly or systematically. The listed practices included reading privacy policies, downloading or deleting data, adjusting cookie settings, selecting "do not sell my information," using an incognito window or a different browser, and using search engines presented as offering stronger privacy protections.

The most common reported practices included clearing data and cookies, changing settings in repeated pop-ups, selecting "do not sell my personal information," and using incognito browsing. Reading privacy policies was relatively uncommon. The lecture interpreted all of these practices as forms of discrete, individual control that fit the 1970s privacy paradigm.

The framework was said to be ineffective in several connected ways:

- It places the labor of privacy protection on each person who cares enough to take repeated action.
- It often offers an all-or-nothing choice because declining required cookies or refusing terms may mean losing access to a website or service.
- Defaults commonly steer people toward opting in.
- Companies can count on most people not reading terms of service.
- Individual choices provide little capacity to change the larger systems that collect and monetize data.
- Weak enforcement can make actions such as selecting "do not sell my information" feel symbolic.
- The framework concentrates on personal privacy even when one person's data can affect groups or other people connected to that person.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

**Claim:** Formal consent does not necessarily provide meaningful privacy control.

**Evidence:** Users must often accept terms to obtain access, privacy documents are difficult to read, defaults favor collection, and individuals must repeatedly manage settings across services. The lecturer also described being unable to remove old social-media posts despite no longer using the platform.

**Interpretation:** Consent under these conditions is embedded in unequal relationships and constrained choices. It may legally authorize collection without giving individuals practical influence over business models built around harvesting and reusing their information.

## Beyond Personally Identifiable Information

The lecture cautioned against treating privacy protection as equivalent to protecting personally identifiable information, or PII. It argued that this narrow focus misses collective effects, metadata, sensitive communications that do not meet a legal definition of PII, and the cumulative effects of combining information across systems.

Anonymization was also presented as insufficient by itself. Even if information cannot be connected back to a named person, its movement into a different purpose or relationship may violate expectations associated with the context in which it originated.

The lecture assembled several limitations of the individual-control and PII model rather than asking students to memorize a fixed list. Its central concern was that legal compliance can leave out:

- Context-specific expectations.
- Relationships and obligations.
- Collective and group interests.
- Sovereignty and self-determination.
- Bodily dignity and vulnerability.
- Differential effects on people whose identities or bodies depart from institutional norms.
- Power over whether a person can meaningfully refuse participation.
- Harms produced by combining data across otherwise separate systems.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

**Claim:** The privacy interests at stake in contemporary data practices extend beyond whether named or directly identifiable personal information is released.

**Evidence:** The subsequent cases involved anonymized crisis conversations, classroom communications, blood samples with collective significance, and body scans that were reportedly protected from storage and facial identification.

**Interpretation:** A system can satisfy formal PII protections while still changing relationships, exposing vulnerable people, undermining collective authority, or violating expectations concerning how information and bodies should be treated.

## Case: Havasupai Blood Samples

The lecture described a case involving the Havasupai, a Native American people living at the bottom of the Grand Canyon. In 1990, members of the tribe supplied blood samples to researchers at Arizona State University for research into the genetics of diabetes within the tribe. Researchers subsequently used the samples for other genetic studies, including research concerning mental illness and the tribe's geographic origins.

These additional purposes were not included in the original individual consent. The consequences went beyond that procedural failure. Researchers were banished from tribal land, litigation followed, and a settlement required the university to pay $700,000 and return the blood samples.

The lecture situated this response in a longer history of efforts to return human items, human remains, and cultural patrimony taken from Native Americans. It therefore treated the samples as material objects embedded in relationships and historical experience, not merely containers of abstract genetic information.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

**Claim:** The case cannot be fully understood as a simple failure to obtain individual consent for secondary data uses.

**Evidence:** Uses expanded from diabetes research to mental illness and geographic-origin research, the tribe excluded the researchers from its land, and the settlement included the physical return of the samples as well as payment.

**Interpretation:** The case implicated tribal sovereignty, governance, collective identity, historical relations, and self-determination. Returning property addressed one aspect of the conflict, but the lecture emphasized that the original taking and reuse were tied to a longer relationship between settler institutions and Native peoples. Because genetic information shared aspects of group identity, decisions involving one person's data could also affect others.

## Case: Crisis Text Line and Changed Purposes

Crisis Text Line was described as a prominent nonprofit mental-health support line that uses data science and other tools to assist people experiencing emotional abuse, self-harm, or thoughts of suicide. Data from text conversations with people in moments of acute distress did not remain solely within the helping relationship. A for-profit spinoff used a processed version of the information to create customer-service software marketed as using machine learning to make support human, empathetic, and scalable.

The lecture stated that hotline users consented before connecting with counselors. Crisis Text Line also maintained that shared data was completely anonymized and stripped of details that could identify the people seeking support. Both organizations described their work as intended to make the world better.

The case became a significant public controversy in 2022. The lecture did not present the case merely to condemn the organizations or determine legal liability. Instead, it used the example to ask what formal consent and anonymization fail to capture.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

**Claim:** A data practice can meet consent and anonymization requirements while still producing a troubling change in context.

**Evidence:** Information supplied during crisis-support conversations was repurposed for a commercial customer-service product. Users reportedly consented, and the data was described as anonymized.

**Interpretation:** The movement from a caring human relationship during a moment of distress to a for-profit customer-service system altered the purpose, beneficiaries, and relational meaning of the information. The people providing the data were not the primary beneficiaries of the commercial use. The lecture presented this mismatch as a reason to examine expectations and relationships in addition to identifiability.

## Contextual Integrity

Contextual integrity was introduced as an alternative privacy framework developed by Helen Nissenbaum approximately twenty to twenty-five years before the lecture. The framework emerged from dissatisfaction with the limits of the FIPPs and PII approaches and had gained traction in parts of information science, data science, and computer science.

Rather than treating people as isolated and decontextualized individuals, contextual integrity asks about:

- The context in which information was generated or shared.
- The relationships among the people and institutions involved.
- The expectations appropriate to those relationships.
- The movement of information from one context or purpose to another.
- The interests and obligations of the participants.

The lecture said that contextual integrity is better than abstract legal concepts of security and consent at predicting when people will feel that their privacy has been violated. It replaces a compliance-only question - whether collection is legal - with questions about what participants expected, which commitments governed the original interaction, and how information flows affect those commitments.

The framework can also guide technical design. It encourages practitioners to ask whether their goals require collecting as much data as planned, whether information must leave its original context, and whether collected information could be used with greater respect for the people represented in it.

The lecture nevertheless stopped short of presenting contextual integrity as sufficient. It described the framework as making meaningful headway while suggesting that contemporary privacy problems may require going further, particularly by questioning the underlying drive to generate and retain ever more data.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

**Claim:** Contextual integrity offers a richer approach to privacy than a checklist centered on consent and PII.

**Evidence:** The crisis-support, classroom, Canvas, and Havasupai cases all involved information moving beyond the relationships and purposes in which it originated, even when direct identification or formal consent did not fully explain the concern.

**Interpretation:** Privacy problems can arise from contextual shifts rather than simply from disclosure. Attention to context and relationships can expose risks that legal compliance overlooks and can support choices to collect, retain, or transfer less information.

## Case: Classroom Recording and University Data Use

The lecturer used the classroom itself to make contextual integrity concrete. Berkeley classrooms contain microphones and recording systems capable of capturing both instructors and students. The lecturer described participating in university data-privacy committees where proposals had included applying machine learning to student and course data or developing predictive analytics about matters such as who would speak in class.

The lecture distinguished between the language of baseline legal compliance and the language of relationships. Questions such as whether data could be deleted, who controlled it, and whether students or instructors consented reflected the inherited FIPPs model. Contextual integrity added a different concern: students may speak because they understand themselves to be in a particular relationship with an instructor and classmates, not because they expect their words to be stored indefinitely, broadcast, or used to train algorithms.

The lecture also described a previous opt-out default for classroom recordings. Faculty or graduate student instructors had to affirmatively opt out if they did not want even small discussion sections recorded. Attention to context and relationships helped change the default so that recording could instead become a matter for discussion between students and instructors. The lecturer acknowledged that the new arrangement had both advantages and disadvantages.

The example also connected data collection to labor and political circumstances. During a strike, the ability of administrators to determine whether an instructor was present in a classroom could matter, showing that classroom technology could operate as surveillance rather than simply as a neutral recording convenience.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

**Claim:** Classroom data cannot be adequately governed by isolating the available notes technology or asking only whether recording is legally permitted.

**Evidence:** Classroom systems capture relational speech, administrators have considered secondary analytic uses, previous defaults favored recording, and attendance information could acquire significance during a strike.

**Interpretation:** The meaning and consequences of recording depend on the relationships among students, instructors, and administrators. The same infrastructure that makes lectures available can also support indefinite storage, algorithmic training, or institutional surveillance.

## Case: The Canvas Breach

The lecture discussed a Canvas breach from the previous semester. It stated that the incident did not involve the release of information classified as PII and therefore did not trigger a university obligation to notify everyone affected. Many people may have learned about it through the student newspaper before receiving information from the university.

The exposed material reportedly included course discussions and messages exchanged through the learning platform. The lecturer emphasized that such communications could contain sensitive descriptions of personal circumstances, including immigration status or reasons for contacting an instructor. Campus leaders were notified that some messages might have been released, but the wider campus community was not initially notified under the PII-based framework.

The lecturer said that the matter was raised on a data-governance committee and acknowledged as a problem, but no further action followed in the account presented.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

**Claim:** Information can be highly sensitive in context without meeting the legal category that triggers a breach response.

**Evidence:** Course discussions and private messages may have been exposed, yet the absence of a PII release reportedly meant there was no general notification obligation.

**Interpretation:** The sensitivity of a communication depends on why it was shared, with whom, and under what expectations. A classification system focused on PII can miss the harm created when confidential educational communications escape their expected context.

## Case: TSA Body Scanners

The final major case concerned post-September 11 airport screening technologies. The scanners were described as obligatory passage points designed to produce realistic full-body images. Although early public criticism focused on machines effectively stripping away clothing and exposing bodies, the lecture said concern had largely faded for people whose bodies fit expected norms.

The Transportation Security Administration performed a privacy impact assessment, and the scanners incorporated measures intended to protect personal information. These included blurring facial areas and disabling image storage. The lecture used those measures to show that the system could pass formal privacy requirements without addressing all of the underlying concerns.

Participation was not meaningfully voluntary for someone who wanted to board a plane. Travelers had to undergo either scanning or another potentially invasive screening procedure. The scanners also compared bodies to templates of normal anatomy. When a body did not fit the template, the system could flag it for additional scrutiny and pat-downs.

Operators made male or female selections when using the equipment and also made gendered decisions about who would conduct a pat-down. The lecture highlighted the experiences of transgender and nonbinary travelers whose bodies did not fit these default classifications. It stated that the TSA took roughly a decade to make progress on the issue and that the result remained imperfect.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

**Claim:** The core privacy issues raised by body scanners extend beyond image identification, retention, or data security.

**Evidence:** Faces were blurred and image storage was disabled, yet travelers lacked a meaningful opportunity to avoid screening. Gendered templates and operator classifications could cause some bodies to be flagged for further searches.

**Interpretation:** The scanners implicated bodily dignity, compulsory exposure, institutional power, and differential treatment. People whose bodies matched normative templates could experience the scan as an ordinary inconvenience, while transgender, nonbinary, or otherwise nonconforming bodies faced a greater likelihood of invasive scrutiny. The technology therefore distributed privacy burdens unevenly.

## Data Minimization and Limits on Datafication

The lecture concluded by moving from better information governance toward a more fundamental question: should all possible data be generated in the first place? Contemporary institutions are driven to keep data flowing and to generate more of it. Contextual integrity can help practitioners ask whether they could achieve their purposes while collecting less data, but the lecture suggested that privacy may also require deliberately placing obstacles in the path of datafication.

This possibility differs from asking users to adjust privacy settings after collection has already been normalized. It asks whether particular experiences, relationships, communications, or bodily features should be converted into data at all. The concern was tied to preserving power, dignity, freedom, and respect for vulnerable people rather than merely preventing the disclosure of identifiers.

The lecture did not present privacy as a single natural concept. Its cases showed that situations labeled as privacy problems can also involve identity, sovereignty, governance, vulnerability, historical relationships, and unequal exposure. The purpose of the cases was to train students to look beneath the surface conclusion that something is simply a privacy violation and identify the broader stakes that legal compliance may conceal.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

**Claim:** Protecting privacy in a datafied world may require collecting less information or refusing to datafy certain domains.

**Evidence:** The cases showed that consent, anonymization, security controls, and privacy assessments could coexist with changed purposes, collective harms, surveillance, or differential exposure.

**Interpretation:** Once extensive collection is treated as inevitable, individuals are left to manage its consequences through limited settings and opt-outs. Questioning data generation itself shifts attention toward the design choices and institutional incentives that produce the need for those individual defenses.

## Human Contexts and Ethics Toolkit

### Power

The lecture repeatedly connected privacy to asymmetric capacities to shape other people's behavior. Companies determine the terms users must accept to access services and profit from information about preferences, connections, and communication. Users can adjust some settings, but they have little ability to alter the underlying business model or negotiate alternatives.

The Havasupai case connected scientific authority to tribal sovereignty. Researchers controlled the expansion of research purposes, while the tribe's response - excluding researchers, litigating, and securing the samples' return - asserted governance and self-determination against that institutional power.

University recording systems similarly distribute authority unevenly. Administrators can retain classroom data, propose secondary analytics, or use recordings to observe classroom activity, while students and instructors may have limited control over future uses. The possible use of attendance information during a strike made the link between technical and political power especially visible.

TSA screening presents a direct form of compulsory power. Travelers cannot meaningfully refuse all screening if they wish to fly, and the system can subject people whose bodies depart from expected templates to additional searches. In each example, privacy concerns arose not only from information disclosure but also from who could set the conditions of participation, define normality, and decide how collected information would be used.

### Sociotechnical Systems

The lecture treated privacy systems as combinations of technical infrastructures, institutional policies, legal categories, human judgments, commercial incentives, and social relationships. Terms-of-service systems depend simultaneously on interface defaults, legal agreements, user behavior, company business models, and government enforcement. Their consequences cannot be attributed to the document or the technology alone.

Crisis-support data moved through a system involving people in distress, counselors, a nonprofit, a for-profit company, anonymization procedures, and a machine-learning product. The ethical question arose from the relationship among these components and from the transition between purposes.

Berkeley classroom recording involved microphones, storage systems, opt-out defaults, instructors, students, administrators, committees, and possible analytic uses. The Canvas case likewise joined a technical breach to legal definitions and institutional notification practices. TSA screening combined body-imaging technology with binary operator choices, anatomical templates, human pat-down decisions, and compulsory airport procedures. Across these cases, the lecture's central point was that technical privacy features could not be isolated from the institutions and relationships through which they operated.

### Identity/Positionality

The Havasupai case showed that data can implicate collective identity as well as isolated individuals. Genetic research concerning mental illness and geographic origins affected how a tribe could be understood and represented. The lecture connected this effect to Native history, settler relations, sovereignty, and cultural patrimony.

The TSA case made differential treatment especially explicit. Scanner templates defined particular bodies as normal, while transgender and nonbinary travelers could be marked as anomalous and subjected to additional scrutiny. Operators' male-or-female selections embedded assumptions about gender and anatomy in the screening process.

The Canvas example indicated that educational communications might disclose sensitive aspects of a person's position, including immigration status. The lecture also emphasized vulnerability in the Crisis Text Line case: consent was obtained from people seeking assistance during moments of severe distress, which shaped the relational meaning of their decision. These examples showed how privacy practices affect people differently depending on collective membership, bodily conformity, vulnerability, and institutional position.

### Narratives

The lecture challenged the narrative that privacy is principally a matter of autonomous individuals making informed choices. Terms of service, cookie settings, and opt-outs make this account appear natural by presenting privacy as something users personally manage. The lecture countered that users often face constrained, all-or-nothing choices within systems designed to preserve data collection.

A second narrative treats more data as both inevitable and desirable. The lecture questioned this assumption by asking whether privacy might require obstacles to data generation and whether some matters should not be datafied at all.

The cases also exposed competing accounts of beneficial innovation. Crisis Text Line and its spinoff reportedly described their goal as making the world better, while critics focused on the movement of vulnerable people's conversations into a commercial product. University analytics could be narrated as improving education while also functioning as surveillance. TSA scanners could be presented as security technologies while defining some bodies as abnormal and exposing them to additional scrutiny. Contextual integrity offered a way to examine which account of a technology becomes dominant, whose expectations it recognizes, and which alternative futures remain possible.

## Open or Unclear Points

- The lecture referred to "seven limits" exposed by the cases, but these notes did not preserve a clear, numbered list of all seven.
- Some terminology for the five fair-information principles was transcribed inconsistently as "FIPPs," "FIPs," "fits," or "hips."
- The instructor invited further conversation about why regulation might not currently be the answer but did not develop that argument in the recorded lecture.
- The lecture said contextual integrity might not go as far as necessary, but it did not settle on a complete replacement framework beyond suggesting data minimization and possible limits on datafication.
