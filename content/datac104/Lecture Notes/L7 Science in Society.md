# L7 Science in Society

## Executive Summary

The lecture extends an earlier account of scientific knowledge associated with Thomas Kuhn. Scientific authority depends not only on observation, induction, empiricism, and other conceptual methods, but also on the organization of scientific communities around shared paradigms, institutions, and puzzle-solving practices.

This lecture places science back within society by asking who belongs to scientific communities, who benefits from scientific knowledge, who participates in producing and trusting it, and why society grants science authority and resources. Its central concept is expertise, understood as both domain-specific competence and a socially recognized position from which people make consequential recommendations under uncertainty.

Four cases develop the argument. Organized campaigns around climate science show how media conventions, industry funding, and scientific uncertainty can interact to manufacture public doubt. The experience of Cumbrian sheep farmers after Chernobyl shows how excluding local knowledge can weaken both research and public trust. The L'Aquila earthquake case distinguishes failures of prediction from failures to communicate risk. Pasteur's work illustrates science as the construction of durable networks among laboratories, practices, institutions, objects, and affected groups.

Together, these cases support the idea of socially robust knowledge. Scientific knowledge becomes socially robust when scientific communities function well internally and sustain strong relationships with the broader publics affected by their work. This creates an enduring tension between scientific autonomy and public inclusion, as well as between maintaining boundaries around expertise and opening scientific practices to relevant knowledge from outside established institutions.

## From Scientific Communities to Science in Society

The lecture begins from Thomas Kuhn's account of scientific knowledge. Kuhn helps explain scientific power by emphasizing the social organization of scientific communities alongside the conceptual techniques of science.

At a given time, a scientific community is unified around a paradigm or shared theory. Its members solve lower-level puzzles through experimentation within that paradigm. When puzzles continually resist solution, the community may eventually reevaluate the paradigm and adopt a new one. Scientific authority therefore rests partly on organized communal practices rather than solely on methods that individuals can apply independently.

This account makes journals, professional organizations, and the organization of scientific communities as important to scientific robustness as induction, observation, empiricism, and the philosophy or logic of science. For science to function well, these conceptual and social elements must work together.

Kuhn's focus leaves additional questions open:

- Who is permitted to belong to the scientific community?
- Who benefits from scientific knowledge?
- Who participates in producing that knowledge?
- Who is expected to trust and act on it?
- Why does science possess authority in society?
- Why does society devote substantial resources to science?
- How is the trust supporting scientific authority lost or eroded?

The lecture describes its task as putting science back into society. It does not treat science as an isolated activity that is occasionally influenced from outside. Instead, it investigates the relationships connecting scientific communities, experts, governments, industries, media institutions, and affected publics.

## Climate Science, Manufactured Doubt, and the Boundary of Science

The lecture's first case concerns the science of anthropogenic climate change in the late twentieth century. According to the lecture, climate scientists had strong data beginning in the 1970s connecting rising global temperatures to human causes, and a strong scientific consensus developed around this conclusion despite some dissent.

American news media often represented the issue as a much deeper scientific controversy. In pursuit of balanced coverage, media outlets gave equal attention to one expert supporting the consensus and another opposing it. This format suggested that expert opinion was evenly divided even though, as presented in the lecture, the balance of expert opinion overwhelmingly supported anthropogenic explanations.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The initial interpretation is that media practices politicized or inaccurately communicated an established scientific consensus. The convention of giving equal attention to two sides did not accurately represent the relative weight of scientific opinion.

The lecture then adds a second explanation drawn from historian of science Naomi Oreskes and the book *Merchants of Doubt*. A small number of scientists, some associated with Cold War defense research, participated in a sustained campaign to cast doubt on the consensus. They emphasized the unavoidable uncertainty of empirical knowledge and highlighted margins of error in scientific studies.

Climate scientists working for organizations such as the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change reported uncertainty as part of responsible scientific practice. Critics then presented these qualifications as evidence that no meaningful consensus existed. The lecture says that some scientists challenging the consensus received research funding from the fossil fuel industry. It also states that some of the same figures had previously worked with the tobacco industry to challenge the connection between cigarette smoking and lung cancer.

The case therefore includes both external and internal institutional vulnerabilities. Media norms shaped public representations of the evidence, private industry supplied financial incentives, and participating scientists used genuine features of empirical knowledge to foster doubt.

### Science and Politics

One response to the case is to argue that outside political or corporate forces corrupted an otherwise neutral, objective, impartial, and apolitical science. The lecture treats that explanation as part of the story but questions the assumption that good science exists wholly outside politics.

Drawing a firm boundary can help distinguish scientific expertise from financially motivated interference. At the same time, later cases show that protecting science by excluding outsiders can also eliminate relevant knowledge. The climate case therefore introduces a tension that continues throughout the lecture: scientific authority may require boundaries, but those boundaries can neither eliminate science's political relationships nor determine in advance whose participation will improve knowledge.

## Chernobyl, Cumbrian Sheep Farmers, and Local Knowledge

The second case concerns the effects of the 1986 Chernobyl nuclear disaster on sheep farming in Cumbria in northwestern Great Britain. Radioactive material spread through rain, creating the possibility that sheep grazing on contaminated land would produce unsafe meat and milk.

When radiation persisted in sheep and their waste, the British government extended bans on slaughtering and selling the animals. These restrictions were financially devastating for farmers who depended on sheep for their livelihoods. Government scientists investigated how to limit exposure, reduce radiation levels, and identify which sheep or areas were most affected.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The farmers attempted to contribute knowledge about regional geography, water accumulation, land use, and sheep behavior. The scientists ignored these suggestions. According to the lecture, the farmers consequently came to resent and distrust the experts, with some suspecting a political conspiracy involving radiation from a nearby nuclear facility.

Their distrust did not arise simply from rejecting science's capacity to determine facts. It also reflected their exclusion from a process to which they could contribute relevant observations unavailable to the visiting experts. Later study reportedly showed that much of their understanding was correct. Consulting them could have shortened the disruption to their livelihoods and reduced the erosion of trust in the government and its experts.

The case supports the argument that outside contributions do not necessarily corrupt science. In some circumstances, challenging scientific norms about whose knowledge is trustworthy can make scientific knowledge more robust.

### Boundary Tension

The climate and Cumbria cases point in different directions. The climate case demonstrates why scientific communities may need to protect their processes from organized, financially motivated doubt. The Cumbria case demonstrates why a community can produce worse knowledge by treating its institutional boundaries as sufficient grounds for excluding lay participants.

The lecture does not offer a simple rule for resolving this tension. It identifies a continuing need to distinguish harmful interference from meaningful participation without assuming that credentials alone settle the value of a person's knowledge.

## Expertise as Knowledge, Social Position, and Judgment

Expertise occupies the intersection between science and society. Modern life depends on expert systems that test medicines, regulate industries, and evaluate the safety of food, water, and air. Experts perform or oversee these processes and make recommendations that others are expected to act upon.

Not all expertise is scientific, but the lecture describes expertise generally as institutionalized skill or knowledge within a domain. Expertise is more than competence. It is also a social position connected to education, credentials, reputation, and recognition by others.

Expertise is therefore relational. A person does not become an expert merely by knowing how to do something well. Other people and institutions must recognize that person as entitled to provide authoritative judgments. Because experts make recommendations affecting others, expertise is directly connected to social power and decision-making.

### Trust and the Problem of Legitimate Expertise

People regularly defer to experts, often without actively considering that trust. They may also distrust experts in particular fields. The lecture identifies two problems with the simple instruction to trust experts:

- Experts within the same field can disagree, leaving the public to decide which expert judgment deserves confidence.
- People who lack conventional credentials can present themselves as experts, particularly through social media, making traditional indicators of authority less decisive.

Earlier philosophers of science, including logical positivists and Karl Popper, sought clear criteria distinguishing science from pseudoscience. The lecture connects this effort to their concern about fascism and the spread of claims such as race science in 1930s Vienna. Although the previous lecture had discussed limitations in their proposed criteria, their desire for a clear demarcation responded to a significant problem: how people can identify trustworthy knowledge when competing claims present themselves as scientific.

### Judgment Under Uncertainty

Scientific expertise cannot be grounded in possession of absolute truth. The lecture recalls the problem of induction and the black swan example to emphasize that past observation cannot provide complete formal certainty. Empirical knowledge always contains uncertainty and fallibility, even when it is strong and reliable.

Karl Popper's position, as presented here, is that experts can still act using the least false or best available knowledge. Sheila Jasanoff's account makes this capacity central to expertise: experts exercise judgment when making rapid, high-stakes decisions with imperfect information and are expected to do so in the public interest.

The distinction between judgment and decision matters. Judgment is necessary precisely because the available science does not dictate one obvious action. Experts must interpret uncertainty, consider context and possible outcomes, and recommend action without knowing the full truth of the matter.

## Expert Error and the Erosion of Public Trust

Because experts repeatedly make consequential judgments with imperfect information, some errors are inevitable. The lecture describes occasional error as a feature of a system of expert judgment rather than something that can be entirely eliminated.

Members of the public may nevertheless have little tolerance for such errors because expert recommendations can affect health, safety, families, and livelihoods. A small number of failures may damage credibility. Repeated failures in particular fields can also develop into broader distrust of science.

Education can counteract this process. Primary, secondary, and university education can familiarize people with how expert judgments are formed. Greater literacy can help members of the public evaluate sources, understand methods, and interpret expressions of uncertainty without treating uncertainty as proof that the underlying knowledge is worthless.

The lecture nevertheless argues that distrust cannot be explained exclusively as a failure of public understanding. Governments, media organizations, and scientific institutions can contribute to distrust by excluding relevant participants, misrepresenting evidence, or mishandling the communication of uncertainty. Scientific institutions can therefore fail through their relationships with the public even when the internal methods of observation, hypothesis formation, and data gathering appear proper.

The Cumbria case illustrates exclusion from knowledge production. The climate case illustrates intentional or institutionally facilitated misrepresentation. The lecture also briefly identifies the history of AIDS research as another instance in which resistance to working with patients was eventually challenged by activists, although the details of this example are incomplete in these notes.

## The L'Aquila Earthquake and Risk Communication

The lecture's third extended case concerns laboratory technician Giampaolo Giuliani, Italian seismologists, and an earthquake near L'Aquila. Giuliani, who worked for Italy's National Institute of Nuclear Physics, predicted a major earthquake based on increased radon gas emissions. His claim received mass-media attention and contributed to public concern.

Professional seismologists characterized Giuliani as an amateur and alarmist rather than an expert in their field. The lecture explains that radon emissions had not been empirically demonstrated to provide a sufficiently certain basis for predicting earthquakes and that the relevant scientific consensus held that temporally precise earthquake prediction was not possible. Giuliani's claim had not been vetted through the scientific community's established practices.

One month later, a magnitude 6.3 earthquake struck L'Aquila and killed more than 300 people. Six seismologists who had made public statements were subsequently sentenced to prison.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

A results-based interpretation might conclude that the seismologists should have accepted Giuliani's warning because the earthquake occurred. The lecture rejects that retrospective logic as an adequate guide for future decisions. Giuliani was correct about the outcome, but the lecture argues that the scientific community still had appropriate grounds for skepticism because his method lacked support within its established evidence and practices.

The lecture says that the legal judgments did not punish the seismologists merely for failing to predict the earthquake or for ignoring a correct outsider. Instead, the central failure was their communication of the evidence. They told the public that there was no earthquake risk even though, according to the information described in the lecture, the absolute risk remained very small but had doubled during the preceding week.

The absolute probability and the recent change conveyed different but simultaneously relevant information. By communicating no risk, the experts encouraged residents to lower their guard. A clearer account of the small absolute risk and its increase could have enabled members of the public to take precautionary action.

### Relationship to the Other Cases

The L'Aquila case differs from Cumbria. The sheep farmers possessed direct local knowledge about their land and animals that was relevant to the investigation. The lecture does not describe Giuliani as possessing a comparable source of reliable local expertise. The seismologists' principal fault was therefore not their defense of communal scientific standards but their failure to communicate the evidence and uncertainty properly.

This distinction reinforces the lecture's broader argument: socially robust expertise does not mean automatically accepting every outside claim. It means managing the relationship between vetted scientific knowledge and affected publics, including explaining uncertainty accurately enough for people to understand and act upon it.

## Socially Robust Knowledge

Epistemic or logical robustness depends on such factors as the quality of observations, instruments, and data. The lecture argues that these factors matter but do not exhaust what makes scientific knowledge strong.

Social robustness derives from the groups, institutions, and communities that produce and apply knowledge. It includes:

- The scientific community's internal organization for solving puzzles within a paradigm.
- Its methods for managing disagreement and evaluating new theories when existing paradigms encounter persistent problems.
- Appropriate openness to groups possessing relevant observations or knowledge.
- Appropriate closure against forms of participation that undermine rather than improve inquiry.
- Effective communication among scientists, governments, media institutions, and affected publics.
- Practices that preserve or strengthen public trust in scientific expertise.

Knowledge is socially robust when the internal organization of scientific communities functions well and their relationships with the broader public are strong. Public communication and participation are consequently not optional additions to an otherwise completed scientific process. They can affect the quality, legitimacy, and practical operation of scientific knowledge itself.

## Witnessing, Transparency, and Scientific Legitimacy

The lecture locates questions of public trust within the longer history of modern experimental science. In seventeenth-century experimental settings, witnesses observed demonstrations and attested that reported events had occurred. Their presence allowed other scientists, including distant researchers unfamiliar with an experimenter's personal reputation, to place greater confidence in the results.

Witnessing contributed to a democratic ethos of science as public, open, and transparent. Scientific claims were to be demonstrated before others and supported through evidence and reasons.

At the same time, scientific legitimacy came to rest partly on a different ideal: science as uniquely impartial, value-neutral, and separate from politics and society. This image made scientific autonomy appear to depend on maintaining a clear division between scientific work and the larger social world.

The lecture presents these ideals as being in tension. Transparency and public witnessing open science to broader scrutiny, while the myth of complete neutrality locates legitimacy in science's separation from political and social life.

## The Social Contract for Science

The boundary between science and society became increasingly difficult to sustain during the twentieth century as scientific work changed in scale and organization. Since World War II, the lecture argues, science has become increasingly dependent on state and industrial funding. States and industries, in turn, have relied more heavily on scientific evidence, research, and expert advice.

This relationship is described as a social contract for science. Scientific research was expected to strengthen state power and security, encourage economic growth, and improve public health. In exchange, science received funding, reputation, and a degree of autonomy in choosing and conducting research.

As society became more dependent on science and technology, experts acquired greater influence over consequential public decisions. Scientific institutions also became implicated in pollution, environmental degradation, failures, and accidents. These controversies contributed to demands, especially from the 1970s onward, for greater transparency and regulation.

### Legitimation and Extension

The lecture frames the resulting tension as a problem of legitimation and extension:

- Scientific legitimacy can be associated with autonomy and the maintenance of a boundary between science and non-science.
- Scientific legitimacy can also be associated with openness, transparency, and participation by the public.
- Extending participation can strengthen public trust and supply relevant knowledge, but it can also blur the boundaries used to identify legitimate expertise.

Maintaining public trust may therefore conflict with maintaining a firm boundary between scientific and non-scientific knowledge. Both autonomy and openness can support legitimacy, but the lecture does not provide a simple formula for combining them.

## The Scientization of Politics and Politicization of Science

The lecture describes a reciprocal historical process in which politics became more scientific and science became more political. Drawing on historian Steven Epstein, it states that political institutions increasingly call upon science to produce consensus and settle social disputes.

When political controversies are reframed as technical disputes, competing groups recruit their own experts, much as lawyers present rival expert witnesses to a jury. The scientization of politics therefore contributes to the politicization of science. Science is not simply a separate institution that politics occasionally contaminates. Its political role follows from how societies use expert knowledge to govern, allocate resources, resolve disputes, and justify action.

This account complicates the view introduced through the climate case that political influence is always an outside intrusion into otherwise apolitical science. The lecture does not erase distinctions between scientific and political practices, but it argues that their historical and institutional relationships must be examined rather than treated as accidental.

## Pasteur, Networks, and Science as World-Making

The lecture concludes with Bruno Latour's interpretation of Louis Pasteur's work on germ theory and vaccines. Pasteur's achievement is presented not simply as observing something previously unseen through a microscope. It also involved making germ theory a practical reality across French society.

Pasteur persuaded multiple relevant groups to pay attention to entities such as the anthrax bacillus affecting cattle and to act in the ways his research prescribed. He left the laboratory, entered agricultural settings, translated problems encountered in the field into laboratory terms, and convinced cattle owners that his methods could help them.

His laboratory became an "obligatory passage point": cattle owners wishing to protect their animals increasingly had to work through Pasteur's laboratory, methods, and concepts. His success therefore depended on rhetoric, political skill, translation, and enrollment as well as experimental observation.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The claim is that there is no clear separation between the laboratory and the society outside it. Science is social not only because communities validate experimental knowledge, but also because scientific practices organize conduct beyond the laboratory.

Pasteur's work illustrates this claim by connecting microbes, laboratory practices, vaccines, cattle, cattle owners, and existing veterinary practices in a new network. Germs became part of French social reality because institutions and people learned to recognize, reproduce, manage, and act upon them.

The lecture interprets science as a form of world-making. Communication is not merely an additional task performed after research is complete. Building relationships, persuading participants, and stabilizing networks are part of scientific work. On this account, scientific knowledge is socially robust insofar as it constructs durable networks of people, objects, practices, and institutions.

## Human Contexts and Ethics Toolkit

### Power

Expertise gives some people an asymmetric capacity to shape how others behave. Doctors, lawyers, scientists, regulators, and other experts make recommendations that patients, citizens, governments, and industries are expected to follow. This authority is not based only on personal competence. Credentials, education, professional reputation, journals, regulatory bodies, and institutional recognition help determine whose judgment can structure public action.

The climate case connects scientific and political-economic power. Media organizations determined how expert disagreement was presented, while fossil fuel industry funding created incentives for a subset of scientists to emphasize uncertainty. These arrangements shaped what the public understood as the state of scientific knowledge and could influence responses to climate change.

The Cumbria case shows experts and government institutions exercising power over farmers' livelihoods through restrictions on sheep sales. The farmers bore substantial consequences but initially lacked influence over the research process, even though they possessed relevant knowledge about the land and animals. Their exclusion demonstrates how institutional authority can determine both whose evidence counts and who must absorb the costs of expert decisions.

In L'Aquila, seismologists' public authority shaped residents' understanding of earthquake risk. Saying that there was no risk could alter whether people took precautions. The lecture consequently links expert power to a duty to communicate uncertainty in ways that allow affected people to make informed decisions.

Pasteur's laboratory became an obligatory passage point by reorganizing how cattle owners addressed disease. This illustrates scientific power as the capacity to build a network in which other actors must pass through scientific institutions, concepts, and practices to achieve their goals.

### Sociotechnical Systems

The lecture treats scientific knowledge as the product of intertwined social and technical arrangements. Observation, instruments, data, and experiments cannot be isolated from professional organizations, paradigms, funding structures, media practices, government regulation, and public trust.

The climate controversy involved scientific studies and margins of error, but it also depended on industry grants, media norms of balanced coverage, expert status, and political disputes. The public meaning of climate evidence emerged from this combined system rather than from data alone.

The Cumbria case joined radioactive fallout, rain, soil and water patterns, sheep behavior, scientific measurement, government bans, and farmers' livelihoods. The technical task of measuring radiation could not be separated from local environmental knowledge or from the institutional decisions determining which sheep could enter the market.

The L'Aquila case similarly connected risk estimates, radon measurements, professional credentialing, media coverage, public communication, precautionary behavior, and legal accountability. The lecture distinguishes the production of a risk estimate from the social process through which that estimate becomes actionable.

Latour's account makes the sociotechnical argument explicit. Pasteur's scientific achievement consisted in assembling a durable network of microbes, laboratories, instruments, practices, vaccines, livestock, cattle owners, and institutions. Science became effective by reorganizing this entire network rather than by acting as an isolated technical component.

### Identity/Positionality

These notes provide limited discussion of identity categories such as gender, race, disability, or immigration status. It does, however, show how professional and economic positions shape whose knowledge is recognized and whose interests are affected.

Credentials position people differently within systems of expertise. Professional seismologists could dismiss Giuliani as a laboratory technician working outside their discipline, while the Cumbrian farmers were treated as non-scientists despite their situated knowledge of local geography and animal behavior. The lecture does not claim that every excluded outsider possesses reliable knowledge. It instead shows that institutional position affects whether a person's observations receive consideration.

Class and livelihood are relevant in the Cumbria case. Farmers depended economically on selling sheep and therefore had more directly at stake than the visiting scientists. Their position gave them distinctive knowledge and exposed them to the financial consequences of government decisions, yet it did not initially grant them corresponding authority in the research process.

The lecture also mentions patients and AIDS activists as participants whose experience was initially resisted and later incorporated into research, although this example is not developed fully. Across these cases, positionality matters because the people most affected by scientific decisions may possess relevant experience while lacking conventional credentials or institutional power.

### Narratives

The lecture identifies several competing narratives about science and expertise. One presents science as neutral, objective, impartial, and separate from politics. This narrative makes failures appear to result from outsiders introducing political or financial interests into an otherwise autonomous institution. The climate case partly supports concern about such interference, but the lecture complicates the broader story by showing that scientific institutions already depend on governments, industries, media, and public legitimacy.

A second narrative is that the public should simply trust experts. The lecture complicates this account because experts can disagree, make unavoidable errors, communicate poorly, exclude useful knowledge, or participate in organized efforts to manufacture doubt. Public distrust is therefore not narrated solely as ignorance or educational failure.

Media narratives also shape how scientific disagreement appears. Balanced climate coverage converted an uneven distribution of expert opinion into the appearance of two equally supported positions. In the earthquake case, a retrospective story of a lone outsider who correctly foresaw disaster obscured the distinction between a correct outcome and an adequately supported predictive method.

The lecture offers socially robust knowledge as an alternative narrative. Science gains strength not by existing outside society but through well-functioning communities and trustworthy relationships with affected publics. Latour's Pasteur case extends this account into a narrative of science as world-making, in which scientific success means creating stable networks that make new entities, practices, and forms of action part of social reality.
