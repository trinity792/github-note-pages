# L2 Making the Datafied World 2

## Executive Summary

The lecture continued the course's foundations unit by explaining how the present datafied world was historically made and how it might be made differently. It presented the datafied world as a stack of data, analytics, algorithms, and AI or machine learning systems, emphasizing that the upper layers depend on ubiquitous data.

Meta illustrated a datafied business model in which platforms collect users' content, connections, preferences, and behavior, then use automated systems to personalize content and sell advertising. The example also showed how accumulated data and revenue support later products, while data-intensive technologies generate legal and social resistance and alter relationships in settings such as the classroom.

The main case concerned rental-pricing algorithms. These systems combine property, tenant, rental-history, and market data to recommend prices intended to maximize predicted landlord revenue. The lecture situated them within a longer infrastructure of property-management software and private real-estate data collection, then described disputes over whether shared data and algorithmic recommendations constitute coordination or price-fixing.

The HCE lenses - power, sociotechnical systems, identity and positionality, and narratives - were used to move beyond judgments about whether an algorithm or its users are simply good or bad. These lenses direct attention to who controls data and optimization goals, the wider system surrounding an algorithm, how different social positions shape access and experience, and the competing stories through which technological change is understood. The lecture concluded by contrasting technological and social determinism with a sociotechnical account of how society and technology form one another.

## Course Structure and the Foundations Unit

The course is organized into weeks of lectures and readings. The first week establishes foundations through two lectures on making the datafied world. Later units address making data, making knowledge, governance and social order, data- and AI-filled futures, the technology ecosystem, and ethical practice. The course therefore builds toward ethics through contextual knowledge about data, machine learning, and AI.

Each lecture week prepares students for discussion during the following week by connecting readings, topics, and case studies. The early lectures are intended to make the contemporary world of data more visible as a historically produced arrangement rather than a natural or inevitable condition. The instructor described this as learning to “denaturalize the present” and think critically about how the future could be made differently.

The concepts introduced early in the semester may initially appear abstract, but the course will reinforce them through subsequent lectures and cases.

## The Datafied World

The lecture defined a datafied world as one in which data collection, analytics, data-driven services, machine learning, and AI - all supported by massive datasets - shape increasing portions of life. These systems affect:

- They affect how people understand themselves.
- They affect how people relate to one another.
- They affect how collective life is organized.
- They affect how people imagine the future.

The datafied world includes diverse applications, such as social media, large language models, predictive policing, and surveillance. Rather than treating these as an unstructured collection of new technologies, the lecture introduced concepts for identifying their shared foundations and analyzing how they operate in context.

### Example or Case: Meta's Platforms

A 2015 image of Mark Zuckerberg standing before a map of Facebook users and their connections illustrated one way of visualizing the datafied world. Facebook use produced data about users, their text and images, their connections, their preferences, their clicks, and their movement through the platform. These data made global social connections visible in a new form.

The lecture characterized social media as a datafied business model. Meta's ecosystem includes Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, Messenger, and Threads. Its platforms collect data about users and use that information to deliver targeted content and advertising. The instructor stated that advertising accounts for about 95 percent of Meta's revenue.

At this scale, decisions about which content users see must be automated. Algorithms operate on previously collected data to generate knowledge about users and personalize platform experiences, with engagement serving as an optimization goal.

The lecture also connected the business model to social resistance. It cited recent penalties and a settlement concerning child-safety laws and algorithms alleged to be addictive or inadequately explained to users. These examples were presented to show that massive data collection and datafied business models develop alongside legal disputes, political action, and social struggle.

Accumulated data and revenue also become resources for later products. Meta uses data-intensive capabilities in AI assistants, image and voice recognition, translation, recommendation systems, search, and AI-enabled glasses. The transition from one generation of platforms to another demonstrates that data collected for current products can support products whose eventual form has not yet been determined.

## Datafied Technologies and Social Relations

The presence of datafied devices can change social relationships even when their technical capabilities are not actively demonstrated. The instructor used AI-enabled glasses and the course prohibition on unauthorized recording to illustrate this point. Because an instructor may be unable to determine whether someone is recording, the possibility of recording changes the relationship between instructor, students, and the potential wider audience.

The example connects technical capability to social order. A classroom interaction organized around a defined relationship between instructors and enrolled students becomes different if the lecture may be recorded or broadcast beyond the room. Datafied technologies therefore do not merely collect information; their presence can alter expectations, trust, behavior, and the boundaries of an interaction.

## The Datafied World as a Stack

The lecture presented four interdependent layers of the datafied world.

1. Data form the foundation. Trackers, sensors, and signals convert aspects of the world into ubiquitous data.
2. Analytics build on data to produce knowledge. They may characterize the past or present, predict the future, classify cases, or support decisions.
3. Algorithms provide step-by-step, rule-governed, automated processes. They regularize and automate both practical activities and ways of thinking.
4. AI and machine learning systems combine the lower layers in systems that can learn or adapt while monitoring, optimizing, and planning.

The main claim was not that these layers are always separate in practice. Rather, the stack provides a way to identify which part of a datafied system is under discussion and to recognize that AI and machine learning depend on data, analytics, and algorithms. Ubiquitous data are foundational to all four layers.

## The HCE Analytical Lenses

The lecture returned to four top-level HCE tools introduced in the previous class:

- Power examines who can act, decide, control resources, or shape outcomes.
- Sociotechnical systems direct attention beyond an isolated device or algorithm to the people, institutions, data, infrastructure, and practices around it.
- Identity and positionality examine how people's social locations affect their experiences of and access to a system.
- Narratives identify the stories that connect past, present, and future and make particular interpretations of technology appear plausible.

These tools are analytical lenses for recognizing specific dimensions of real situations. The lecture suggested that all four will often be relevant to a datafied-world case. Their purpose is to produce a more sensitive analysis and better guidance for action than judgments framed only in terms of good or bad algorithms, technologies, or people.

The earlier example of Langdon Winner's bridges or overpasses had been used to show how technologies can have politics, structure social order, and help make worlds. The rental-pricing case extended that analysis from physical infrastructure to systems built through and around data.

## The Datafication of Rental Housing

The rental market is datafied through many forms of information, including credit scores, household income, household composition, property features and conditions, location, crime statistics, school districts, insurance variables, walkability scores, financing information, rental listings, and application data.

Rental applications require prospective tenants to provide personal information as a condition of seeking housing. Property owners and managers also hold data about units, occupancy, rental histories, and tenants. This creates an information-rich environment in which rental decisions can be supported or automated through analytics and algorithms.

Not everything relevant to renting is equally easy to quantify. The lecture contrasted readily measured property and market attributes with experiences and choices that may remain outside the dataset, such as renters' lived experiences or decisions between paying rent and supporting a family. Datafication captures what can readily be quantified while leaving other concerns out. One possible response is to attempt to quantify the omitted concerns as well, although the lecture did not present that as a complete resolution.

## Rental-Pricing Algorithms

Rental-pricing companies work with landlords who provide detailed data about their properties, rental histories, units, and tenants. An algorithm then recommends a pricing strategy for each unit.

The system is described as “optimal” only relative to a specified goal. In the case presented, optimization means maximizing predicted revenue from a unit based on what local tenants are predicted to be willing and able to pay. The lecture emphasized that other actors could define different objectives. A local politician concerned with affordable housing, for example, might optimize for a different value than maximum landlord revenue.

This makes the optimization target a social and political choice rather than a purely technical property. The companies developing the systems sell access to an algorithm or a larger software package to property owners. The systems can be described as predictive analytics, optimization, decision-support algorithms, or, increasingly, AI. The instructor noted that “AI” has become an amorphous label for work that data scientists have also described in other terms.

### Historical and Infrastructural Context

The lecture situated rental-pricing algorithms within a longer history of data-intensive real estate:

- Property-management and accounting software for multifamily housing has existed since the 1980s and initially ran on personal computers.
- Apartment-data providers have collected unit-, property-, and market-level data over approximately three decades.
- Other private companies have collected multifamily-property sales data over roughly 25 years.
- Forecasting software has been used to estimate future changes in rental-property values.

This history shows that contemporary rental algorithms did not emerge without supporting infrastructure. They depend on decades of privately collected and aggregated data, software systems, and established property-management practices. The technical task of incrementally improving a landlord's revenue optimization may be relatively simple, while its implementation within housing markets is politically complex.

The lecture used “political” broadly to mean negotiation over social relationships and social order when power is at stake, not merely conflict between political parties.

## RealPage and the Politics of Algorithmic Rent-Setting

The central case concerned RealPage, whose pricing system had been called YieldStar and later AI Revenue Management. The lecture also named competitors and related firms, including Yardi, RentCafe, and PropertyShark, to emphasize that RealPage belongs to a larger property-technology ecosystem.

A 2022 ProPublica investigation focused public attention on whether one company's algorithm could contribute to rising rent. According to the lecture, the reporting generated public outrage and calls for political action.

The instructor also noted that money generated in the property-management industry can circulate into other institutions. Yardi's connection to a scholars program in Berkeley's political science department illustrated how revenue from these systems may later appear as philanthropy.

### Local and State Responses

The lecture described several government responses:

- San Francisco banned residential landlords from using algorithms to set rent in 2024. The city treated the interaction of landlords' data through a shared algorithm as a form of collusion.
- Berkeley enacted a similar ban in March 2025.
- RealPage sued Berkeley, arguing that a ban on data-driven pricing algorithms was an unconstitutional restriction on free speech.
- California subsequently enacted Assembly Bill 325, effective in January of the lecture year. According to the lecture, it did not ban pricing software itself but prohibited using data from outside an individual landlord's own data silo.

The California approach distinguished between using an algorithm on a company's internal information and sharing information across otherwise separate companies. The shared-data arrangement was treated as the central source of potential collusion.

### National Responses

The Biden administration sought to quantify the effect of the algorithms on rent. The lecture reported estimates of an additional $62 per month for San Francisco renters and $100 per month for San Diego renters whose landlords used RealPage. Location-specific figures made the effects more legible to affected renters and policymakers.

The Biden administration sued RealPage, characterizing the system as market-rigging through price-fixing. The lecture stated that the Trump administration continued the broader line of argument by suing six large landlords for allegedly using the tools to share information and coordinate prices rather than directly targeting only the software provider.

The case involving Greystar was settled in summer 2025. Greystar agreed to stop using RealPage while continuing to maintain that its conduct was legal. The instructor emphasized that a settlement is not a verdict. The settlement was presented by Attorney General Pam Bondi as an effort to make housing affordable by opposing conduct described as a violation of free-market competition.

### Claim, Evidence, and Interpretation

The lecture distinguished the operation of the algorithm from the interpretation of its market effects.

The basic critical claim is that landlords and tenants contribute data under unequal conditions, the algorithm combines those data, and the resulting recommendations can raise the financial burden on some of the same tenants whose information helped make the system possible. Legal challenges interpret the sharing of information through a common algorithm as coordination among firms that should otherwise compete.

However, the instructor resisted reducing the case to good renters and bad landlords or to the idea that algorithms inherently remove human control. Algorithms produce knowledge from data, raising the question of why access to knowledge should itself be denied to landlords. The lecture also presented the possibility that data-based pricing could avoid some prejudices involved when an individual landlord looks at a prospective tenant and makes an intuitive demographic judgment.

These points were not offered as a final defense or rejection of rental-pricing algorithms. They illustrated why the case requires analysis of goals, power, system structure, and consequences rather than a binary moral classification.

## Power in Rental-Pricing Systems

Power is visible in the asymmetry between renters and landlords. Renters may have to surrender personal data to obtain housing, while landlords control access to pricing tools and use the resulting recommendations.

The lecture encouraged a broader inventory of relevant actors:

- Renters provide data, pay recommended prices, and experience the housing market.
- Landlords and property-management companies control units and decide whether to use pricing software.
- Software firms build algorithms and determine the services offered to property owners.
- Governments regulate housing, competition, data sharing, and algorithm use.
- Investigative journalists make otherwise obscure arrangements publicly visible.
- Other actors and market conditions may also contribute to rising rents.

Power analysis asks what kinds of power these actors hold and how the balance changes over time. Cities, states, national governments, companies, and public-interest actors all contend to shape the future of rental-pricing systems.

The question of who defines the optimization objective is itself a question of power. Control is exercised not only through individual rental decisions but also through setting the terms on which the entire decision process operates. Choosing maximum property-owner revenue rather than affordability or another value establishes the curve along which subsequent technical work proceeds.

The algorithm can become an “obligatory passage point” when people in a large housing market cannot realistically avoid datafication and algorithmically generated prices or evaluations. In that role, software becomes part of the environment that renters and landlords must navigate, structuring their relationship from the position of actors who control the data and system.

## Rental Pricing as a Sociotechnical System

A sociotechnical analysis treats the algorithm as only one component of a larger arrangement. The full system includes:

- It includes historical property and market data.
- It includes tenants and the information required from them.
- It includes landlords and property managers.
- It includes software companies and data scientists.
- It includes property-management platforms and related business practices.
- It includes market conditions, financial incentives, and investment.
- It includes journalists, regulators, courts, legislatures, and local governments.
- It includes the narratives and values through which the technology is interpreted.

This lens complicates explanations that attribute outcomes solely to a piece of software. Rental-pricing algorithms enter an already data-intensive market with existing shortages, affordability problems, ownership structures, and revenue goals. At the same time, their recommendations can reorganize how market participants act and how information passes among them.

The case therefore shows a back-and-forth relationship. Social actors decide what data to collect, what goals to optimize, and how systems may be used. Once implemented, the systems reshape practices, relationships, and available decisions. Technology and society are formed through this interface.

## Identity and Positionality

Identity and positionality affect how people encounter rental markets and how they interpret rental-pricing systems. Students in the classroom were likely to have experience primarily as renters, but other positions include belonging to a family that owns one or more rental properties, working for a rental company, becoming a landlord, or participating in housing activism.

The instructor explicitly identified her own position as a homeowner who was not currently in the rental market and whose recent involvement occurred through helping a child rent a property. That position may reveal different features of the system than those visible to a current renter or rental activist.

The lens also directs attention to unequal access:

- Wealth and income affect whether someone can become a property owner.
- Social position affects whether someone encounters the system as a renter, landlord, guarantor, or activist.
- Education and professional access affect who can become a data scientist and participate in designing the algorithms.
- Different positions expose people to different benefits, burdens, and forms of knowledge.

Positionality does not merely supply different opinions about the same object. It can determine which parts of a system are visible and which consequences are experienced directly.

## Narratives Shaping the Rental-Algorithm Debate

Narratives connect accounts of the past to expectations about the future. They can remain implicit, so the analytical task is to identify them, make them visible, and examine how they guide interpretation.

The lecture offered several competing narratives:

- One narrative treats the algorithm as merely a new tool. Landlords have always needed ways to price property, so the software is interpreted as a continuation of an existing practice rather than a fundamental break.
- Another narrative treats pricing algorithms as inevitable and cutting-edge. Technological development appears unstoppable, making resistance seem futile.
- A third narrative presents the system as another mechanism through which landlords exploit renters. This story connects the algorithm to a longer history of unequal rental relations and supports regulation or prohibition.
- Another narrative claims that algorithmic rent gouging contributes to housing unaffordability and shortages.
- A competing account argues that unaffordability and shortages predated the algorithms and that other causes may be more important.
- A broader structural narrative treats pricing algorithms as downstream effects of private equity, financialization, and the pursuit of returns in real estate.

These narratives place causal force in different locations. Some center the technology, some center landlord-renter relations, and others center broader economic changes. Making the narratives explicit allows people to question which causal story they are carrying and what kinds of action follow from it.

The lecture also asked which familiar narratives are absent. Although privacy is often treated as the central problem in a datafied world, the public dispute over rental-pricing algorithms was not primarily framed as a privacy controversy. Instead, the dominant narratives concerned competition, collusion, affordability, exploitation, and market structure.

## Technological Determinism, Social Determinism, and Sociotechnical Formation

A technological-determinist narrative treats advances in data, algorithms, and computing as the main force driving social outcomes. As technology becomes stronger, resistance appears increasingly difficult, and people are left primarily with the task of mitigating its effects.

An opposing deterministic narrative treats outcomes as entirely social and views technology as only the form through which preexisting social relationships are expressed.

The lecture presented sociotechnical analysis as a way to avoid reducing the case to either side. Rental-pricing systems emerge through interaction among technical capacities and social choices. People and institutions decide who builds an algorithm, which data it uses, and what it optimizes. Those choices can then be contested and altered by people with other positions and values.

The rental case therefore demonstrates how society shapes what technology is able and authorized to do while technology restructures social practices and relationships. The datafied world is made at this interface rather than by technology or society acting alone.

---

## From Analysis to Possible Action

The HCE tools are intended to produce sharper guides for action by showing where intervention is possible. In the rental case, potential intervention points include the data shared across landlords, the goal used for optimization, landlord use of recommendations, software-company practices, market regulation, and the narratives through which problems are defined.

Different diagnoses produce different responses. If the main problem is understood as cross-company data sharing, a policy may permit algorithms using internal data while restricting pooled information. If the system is interpreted as an instrument of landlord exploitation, stronger bans may appear relevant. If it is understood as a downstream effect of financialization or housing scarcity, attention may shift away from the algorithm alone.

The lecture did not prescribe one response. Its argument was that shaping the future of rental pricing requires understanding the actual distribution of power, the complete sociotechnical system, the positions from which people experience it, and the narratives that assign causal importance.
