# The State of Undergraduate Learning (pp. 756–762)

## Executive Summary

Josipa Roksa and Richard Arum investigate the fundamental question of how much undergraduate students actually learn during their postsecondary education. While higher education faculty almost universally agree that developing critical thinking, analytical reasoning, and effective writing skills is an essential purpose of undergraduate education, empirical assessment reveals a stark gap between institutional aspirations and actual student cognitive growth. Drawing on a landmark longitudinal study conducted by the Social Science Research Council (SSRC) and published in _Academically Adrift_, the authors track over 2,300 traditional-age undergraduates across 24 representative four-year colleges and universities from their entry in fall 2005 through the end of their sophomore year in spring 2007. Using the Collegiate Learning Assessment (CLA) performance task, the authors measure individual changes in core cognitive capacities over time.

The empirical findings present a sobering assessment of higher education efficacy: over their first two years of college, undergraduates demonstrate an average gain of only 0.18 standard deviations on the CLA, which translates to a modest 7 percentile point increase. Strikingly, 45 percent of all students in the sample show no statistically significant improvement in critical thinking, analytical reasoning, or writing skills after two full years of postsecondary enrollment. Roksa and Arum demonstrate that this limited cognitive growth is directly tied to low levels of academic effort and a widespread lack of course rigor. On average, sophomores spend only 12 hours per week studying, with more than 40 percent studying less than 10 hours weekly. Furthermore, a large proportion of undergraduates encounter minimal course requirements, with half taking no courses in a semester that require more than 20 pages of writing, and a quarter taking courses that require neither substantial writing nor reading.

The authors argue that these widespread patterns of limited learning are produced by institutional disincentives and organizational structures within contemporary higher education. Faculty members and students frequently operate under what George Kuh terms a "disengagement compact," an unspoken arrangement in which professors lower academic demands in exchange for minimal student complaints and favorable course evaluations. This compact is reinforced by university reward systems that prioritize faculty research productivity over instructional quality for tenure and promotion, a lack of pedagogical training in doctoral programs, federal funding allocations that overwhelmingly favor research over teaching, and popular college ranking systems that evaluate institutions based on admissions selectivity rather than student learning outcomes. Ultimately, Roksa and Arum call for a fundamental institutional recommitment to rigorous undergraduate education and academic challenge.

## Assessment and the Measurement of Collegiate Learning

### Faculty Consensus vs. National Critiques

- **Universal Agreement on Core Learning Goals**: Faculty members across higher education institutions rarely reach consensus on the moral, social, or civic purposes of higher education, yet they overwhelmingly agree on the cognitive goals of college. Surveys conducted by the Higher Education Research Institute reveal that virtually all college faculty view the development of students' critical thinking and written communication skills as essential objectives of an undergraduate education.
- **The Spellings Commission Critique**: Despite faculty consensus surrounding these goals, national evaluations have increasingly challenged the quality of postsecondary instruction. The Secretary of Education's Commission on the Future of Higher Education (the Spellings Commission) cited data from the National Assessment of Adult Literacy to assert that student learning at U.S. colleges is inadequate and declining, highlighting the urgent necessity for standardized, objective measures of collegiate learning outcomes.

### The Collegiate Learning Assessment (CLA) Methodology

- **Measuring General Cognitive Capacities**: To evaluate student learning objectively, researchers rely on instruments such as the Collegiate Learning Assessment (CLA), which focuses on assessing broad, higher-order cognitive skills—specifically critical thinking, analytical reasoning, and written communication—rather than subject-specific content knowledge.
- **The Realistic Structure of Performance Tasks**: The most innovative component of the CLA is its computer-administered performance task. Students are allotted 90 minutes to respond to an open-ended writing prompt based on a realistic scenario, requiring them to analyze, synthesize, and evaluate a variety of background documents, such as technical reports, newspaper articles, memoranda, and data tables, to resolve a complex problem.

### The SSRC Longitudinal Research Design

- **Nationally Representative Sample**: The SSRC study tracked 2,300 traditional-age, full-time undergraduates across 24 four-year institutions beginning in fall 2005. The participating colleges and universities represented the national distribution of four-year postsecondary institutions in terms of institutional selectivity, academic preparation (SAT/ACT averages), gender, and racial/ethnic diversity.
- **Longitudinal Cohort Tracking**: Students completed the CLA at two distinct developmental points: upon initial entry into higher education in fall 2005, and again at the conclusion of their sophomore year in spring 2007. This longitudinal tracking design enabled researchers to measure individual-level cognitive growth ("value-added" learning) across the first half of the undergraduate career.

## Empirical Findings on Limited Cognitive Growth

### Minimal Overall Score Gains

- **Modest Standard Deviation Improvements**: Analysis of CLA scores reveals that undergraduates achieve very small average learning gains during their first two years of college. On average, students improved their performance by only 0.18 standard deviations between the start of their freshman year and the end of their sophomore year.
- **Translating Gains to Percentiles**: Expressed as percentile ranks, a 0.18 standard deviation improvement corresponds to a modest 7 percentile point gain. This indicates that an average freshman entering college at the 50th percentile of academic performance would perform at a level equivalent to only the 57th percentile of the incoming freshman class after completing two full years of higher education.

### The Forty-Five Percent Threshold

- **Absence of Measurable Improvement**: To provide an intuitive understanding of cognitive progress, Roksa and Arum evaluate the proportion of individual students who fail to demonstrate statistically significant growth. Their analysis reveals that 45 percent of all undergraduates show no measurable gains in critical thinking, analytical reasoning, or writing skills over their first two years of college.
- **Substantial Sub-Group Variation**: Although overall cognitive growth across higher education is remarkably low, individual student outcomes vary widely depending on academic preparation, student behaviors, coursework requirements, and campus environments.

## Time Investment and the Dynamics of Study Behaviors

### Low Total Academic Time Commitments

- **Meager Study Hours**: Student self-reports indicate that undergraduates invest very little time in academic preparation. During their sophomore year, students spent an average of only 12 hours per week studying outside of class. More than 40 percent of undergraduates prepared for their courses less than 10 hours per week, and nearly 40 percent dedicated less than 5 hours per week to studying alone.
- **The Minority Commitment to Academic Rigor**: Less than 20 percent of sophomores reported studying at least 20 hours per week, and fewer than 10 percent spent 20 hours or more studying alone—a level of academic preparation that was considered modest or average in previous decades.
- **Overall Time Allocation**: When combining out-of-class study time (12 hours) with classroom and laboratory instruction (15 hours), the average undergraduate spends less than 27 hours per week on academic pursuits, representing less than 16 percent of a standard seven-day week.

### Solitary vs. Peer Studying Paradox

- **Positive Returns on Solitary Study**: Controlling for academic preparation (SAT/ACT scores, high school GPAs, AP courses) and institutional characteristics, Roksa and Arum find a consistent, positive relationship between solitary study time and CLA performance gains. The more hours a student spends studying alone, the greater their cognitive improvement.
- **Negative Returns on Unguided Peer Study**: In contrast, time spent studying with peers shows a statistically significant _negative_ association with CLA learning gains. Unstructured peer study groups frequently degenerate into social interaction, reducing cognitive focus and diminishing academic gains.
- **Reconciling Educational Literature**: This finding appears to contradict traditional higher education literature (e.g., Alexander Astin, Vincent Tinto, and George Kuh) advocating active and collaborative learning. Roksa and Arum clarify that collaborative learning is beneficial only when structured within formal, guided programs—such as Uri Treisman’s Emerging Scholars model in mathematics or structured learning communities. Unguided peer study outside the classroom yields no cognitive benefits.

## Course Expectations, Academic Rigor, and Student Outcomes

### Prevalence of Low Course Demands

- **Minimal Writing Requirements**: Fifty percent of all undergraduates reported that during the prior semester, they did not take a single course that required more than 20 pages of cumulative writing.
- **Minimal Reading Requirements**: One-third of undergraduates reported taking no courses in the prior semester that required an average of even 40 pages of reading per week.
- **The Low-Rigor Quarter**: Combining these measures, 25 percent of undergraduates took courses that featured _neither_ requirement (>20 pages of writing or >40 pages of reading weekly). Only 43 percent of students experienced _both_ demands simultaneously during a semester.

### Synergies of Combined Academic Press

- **Cognitive Gains Through Dual Demands**: Significant improvements on the CLA occur only when students take courses that combine both substantial reading (>40 pages/week) and writing (>20 pages/semester) requirements. Neither reading nor writing demands in isolation are sufficient to generate meaningful cognitive growth.
- **Spurring Student Effort**: High course requirements actively alter student behavior. Students enrolled in courses featuring combined reading and writing demands report spending two additional hours per week studying compared to peers without such requirements.
- **The Power of High Expectations**: Aligning with classical sociological models of educational attainment, students who report that their professors hold high expectations demonstrate significantly greater gains in critical thinking, analytical reasoning, and writing skills.

## Systemic Incentives and the "Disengagement Compact"

### The Disengagement Compact

- **Mutual Non-Interference**: Roksa and Arum argue that the lack of academic rigor stems from an institutional "disengagement compact" between faculty and students. To protect their time for research or personal pursuits, professors demand relatively little from students; in return, students offer high course evaluations and refrain from complaining about workloads.

### Misaligned Institutional Reward Structures

- **Tenure Rewards Research Over Teaching**: At four-year colleges and universities, institutional tenure and promotion criteria heavily reward published research productivity while offering negligible rewards for instructional excellence or rigorous grading.
- **Neglect of Pedagogical Training**: Graduate doctoral programs train future professors almost exclusively as specialized researchers, neglecting instructional preparation. Surveys indicate that only half of doctoral students receive formal teaching assistant training or discipline-specific pedagogical workshops.
- **Federal Funding and Institutional Ranking Distortions**: Federal research and development funding vastly outweighs federal grants aimed at improving postsecondary teaching. Furthermore, dominant college ranking systems (such as _U.S. News & World Report_) measure institutional quality based on input selectivity (who enrolls) rather than value-added cognitive development (what students learn).

## Key Theoretical Definitions

- **Collegiate Learning Assessment (CLA)**: A standardized, computer-administered assessment tool that uses open-ended performance tasks and analytical writing prompts to evaluate undergraduates' general cognitive capacities, specifically critical thinking, analytical reasoning, and written communication.
- **Academically Adrift**: The landmark empirical study authored by Richard Arum and Josipa Roksa documenting limited learning and minimal cognitive growth among American college undergraduates.
- **Disengagement Compact**: An informal organizational agreement prevalent on college campuses wherein faculty members require modest academic work from students in exchange for positive student evaluations and minimal conflict, allowing faculty to focus on research.
- **Academic Press / Academic Challenge**: An institutional environment characterized by rigorous course requirements, high faculty expectations, and structural demands that compel students to dedicate substantial time to solitary study and cognitive analysis.
- **Performance Task**: The primary component of the CLA that presents students with a 90-minute real-world problem-solving scenario, requiring them to synthesize diverse background documents and compose an analytical written response.
- **Time on Task**: A foundational educational framework asserting that student learning gains are directly proportional to the volume of time spent actively engaged in solitary academic study and preparation.

## Summary

Josipa Roksa and Richard Arum's "The State of Undergraduate Learning" demonstrates that higher education in the United States is failing to deliver meaningful cognitive growth for a vast segment of its student population. Tracking over 2,300 undergraduates across 24 four-year institutions, their research reveals that 45 percent of students show no statistically significant improvement in critical thinking, analytical reasoning, or writing skills over their first two years of college. Average gains on the Collegiate Learning Assessment are meager, reflecting an overall postsecondary system that prioritizes social engagement and credential acquisition over intellectual development.

The empirical evidence identifies low academic expectations and minimal coursework requirements as primary drivers of limited learning. Undergraduates spend an average of only 12 hours per week studying, while large proportions encounter courses that require minimal reading and writing. Crucially, the authors show that cognitive growth requires "academic press"—specifically, taking courses that combine demanding reading (>40 pages/week) and writing (>20 pages/semester) requirements, spending time studying alone, and encountering high faculty expectations.

Ultimately, Roksa and Arum demonstrate that limited learning is an institutional product of structural disincentives. Driven by the "disengagement compact," tenure systems that reward research over teaching, doctoral programs that neglect pedagogical training, and ranking systems that reward input selectivity rather than learning outcomes, higher education institutions have drifted away from their primary educational mission. Reversing this trend requires federal, state, and institutional leaders to realign incentives, increase academic rigor, and recommit higher education to cultivating student learning.
