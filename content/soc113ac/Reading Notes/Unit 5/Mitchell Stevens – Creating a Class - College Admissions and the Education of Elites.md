# Creating a Class: College Admissions and the Education of Elites (pp. 686–693)

## Executive Summary

Mitchell Stevens provides an inside ethnographic account of the admissions office at a highly selective private liberal arts college ("The College"), detailing how elite postsecondary institutions assemble an incoming freshman class. Set against the backdrop of the 2003 United States Supreme Court rulings in _Gratz v. Bollinger_ and _Grutter v. Bollinger_—which struck down mechanical point-based affirmative action systems while endorsing "individualized consideration"—Stevens investigates the actual organizational mechanics of holistic admissions evaluations. He demonstrates that while holistic review is publicly celebrated as a fair and humane method for assessing individual merit, its reliance on qualitative, narrative-driven assessments inadvertently constructs subtle yet powerful forms of social class bias that systematically favor affluent applicants.

To manage thousands of applications annually, admissions officers organize their work through a three-stage evaluative framework: **reading and rating**, **evaluative storytelling**, and **class crafting**. The initial reading and rating phase operates as a "coarse sort," converting transcripts, test scores, and extracurricular records into quantitative ratings and summary "pink sheets" to separate obvious acceptances and rejections from a large middle pool of borderline candidates. In the second stage, officers engage in collaborative "evaluative storytelling" during committee meetings, constructing compelling narrative portraits ("holograms") of borderline applicants to make fine distinctions and justify making admissions exceptions. Finally, during "class crafting," administrators aggregate these individual decisions to satisfy broader institutional imperatives, balancing tuition revenue targets against financial aid budgets through high-stakes "F-rounds" (financial aid rounds) while fulfilling commitments to coaches, development officers, and campus leadership.

Stevens' central theoretical insight is that "individualized consideration" is not an objective, isolated judgment of inherent talent, but a collaborative narrative construction that requires a rich supply of "narrative raw materials". Socioeconomically privileged applicants possess vast structural advantages in this evaluative economy because their families and elite prep schools furnish abundant information—such as elaborate letters of recommendation, specialized course offerings, and direct advocacy from guidance counselors who act as influential information brokers. Conversely, working-class applicants with standard credentials remain unembellished "types" who lack the requisite narrative currency to inspire administrative "stretching". Consequently, holistic admissions practices naturalize class privilege under the guise of individualized merit, preserving the elite character of selective higher education in America.

## Legal Context and the Imperative of "Individualized Consideration"

### Judicial Mandates and Institutional Logic

- **The 2003 Supreme Court Benchmarks**: In _Gratz v. Bollinger_ and _Grutter v. Bollinger_ (2003), the U.S. Supreme Court established the constitutional boundaries for race-conscious college admissions. While _Gratz_ invalidated point-allocation systems that automatically awarded fixed numerical bonuses to minority candidates, _Grutter_ upheld affirmative action when conducted through "individualized consideration". Writing for the majority, Justice Sandra Day O'Connor specified that holistic review requires assessing each applicant as a whole person in a flexible, non-mechanical manner rather than relying on quota systems or rigid numerical cutoffs.
- **Institutional Adoption of Holistic Review**: The Supreme Court's endorsement elevated "individualized consideration" into the gold standard of selective higher education admissions. Selective colleges embraced holistic evaluation because it provided a legally defensible mechanism for pursuing student diversity while preserving institutional discretion to evaluate candidates across multiple qualitative dimensions.

### Public Myths vs. Sociological Realities

- **The Myth of Autonomous Individual Evaluation**: Popular depictions and admissions rhetoric frame committee deliberations as pure, isolated evaluations of personal character and academic promise. Journalists and admissions officers frequently overplay committee meetings as the singular, decisive moment where an applicant's intrinsic worth is adjudicated.
- **The Sociological Reality of Institutional Constraint**: Stevens counters that individual evaluation does not occur in an organizational vacuum. Admissions decisions are constrained by years of preparatory evaluations, structural resource limits, and complex organizational trade-offs. Far from being autonomous judges, admissions officers operate as negotiators balancing competing internal interests—such as athletic recruitment, fundraising targets, and tuition income demands—while assessing individual files.

## The Three-Stage Process of Admissions Evaluation

Admissions officers process thousands of applications through three distinct organizational stages, alternating between coarse quantitative categorizations and fine qualitative distinctions.

### Stage 1: Reading and Rating (Coarse Sorts)

- **Quantitative Worksheets and Summary Ratings**: In the initial evaluation stage, officers read applications individually and complete two standardized forms: the "Applicant Rating" worksheet and a two-sided "pink sheet". The worksheet converts applicant data into a composite 9-point numerical scale (1 = low; 9 = high) known as the "fin rating" (financial aid rating).
- **Component Calculations**: The 9-point scale combines three major sub-scores:
    1. _Academic Rating (4 of 9 points)_: Calculated by averaging four subsidiary ratings—high school quality (measured by the percentage of graduates attending four-year colleges), curriculum rigor (number of Advanced Placement or honors courses), high school grades, and class rank.
    2. _Testing Score (3 of 9 points)_: Standardized SAT or ACT scores converted directly into the 1–9 metric.
    3. _Personal Rating (2 of 9 points)_: A qualitative summary of extracurricular achievements and personal accomplishments.
- **Pink Sheet Crib Notes**: Officers summarize crucial qualitative and contextual details on pink summary sheets, recording household information (parents' occupations and education), legacy status, athletic ratings, essay quality, teacher recommendations, and whether the student requested financial aid. Applicants requiring no financial aid are colloquially designated as "free" (full-pay students who generate revenue), whereas those needing aid are labeled "expensive" or "needy". This stage serves primarily as a "coarse sort," grouping applications into easy acceptances, easy denials, and a massive middle pool of borderline candidates.

### Stage 2: Evaluative Storytelling (Fine Distinctions)

- **Committee Deliberations and Hologram Construction**: During committee meetings (held from November through March), admissions officers transition from coarse quantitative sorting to making fine distinctions among borderline candidates in the middle pool. To advocate for a candidate, officers perform "evaluative storytelling"—stitching disparate attributes into a coherent, aesthetically compelling character portrait. Officers describe this process as building a multidimensional "hologram" of the applicant out of the raw paper file.
- **Justifying Administrative "Stretching"**: When borderline applicants possess mixed credentials (e.g., lower SAT scores but exceptional talent or compelling personal backgrounds), a strong narrative provides the justification for officers to "stretch" or make exceptions to admit them. Applicants who lack rich narrative details remain unembellished "types" who are easily categorized and rejected without debate.

### Stage 3: Class Crafting and "F-Rounds"

- **Aggregating the Cohort**: After individual committee decisions are made, evaluation shifts to "class crafting"—a macro-level assembly stage designed to ensure the incoming freshman class fulfills all organizational imperatives.
- **Financial Aid "F-Rounds"**: In secret, fast-paced meetings known as "F-rounds" (financial aid rounds), admissions directors review the total cost of the admitted pool against the institution's strict financial aid budget. If the admitted cohort exceeds the budget, previously accepted applicants who require significant financial aid are systematically changed from "admit" to "deny" (a process colloquially called being "F-d") and replaced with full-paying ("free") candidates.
- **Reverting Stories Back to Numbers**: To minimize the emotional strain of rescinding acceptances during F-rounds, officers deliberately strip away personal narratives and treat applicants strictly as raw numbers (financial cost and SAT scores).

## Information as Currency and the Reproduction of Class Privilege

### The Evaluative Economy and Guidance Counselor Brokers

- **Information as Currency**: Stevens conceptualizes admissions evaluation as an "evaluative economy" in which information functions as the primary currency used to buy influence, build credibility, and direct decision outcomes.
- **Guidance Counselors as Institutional Brokers**: Elite private boarding schools and affluent suburban public high schools employ dedicated guidance counselors who maintain long-standing, reciprocal relationships with college admissions officers. These counselors act as elite information brokers, placing direct phone calls to admissions officers, providing insider context, and actively lobbying for specific candidates.

### Narrative Raw Materials and Class Disadvantages

- **Unequal Distribution of Narrative Materials**: Evaluative storytelling requires narrative "raw materials"—detailed teacher recommendations, polished personal essays, specialized artistic or athletic portfolios, and counselor context. Privileged applicants arrive with files overflowing with these materials because their families have the resources to purchase expensive schooling, private tutoring, travel, and extracurricular experiences.
- **Structural Disadvantage for Working-Class Applicants**: Working-class and low-income students attending under-resourced public high schools often have brief, generic recommendation letters from overworked counselors who manage hundreds of students. Lacking rich narrative raw materials, their applications appear as sparse, uninspiring "types". Consequently, holistic "individualized consideration" systematically disadvantages poorer applicants, not through explicit animus, but because the machinery of narrative production is heavily skewed by social class.

### Intramural Stakeholders and the "Rule of One Pick"

- **Satisfying Internal Constituencies**: Admissions officers must navigate competing demands from influential internal stakeholders. Athletics departments demand roster spots for recruited athletes, Development offices advocate for wealthy donors' children, the President's office seeks specific institutional connections, and Alumni offices push for legacy admissions. Applicants possessing assets valued by these stakeholders have powerful insider advocates that ordinary applicants lack.
- **The "Rule of One Pick"**: To alleviate administrative fatigue and grant staff personal autonomy, the admissions director enforces an internal tradition known as the "Rule of One Pick". Each junior admissions officer is granted total personal discretion to admit one single applicant of their choice during the cycle, completely suspending standard institutional constraints and stakeholder trade-offs.

## Key Theoretical Definitions

- **Individualized Consideration**: The holistic evaluation model endorsed by the U.S. Supreme Court in _Grutter v. Bollinger_ (2003) that requires postsecondary admissions officers to assess applicants as whole individuals across qualitative and quantitative dimensions rather than relying on mechanical point systems or rigid quotas.
- **Coarse Sorts vs. Fine Distinctions**: The dual organizational mechanisms used by evaluators; _coarse sorts_ use standardized quantitative metrics to rapidly categorize applicants into broad bands, whereas _fine distinctions_ rely on qualitative narratives to discriminate among highly qualified or borderline candidates.
- **Evaluative Storytelling**: The collaborative process through which admissions officers synthesize disparate application details into compelling narrative composites ("characters with names" or "holograms") to persuade committee members to admit borderline applicants.
- **Class Crafting**: The final aggregate stage of admissions evaluation in which officers manipulate the composition of the admitted cohort to satisfy multiple institutional goals, including tuition income targets, financial aid budget limits, SAT averages, and athletic or development preferences.
- **F-Rounds**: The decisive, closed-door "financial aid rounds" where admissions leaders review the financial cost of the admitted pool and replace "expensive" aid-requiring applicants with full-paying ("free") candidates to ensure the incoming class stays on budget.
- **Rule of One Pick**: An internal organizational policy in elite admissions offices granting each junior officer the absolute personal discretion to admit one applicant of their choice per cycle, free from standard institutional requirements or stakeholder negotiations.

## Summary

Mitchell Stevens' "Creating a Class" demonstrates that the process of elite college admissions is an organizational mechanism that actively constructs social class privilege while operating under the egalitarian banner of holistic meritocracy. Following the U.S. Supreme Court's mandate in _Grutter v. Bollinger_, selective universities rely heavily on "individualized consideration" to evaluate applicants. However, far from creating a neutral playing field, holistic evaluation forces admissions officers to rely on "evaluative storytelling" to make fine distinctions among borderline candidates, favoring applicants who can present compelling personal narratives.

Because compelling storytelling requires a continuous supply of narrative raw materials—such as specialized high school course profiles, rich recommendation letters, and direct counseling advocacy—the admissions process inherently advantages affluent families who can afford elite private prep schools and suburban feeder schools. In contrast, under-resourced public school applicants with standard credentials remain unembellished "types" who lack the narrative currency needed to inspire administrative advocacy. Furthermore, during the final "class crafting" stage and financial aid "F-rounds," institutional revenue requirements force colleges to prioritize full-paying students over financially needy ones to balance institutional budgets.

Ultimately, Stevens exposes the paradox of modern elite higher education: while holistic admissions practices were designed to ensure individualized fairness and institutional diversity, their structural reliance on resource-intensive narrative materials and tuition revenue targets causes elite colleges to reproduce socioeconomic inequality, naturalizing class privilege as individual "merit".
