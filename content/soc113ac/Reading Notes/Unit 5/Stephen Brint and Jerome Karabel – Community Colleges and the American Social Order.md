# Community Colleges and the American Social Order (pp. 650–659)

## Executive Summary

Stephen Brint and Jerome Karabel examine the historical evolution and institutional dynamics of two-year junior colleges (later termed community colleges) within the American higher education landscape. They argue that the remarkable growth of the community college system—expanding from under 10,000 students in 1920 to over four million by 1980—embodies both the egalitarian promise of American democracy and the structural imperatives of a stratified capitalist economy. While the ideology of equal opportunity encouraged massive enrollment expansion, two-year institutions were positioned at a critical nexus where democratic ambitions for upward mobility clashed with the limited absorptive capacity of the elite labor market.

To explain why community colleges transformed from primary baccalaureate-transfer institutions into centers predominantly focused on terminal vocational training, Brint and Karabel critique two prevailing sociological models: the liberal **consumer-choice model** (which attributes changes to student demand) and the Marxist **business-domination model** (which attributes changes to corporate dictates). They demonstrate that both models fail historically because, prior to 1970, student consumers overwhelmingly sought liberal arts degrees while business elites remained largely indifferent to community college curricula.

Instead, the authors advance an **institutional model** rooted in organizational analysis. They argue that community college administrators actively drove the vocationalization process as a strategic response to their **dual structural subordination** within both the higher education hierarchy and the labor market. Lacking the authority to grant bachelor's degrees and unable to compete with four-year universities for high-status "training markets," community college leaders engaged in **anticipatory subordination**. By voluntarily re-orienting their institutions toward terminal vocational programs, administrators secured a distinct, non-competitive market niche, guaranteed state funding, and forged an institutional identity, even as this shift effectively constrained the educational and occupational mobility of working-class students.

## Historical Expansion and Democratic Ideology

### Massive Growth and Organizational Innovation

- **Unprecedented Institutional Expansion**: The United States sends a higher proportion of its youth to postsecondary institutions than any other advanced industrial nation, establishing higher education as the primary ideological mechanism for legitimating equality of opportunity. Within this system, the two-year college represents the most successful organizational innovation of twentieth-century higher education, expanding from a single institution in 1901 to over 1,200 colleges by 1980, accounting for nearly 40 percent of all higher education institutions and enrolling over half of all college freshmen.
- **Creation of a Non-Degree-Granting Tier**: The emergence of the junior college introduced a fundamentally new tier into the higher education hierarchy. Unlike even the least prestigious four-year colleges, two-year institutions lacked the authority to confer the bachelor's degree—the traditional _sine qua non_ of an authentic college education—positioning them uniquely between secondary schooling and four-year universities.

### Strategic Position and Ideological Functions

- **Embodiment of Egalitarian Promise**: Community colleges were celebrated in public discourse and internal institutional mission statements as democratic "open-door" institutions designed to extend postsecondary access to lower-income, working-class, and non-traditional students who had historically been excluded from higher education.
- **Legitimation of Social Stratification**: By providing broad geographic and financial access, community colleges bolstered the national belief that upward social mobility was available to anyone willing to work for it. However, because occupational structures remained highly unequal, the expansion of postsecondary access threatened to generate more qualified claimants for elite jobs than the economy could absorb, making institutional mechanisms of sorting and diversion structural necessities.

## Contradictory Pressures and the Management of Ambition

### Democratic Inclusivity vs. Capitalist Selection

- **Poised Between Conflicting Demands**: Community colleges face permanent structural tension because they must simultaneously respond to democratic pressure from below (demands for expanded access and social mobility) and functional pressure from above (the economy's need to select, sort, and stratify workers).
- **Institutional Dilemma**: Institutions are torn between promoting social equality versus increasing economic efficiency, offering broad general education versus specialized technical training, and fostering student aspirations versus adjusting those aspirations to fit an unequal division of labor.

### Elite University Sponsorship and the "Cooling-Out" Function

- **University Leaders as Early Sponsors**: Early junior colleges were actively championed by administrators at elite universities (such as the University of Chicago and Stanford University). These leaders sought to establish two-year colleges to serve as institutional buffers, protecting four-year universities from the influx of "ill-prepared" high school graduates while preserving universities' focus on high-level scholarship and elite professional training.
- **The Cooling-Out Mechanism**: Drawing on Burton Clark's sociological framework, Brint and Karabel highlight that in a democratic society, a fundamental problem arises from the inconsistency between the cultural encouragement to achieve and the actual realities of limited occupational opportunities. Community colleges fulfill a critical social control function by "cooling out" ambitious working-class students—gradually redirecting those who experience structured academic failure away from baccalaureate ambitions and toward terminal vocational diplomas without causing overt civil unrest or delegitimizing the social order.

### The Vocational Solution

- **Differentiating the Curriculum**: Community college leaders adapted a strategy originally developed in secondary education: creating a distinct terminal vocational track.
- **Rechanneling Aspirations**: By framing vocational education as "practical" training tailored to real-world employment, administrators attempted to resolve the contradiction between democratic access and economic stratification. Vocationalism allowed the educational system to accept students into higher education while safely channeling their ambitions away from overcrowded managerial positions and into middle-level technical occupations.

## Critique of Existing Explanatory Models

Brint and Karabel evaluate the two primary theoretical models traditionally used to explain the shift from transfer-oriented liberal arts education to terminal vocational training in community colleges.

### The Consumer-Choice Model

- **Theoretical Premise**: Grounded in classical liberal economics, this model posits that higher education institutions operate in a free market, passively adjusting their program offerings to match the changing preferences and enrollment demands of student consumers.
- **Empirical Flaws**: The consumer-choice model fails to explain the history of community colleges prior to 1970. For over four decades (from 1930 to 1970), student consumers overwhelmingly preferred baccalaureate-transfer programs in the liberal arts and actively resisted terminal vocational tracks. Nevertheless, community college leaders persistently pushed for vocationalization throughout this entire period, demonstrating that institutional policy was driving student options rather than merely responding to student choice.

### The Business-Domination Model

- **Theoretical Premise**: Grounded in instrumental Marxist theory, this model asserts that the vocationalization of community colleges was directly engineered and imposed by corporate business elites seeking publicly funded training programs to supply docile, low-cost technical labor.
- **Empirical Flaws**: This model fails because corporate leaders and major business associations exhibited profound indifference toward community colleges for the first sixty years of their history. Business executives rarely lobbied for specific community college curricula, and active corporate involvement only emerged in the late 1970s—long after administrators had already successfully established vocationalism as the primary institutional mission.

### Dual Structural Subordination

- **Hierarchical Location**: Community colleges occupied a doubly subordinate position within American society: they were subordinate to four-year universities within the higher education field, and subordinate to corporate employers within the labor market.
- **Monopolization of Training Markets**: Higher education institutions compete for "training markets"—the recognized right to serve as the preferred pipeline for specific occupations. Before community colleges expanded, four-year universities and professional schools had already monopolized the most lucrative and prestigious training markets (law, medicine, high-level management). Two-year colleges could not successfully compete for these upper-tier markets.

### Structural Power of Business

- **Influence Without Intervention**: Although business leaders rarely intervened directly in community college governance, corporate capital exercised profound **structural power**. Because private employers control the distribution of jobs in a capitalist economy, community college leaders had to design programs that aligned with perceived corporate needs, constantly analyzing labor trends to ensure their graduates could secure employment.

### Anticipatory Subordination

- **Voluntary Strategic Adaption**: To resolve their precarious organizational standing, community college administrators engaged in **anticipatory subordination**. They voluntarily conceded upper-tier academic and professional training to four-year universities.
- **Capturing a Lower Market Niche**: In exchange for accepting this subordinate status, administrators aggressively cultivated an unexploited, lower-tier market niche: middle-level technical and semi-professional vocational training. This strategy granted two-year colleges a secure organizational identity, guaranteed state budget allocations, and eliminated direct competition with four-year universities, even though it institutionalized a terminal educational track for working-class students.

## Key Theoretical Definitions

- **Cooling-Out Function**: The institutional mechanism identified by Burton Clark through which democratic postsecondary institutions gradually disabuse low-achieving or disadvantaged students of unachievable baccalaureate and professional ambitions, redirecting them toward terminal vocational credentials without provoking overt conflict.
- **Training Markets**: The distinct occupational sectors and employment pathways for which postsecondary educational institutions compete to be the recognized, preferred credentialing agent.
- **Structural Power of Business**: The capacity of capitalist enterprise to shape institutional policies and educational curricula without direct political intervention or overt lobbying, stemming solely from its structural control over job creation and employment opportunities in the economy.
- **Anticipatory Subordination**: The organizational strategy whereby leaders of subordinate institutions voluntarily conform their policies and programs to the perceived requirements of more powerful institutions (such as universities and corporate employers) in order to secure a stable market niche and institutional survival.
- **Organizational Field**: A recognized area of institutional life composed of key suppliers, resource consumers, regulatory agencies, and competing organizations that produce similar services or products (e.g., the field of postsecondary education).

## Summary

Stephen Brint and Jerome Karabel's study demonstrates that the structural transformation of American community colleges into vocational centers was fundamentally an institutional project driven by administrative leaders navigating severe structural constraints. Situated between democratic expectations of universal postsecondary access and the rigid realities of a stratified capitalist labor market, community colleges faced a persistent paradox: their popular legitimacy rested on offering baccalaureate transfer pathways, yet their systemic function required sorting and limiting the number of students who successfully transferred to four-year universities.

By analyzing and refuting both the consumer-choice and business-domination models, the authors prove that community college leaders did not simply obey student preferences or corporate mandates. Rather, through anticipatory subordination, administrators voluntarily chose to vocationalize their institutions to capture an unexploited semi-professional training niche, thereby carving out a secure organizational space within higher education. While this institutional strategy ensured the organizational survival and rapid expansion of two-year colleges, it simultaneously solidified educational tracking at the postsecondary level, channeling working-class and minority students into terminal occupational paths and reinforcing broader structures of social inequality.
