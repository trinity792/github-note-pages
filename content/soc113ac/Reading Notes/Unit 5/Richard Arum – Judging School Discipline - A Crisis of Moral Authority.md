# Judging School Discipline: A Crisis of Moral Authority (pp. 660–685)

## Executive Summary

Richard Arum argues that modern educational research's preeminent pre-occupation with cognitive development and standardized test score performance has overshadowed a profound structural crisis in American public education: the systematic erosion of moral authority and the breakdown of effective school discipline. While public anxiety regarding school disorder is long-standing, the contemporary crisis stems not from an intrinsic decay of student morality, but from legal and institutional transformations in the external school environment. Drawing on a novel empirical dataset combining content-coded records of 1,204 appellate court discipline cases (1946–1992) with four nationally representative surveys of students, teachers, and administrators, Arum demonstrates how judicial court decisions fundamentally restructured the governance of student behavior in American public schools.

The catalyst for this institutional shift was the dramatic expansion of "adversarial legalism" during the "student rights contestation period" (1969–1975). Sponsored heavily by federal War on Poverty funding through the Office of Economic Opportunity (OEO) Legal Services Program, public interest legal centers provided elite advocacy lawyers with the resources required to challenge traditional school authority in appellate courts. This litigation movement secured expanded constitutional due process rights for students in landmark decisions such as _Tinker v. Des Moines_ (1969) and _Goss v. Lopez_ (1975). While intended to protect vulnerable youth from arbitrary administrative overreach, these legal victories had the unintended organizational consequence of crippling administrative discretion, instilling a pervasive fear of litigation among educators, and profaning the perceived "sacred" inviolability of school rules.

Building on Émile Durkheim’s sociology of moral education, Arum emphasizes that discipline is not merely a practical technique for securing classroom tranquility, but an indispensable instrument for internalizing social norms and molding character. When court decisions stripped school discipline of its perceived moral authority, disciplinary practices were rendered either too lenient or arbitrarily coercive, causing students to view school rules as illegitimate. Subsequent judicial shifts after 1975 toward greater deference to school administrators could not overturn this institutionalized legal entitlement mindset. Ultimately, the collapse of legitimate moral authority in public schools has disproportionately harmed at-risk urban youth, contributing directly to classroom disorder, educational failure, and the wider expansion of youth incarceration.

## The Crisis of Moral Authority in American Public Schooling

### Shift from Cognitive Metrics to Moral Socialization

- **The Overlooked Function of Education**: Contemporary educational research and policy focus almost exclusively on standardized test score performance, treating schools strictly as cognitive production sites while ignoring broader socialization functions. Arum contends that primary and secondary schools are fundamentally responsible for the moral socialization of youth, serving as the primary non-familial institution tasked with shaping individual dispositions, civic responsibility, and respect for societal rules.
- **Empirical Dimensions of School Disorder**: Public dissatisfaction with school discipline is grounded in severe empirical realities rather than manufactured panic. At the close of the twentieth century, approximately thirty homicides occurred annually on school grounds, 10 percent of all public school teachers were threatened with physical injury, and 4 percent were physically attacked each year. In urban schools, these rates were even higher—14 percent of teachers were threatened and 6 percent were physically attacked—while over 10 percent of high school males reported carrying weapons on campus and 34 percent of urban seniors reported street gang presence in their schools.

### Threats to Public School Institutional Legitimacy

- **Erosion of Popular and Financial Support**: Unresolved disorder and the failure to socialize youth strike directly at the institutional legitimacy of public education. Generalized public dissatisfaction with lax discipline encourages middle-class parents to withdraw their children in favor of private schools, erodes the popular support necessary for state funding allocations, and fuels political challenges favoring privatization and voucher systems.
- **Public Perception of Crime Linkages**: Public opinion surveys reveal a widespread conviction that public school disciplinary failures are directly implicated in broader societal problems. Over 73 percent of surveyed adults identified the poor quality and lack of discipline in public schools as a critical or very important cause of national crime rates and escalating adult incarceration.

## Legal Mobilization, Adversarial Legalism, and Historical Shifts in Court Climates

### The Student Rights Contestation Period (1969–1975)

- **Emergence of Adversarial Legalism**: Educational litigation experienced an unprecedented explosion beginning in the late 1960s, driven by legal mobilization strategies explicitly modeled on the successes of the Civil Rights Movement. Youth rights advocates embraced precedent-setting appellate litigation as a primary vehicle for achieving sweeping institutional and social reform.
- **Institutional Sponsorship via Federal Funding**: A critical and often overlooked driver of this legal movement was direct federal funding provided through the Office of Economic Opportunity (OEO) Legal Services Program under President Johnson's War on Poverty. OEO funded specialized law reform centers—most notably the Center for Law and Education at Harvard—granting idealistic elite lawyers the financial and organizational backing required to mount persistent appellate challenges against local school boards.
- **Pro-Student Judicial Orientations and Precedents**: Appellate courts during this period decided in favor of students in 44 percent of cases. In _Tinker v. Des Moines_ (1969), the Supreme Court ruled that students do not shed their constitutional rights to free expression at the schoolhouse gate when wearing anti-war armbands. In _Goss v. Lopez_ (1975), the Court established Fourteenth Amendment due process requirements for short-term suspensions. Lower appellate rulings, such as _Ladson v. Board of Education_ (1971) and _Jacobs v. Benedict_ (1973), explicitly prioritized individual student well-being and freedom over administrative authority, with judges asserting superior pedagogical expertise over local school officials.

### The Post-1975 Shift toward Judicial Deference (1976–1992)

- **Institutional Constraints on Litigation**: In 1975, federal legislation created the Legal Services Corporation, placing explicit mandates to curb appellate litigation aimed at social reform. While annual appellate discipline cases declined moderately from their peak (averaging 57 cases per year between 1976 and 1992), the substantive focus of cases shifted from political protest to drug possession (16%), violent acts (14%), weapons (6%), alcohol (6%), and general misbehavior (50%).
- **Re-emergence of Judicial Support for Authority**: Courts after 1975 returned to supporting school authorities in approximately two-thirds of decisions, replacing the earlier emphasis on individual student rights with a protective stance toward the broader student body. Rulings such as _Adams v. City of Dothan_ (1986), _In re Appeal to Huffer_ (1989), _Fishel v. Frederick County_ (1988), and _Lusk v. Triad_ (1990) reaffirmed administrative discretion to combat drug abuse and school violence, upholding strict expulsions for look-alike amphetamines and firearms.

## Institutional Mechanisms and Organizational Adaptations

### Erosion of Teacher Discretion and Administrative Authority

- **Risk Aversion and Defensive Management**: Confronted with hostile court climates, school administrators opted to reduce or eliminate aggressive disciplinary enforcement to avoid expensive, reputation-damaging litigation. Rather than defending rules, administrators adopted symbolic compliance or abandoned disciplinary practices altogether.
- **Teacher Disempowerment and Professional Anxiety**: Teachers increasingly reported feeling that the legal system functioned as a weapon used against them by students and parents rather than a support structure for maintaining order. Surveys indicated that 59 percent of teachers felt hampered by court decisions, prompting teacher training programs to emphasize legal liability insurance and contractual hold-harmless clauses over classroom management.

### Student Perceptions: The Curvilinear Dynamics of Strictness and Fairness

- **Institutional Finality of Eliminating Disciplinary Practices**: In states characterized by hostile court climates, public schools rapidly eliminated traditional sanctions such as corporal punishment. Once eliminated, taken-for-granted organizational assumptions made the reintroduction of these sanctions culturally "unthinkable," insulating schools from subsequent conservative judicial shifts.
- **Curvilinear Relationship Between Strictness and Fairness**: Analyzing student survey data, Arum identifies a non-linear relationship between strictness and perceived fairness. Schools with extremely lenient discipline were perceived by students as arbitrary and unfair. Moderate strictness produced the highest levels of perceived fairness and institutional legitimacy. However, excessively harsh or authoritarian regimes again caused perceived fairness to drop, proving that discipline loses efficacy when it is stripped of moral legitimacy.

## Theoretical Synthesis: Moral Authority, Socialization, and Life-Course Outcomes

### Durkheimian Moral Authority vs. Progressive Deweyan Frameworks

- **Durkheimian Moral Mechanics**: Durkheim posited that disciplinary punishment is essential not for coercive intimidation (the Hobbesian model), but because it publicly reasserts the inviolability of moral rules. When adversarial legalism demonstrated that school authority could be challenged and profaned in court, the sacred character of school rules collapsed, striking discipline at its source.
- **Synthesizing Liberal and Conservative Critiques**: Arum demonstrates that conservative calls for harsh punitive measures and Progressive/Deweyan warnings against authoritarian repression both overlook the central mechanism: moral authority. Discipline fails both when it is overly permissive (Hobbesian normlessness) and when it is rigidly punitive without perceived fairness (authoritarian repression). Discipline succeeds only when students internalize rules as legitimately binding.

### Disciplinary Climates and the School-to-Prison Pipeline

- **Impact on Socialization and Achievement**: Schools maintaining disciplinary climates perceived as both fair and moderately strict foster higher academic achievement, higher test scores, and lower rates of delinquency. Conversely, chaotic or unfairly strict disciplinary environments lead to increased rule disobedience, classroom disruption, and adolescent arrests.
- **The School-to-Prison Pipeline for At-Risk Youth**: As societal shifts and dual-earner working schedules have reduced adult supervision in the home, schools have been forced to accept greater responsibility for youth socialization while simultaneously being stripped of moral authority. In urban public schools lacking vocational pathways, the breakdown of school discipline translates directly into a transition from school failure to adult incarceration, feeding the national prison expansion that saw inmate populations triple between 1980 and the mid-1990s.

## Summary

Richard Arum's analysis demonstrates that the contemporary crisis in American public school discipline is primarily an institutional product of legal intervention rather than a reflection of changing youth morality. Through the rise of adversarial legalism during the student rights contestation period (1969–1975), federally funded public interest litigation succeeded in applying constitutional due process mandates to school disciplinary procedures. While designed to protect individual student rights, these legal interventions inadvertently dismantled the traditional moral authority of public schools, making administrators risk-averse, disempowering classroom teachers, and teaching students to view school rules as contestable and non-binding.

Applying Émile Durkheim’s theoretical framework, Arum proves that school discipline cannot function effectively through either raw coercive force or complete permissiveness. Discipline requires legitimate moral authority—a public belief in the inviolability of institutional rules—so that youth internalize behavioral standards. When judicial decisions undermined this moral authority, public schools lost their capacity to socialize students effectively, resulting in heightened classroom disorder, diminished academic performance, and severe public skepticism regarding the viability of public education.

The long-term consequences of this institutional breakdown have fallen most heavily on vulnerable and at-risk students. In urban public school settings where non-academic and vocational options have been diminished, chaotic disciplinary climates fail to provide the moral structure necessary for successful transition into the adult labor force. Consequently, the erosion of moral authority in public schooling has directly contributed to higher dropout rates, elevated risk of adolescent arrest, and the rapid expansion of the American correctional system over recent decades.
