# Learning to Be Illegal: Undocumented Youth and Shifting Legal Contexts in the Transition to Adulthood (pp. 709–727)

## Executive Summary

Roberto G. Gonzales examines how public schooling and federal immigration law collide to transform the lived experiences and legal meanings of unauthorized status for undocumented immigrant youth as they age into adulthood. Over the past quarter-century, the undocumented population in the United States grew from 2.5 million in 1987 to over 11 million, producing a growing cohort of more than 2.1 million undocumented young people who arrived in the country as children. Known as the "1.5 generation," these youth spend their formative years integrated into American public schools under the protective umbrella of landmark legal rulings. However, as they cross the threshold into late adolescence and young adulthood, this institutional protection evaporates, forcing them through a disorienting "transition to illegality" that fundamentally alters their identity, social networks, and educational and occupational trajectories.

Grounded in four and a half years of ethnographic fieldwork and 150 semi-structured interviews with 1.5-generation Mexican-origin young adults in the Los Angeles metropolitan area, Gonzales constructs a developmental framework tracing three key life-course stages: **discovery** (ages 16–18), **learning to be illegal** (ages 18–24), and **coping** (ages 25–29). He compares the pathways of "college-goers" (those who matriculate into postsecondary institutions) with "early-exiters" (those who exit the education system at or before high school graduation) to identify the mechanisms that mediate legal exclusion during coming-of-age transitions.

The central theoretical insight of Gonzales's study is that unauthorized legal status operates as a master status that systematically breaks the traditional sociological link between educational attainment and occupational mobility. During childhood, public K–12 schools foster a state of "suspended illegality," encouraging undocumented youth to internalize American achievement norms and hold high aspirations. However, upon entering the adult labor market, both college-educated youth and early school-leavers face severe legal restrictions against formal employment, driving both groups into similar low-wage, clandestine labor markets by their late twenties. Consequently, state immigration policy functions as a primary stratification mechanism that converts educated, U.S.-raised youth into a disenfranchised underclass.

## Institutional Collision: Public Schooling vs. Immigration Law

### "Suspended Illegality" in K–12 Education

- **Public Schooling as an Inclusionary Space**: Under the landmark U.S. Supreme Court decision _Plyler v. Doe_ (1982), undocumented children are guaranteed constitutional access to public elementary and secondary education, while the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA) prohibits schools from sharing student records with immigration enforcement. These policies transform K–12 public schools into protective institutional incubators where legal status remains largely dormant and inconsequential to daily life.
- **Assimilation and Identity Formation**: Within public schools, undocumented children experience a "unity of experiences" alongside U.S.-born peers, acquiring English fluency, participating in extracurricular activities, developing social contacts, and adopting mainstream American cultural expectations and mobility aspirations.
- **The Buffer State**: Gonzales conceptualizes childhood and early adolescence as a stage of "suspended illegality". Because young people are legally integrated into schools and sheltered from adult legal requirements, unauthorized residency status has virtually no direct negative impact on their daily routines, creating a false sense of security and belonging.

### Post-K–12 Legal Exclusion and Liminal Legality

- **Abrupt Institutional Shift**: Upon turning 18 or exiting secondary school, undocumented youth lose their institutional protection and enter an adult legal environment characterized by explicit exclusion. They are legally barred from obtaining formal employment, acquiring driver's licenses in most states, voting, accessing federal financial aid, or traveling abroad, while facing the constant threat of deportation.
- **Liminal Legality and Abject Status**: Drawing on Cecilia Menjívar's concept of _liminal legality_ and Susan Coutin's work on migrant illegality, Gonzales emphasizes that undocumented youth inhabit a contradictory gray zone between physical/social presence and legal non-existence. They are culturally and socially American, yet legally designated as "illegal aliens," creating a state of prolonged developmental limbo.

## The Three-Stage Transition to Illegality

Gonzales outlines a three-stage developmental process through which 1.5-generation youth experience and adapt to their unauthorized legal status.

### Stage 1: Discovery (Ages 16–18)

- **Turning Points and Institutional Milestones**: Around age 16, undocumented youth attempt to participate in standard adolescent rites of passage—such as getting a first part-time job, learning to drive, applying for college, or obtaining a state ID—all of which require a Social Security number. The inability to provide this document acts as a jarring turning point that "knifes off" their previous inclusionary trajectory.
- **Divergent Discovery Pathways**:
    - _Early-Exiters_: Over 68 percent of early-exiters discover their illegal status while attempting to secure employment or a driver's license. Many lack strong relationships with school personnel and report feeling cut off from guidance counselors.
    - _College-Goers_: Nearly 60 percent of college-goers discover their status during the college application or financial aid process when confronted with federal eligibility requirements.
- **Emotional Shock and Stigmatized Identity**: Discovery triggers intense feelings of anger, confusion, betrayal, and despair, as youth realize their childhood assumptions of inclusion were "a big lie". To protect themselves from deportation and peer rejection, youth adopt secrecy and lying as daily coping mechanisms, cutting themselves off from supportive peer networks and experiencing "psychological and legal stunted growth".

### Stage 2: Learning to Be Illegal (Ages 18–24)

- **Labor Market Entry and Exploitation**: Early-exiters enter the low-wage, informal labor market out of immediate household financial need, contributing an average of $300 per month to their families. Lacking formal work experience and legal protections, they face workplace exploitation, low pay, unsafe conditions, and high job turnover, competing directly with older immigrant workers who have far less education.
- **Postsecondary Stalls and Detours**: College-goers who matriculate into postsecondary institutions (predominantly community colleges due to lower costs) face severe financial barriers, state/federal financial aid bans, and long commutes. Many experience discontinuous educational paths characterized by frequent leaves of absence, part-time enrollment, and heavy work schedules to pay for tuition.
- **The Critical Role of Extrafamilial Mentors**: Successful college navigation depends heavily on "really significant others"—non-kin adult mentors, such as high school teachers, college professors, or community advocates. These mentors provide crucial information, social capital, and occasional private financial sponsorship, helping undocumented youth delay entry into legally restricted adult environments.

### Stage 3: Coping (Ages 25–29)

- **Flattening of Aspirations**: By their mid-twenties, prolonged legal exclusion forces undocumented young adults to accept that their precarious status may be permanent. They undergo a cognitive process of "putting a lid on hope," adjusting their career expectations down to match their legal realities.
- **Occupational Convergence**: A striking empirical finding of Gonzales's research is the ultimate occupational convergence between college-goers and early-exiters. Regardless of whether an individual earned a bachelor's degree or dropped out of high school, the lack of a Social Security number restricts both groups to the same clandestine, low-wage jobs—such as cleaning houses, working in restaurant kitchens, or laboring in factories.
- **Reconciliation vs. Profound Disappointment**: While some early-exiters achieve a degree of psychological stability by compartmentalizing work and focusing on family or community life, university-educated respondents experience a profound sense of failure and dissonance ("a tremendous fall") as they realize their academic achievements yield no labor market returns.

## Key Theoretical Definitions

- **1.5 Generation**: Foreign-born individuals who immigrated to the receiving country as children (before age 12), undergoing their primary socialization, language acquisition, and schooling within the host nation.
- **Transition to Illegality**: The developmental life-course process whereby undocumented youth move from protected, inclusionary K–12 school contexts into unprotected, exclusionary adult legal environments, fundamentally altering their identity, relationships, and aspirations.
- **Suspended Illegality**: The developmental buffer period during childhood and early adolescence during which public school protections (_Plyler v. Doe_) render unauthorized legal status dormant and functionally irrelevant to daily life.
- **Liminal Legality**: A theoretical concept developed by Cecilia Menjívar describing an ambiguous, legally uncertain state in which migrants experience gray areas between documented and undocumented status across different institutions.
- **Plyler v. Doe (1982)**: Landmark U.S. Supreme Court ruling establishing that undocumented children are entitled to equal protection under the Fourteenth Amendment and cannot be denied access to public K–12 education.
- **Extrafamilial Mentors ("Really Significant Others")**: Non-kin adult figures (such as teachers, professors, and counselors) who provide undocumented youth with institutional knowledge, social capital, and material support to help them navigate legal barriers.
- **Occupational Convergence**: The structural outcome wherein college-educated undocumented adults and early school-leavers end up in identical low-wage, informal occupations due to legal barriers prohibiting formal employment.

## Summary

Roberto G. Gonzales's "Learning to Be Illegal" demonstrates that immigration policy and legal status function as overarching stratification mechanisms in the lives of 1.5-generation immigrant youth. Protected during childhood by the egalitarian ethos and legal mandates of public K–12 schooling, undocumented youth grow up expecting that hard work and academic achievement will lead to upward social mobility. However, the transition to adulthood forces them through a disorienting "transition to illegality," as the requirement for a Social Security number blocks access to legal employment, higher education funding, driving privileges, and formal citizenship.

Through a comparative analysis of early-exiters and college-goers, Gonzales illustrates that while extrafamilial mentors and social capital can help ambitious youth reach college, institutional support cannot ultimately overcome the structural barrier of non-legal status. By their late twenties, both college-educated youth and high school dropouts experience occupational convergence, finding themselves restricted to the same low-wage, clandestine labor markets as their first-generation parents. This structural bottleneck breaks the traditional sociological return on educational attainment, converting highly educated, American-socialized youth into an economically disenfranchised underclass.

Ultimately, Gonzales highlights a profound policy contradiction at the heart of American society: public institutions invest heavily in socializing and educating undocumented youth through high school, only for state immigration laws to systematically bar them from contributing their skills to the formal economy. Without legislative pathways to legal status, the state actively produces and sustains migrant illegality, squandering human capital and condemning millions of U.S.-raised young adults to the margins of American civic and economic life.
