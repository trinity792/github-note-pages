# School Choice or Schools' Choice?: Managing in an Era of Accountability (pp. 733–755)

## Executive Summary

Jennifer L. Jennings examines the complex structural interaction between public school choice policies and high-stakes accountability systems in urban education. Traditional American educational research on school choice has almost exclusively treated student choice as a demand-side phenomenon, positioning parents and families as the sole agents empowered to select academic institutions. Drawing on international marketization literature, Jennings reorients this focus toward the supply side, asking whether and how schools actively exercise choice by selecting, screening, and managing their student populations. In high-stakes accountability environments—such as those governed by No Child Left Behind—schools face severe organizational pressures to produce favorable performance metrics, creating powerful incentives for administrators to secure students who will boost standardized scores while avoiding or excluding students who require extensive remedial resources.

Grounded in 17 months of intensive ethnographic fieldwork (~1,200 hours of observation and 29 in-depth interviews) across three newly founded, nominally "unscreened" public high schools in New York City, Jennings constructs a micro-sociological theory explaining principals' divergent responses to choice and accountability. By synthesizing **sensemaking theory** with **social network theory**, she demonstrates that principals do not passively react to external accountability mandates; rather, they actively "enact" their organizational environments. Principals diagnose environmental constraints through collective sensemaking within their professional networks, where professional biographies, organizational status, and network structures (such as social closure) dictate whether and how administrators learn to "game the system".

Jennings identifies four primary organizational mechanisms through which schools shape their intakes despite official prohibitions against academic screening: strategic marketing/signaling, manipulating district application data under the guise of "informed choice," forging exclusive alliances with selective feeder middle schools, and discovering applicants' ranking preferences. Furthermore, schools maintain control over their student bodies post-enrollment by deflecting mobile "over-the-counter" (OTC) students and "counseling out" low-performing or disruptive youth. Ultimately, Jennings demonstrates that when choice systems operate alongside unadjusted accountability metrics, educational markets perpetuate stratification by allowing schools to choose students just as much as—or more than—students choose schools.

## Theoretical Framework: Sensemaking and Social Network Activation

### Sensemaking and the Enactment of Accountability

- **Cognitive Enactment of External Demands**: Building on Karl Weick’s sensemaking framework, Jennings argues that accountability signals do not exist as objective, self-evident rules waiting for school administrators to read. Instead, principals cognitively filter, interpret, and "enact" their regulatory environment, constructing distinct understandings of environmental demands and defining what constitutes safe or risky organizational behavior.
- **Logics of Action**: Principals interpret local accountability targets (such as attendance, course passage rates, and Regents exam scores) through specific professional lenses:
    - _Logic of Agency_: Viewing accountability metrics as malleable statistics that can be proactively "managed," "protected," and repackaged as organizational sound bites for external stakeholders.
    - _Logic of Constraint_: Viewing accountability indicators as rigid, external benchmarks that dictate survival, yet feeling limited in one's capacity to manipulate those figures directly.
    - _Logic of Educator Professionalism_: Interpreting performance metrics through broader moral and ethical commitments to social justice, refusing to alter admissions practices at the expense of disadvantaged youth.

### Social Networks, Instrumental vs. Expressive Support, and Social Closure

- **Interorganizational Network Embeddedness**: Principals occupy a unique structural location within school organizations, serving as the primary node of contact with central district authorities. Consequently, collective sensemaking regarding accountability policies occurs within interorganizational principal networks rather than internally with classroom teachers.
- **Network Resources and Activation**: Networks provide access to two distinct types of capital:
    - _Instrumental Support_: Operational intelligence, tactical knowledge regarding how to navigate district software, and information about which administrative rules can be safely bent or ignored without drawing district sanctions.
    - _Expressive Support_: Moral validation, shared professional identity, and emotional solidarity among peers facing similar institutional stressors.
- **Role of Social Closure**: Dense, overlapping network structures ("social closure") foster high levels of mutual trust and common norms. In franchise networks characterized by social closure, principals engage in sustained information pooling, sharing sensitive and borderline-unethical strategies for controlling student intake.

## Methodology and Organizational Typologies

### Ethnographic Design and School Profiles

Jennings conducted 17 months of multi-site ethnographic fieldwork (March 2004 to September 2005) across three newly opened small high schools in New York City. These schools belonged to a cohort of 55 new small schools created to replace large, failing comprehensive high schools. Crucially, all three schools were designated as "unscreened" by the NYC Department of Education, meaning admissions were legally open to all students via a lottery system and could not be conditioned on prior academic achievement, attendance, or disciplinary records.

Jennings sampled schools across three distinct organizational status configurations to examine how administrative biographies and network ties shape organizational behavior:

1. **Excel Academy ("Mom-and-Pop" Model)**: Founded by a coalition of local veteran New York City public school educators without elite academic credentials. The principal, Anna, possessed isolated, dyadic network ties grounded in local borough schools, characterized by high suspicion of district politics and low trust toward outside administrators.
2. **Renaissance High School ("Franchise" Model)**: Founded in partnership with "Renaissance Schools," an influential non-profit franchise network backed by corporate philanthropists and elite educational reformers. The principal, Marlena, was embedded in a tightly structured, citywide network of franchise principals that met regularly to pool organizational strategies and monitor performance metrics.
3. **Horizons High School ("Professional Organization" Model)**: Founded with the assistance of progressive professional development and community advocacy organizations committed to social justice. The principal, Rachel, was embedded in a dense network of progressive educators dedicated to serving high-need, historically marginalized urban youth.

## Strategic Mechanisms of Student Selection and Exclusion

### Front-End Intake Management in "Unscreened" Schools

Despite formal regulations designating unscreened schools as open-lottery institutions, principals who sought to shape their student bodies deployed four distinct organizational mechanisms:

- **Strategic Marketing and Signaling**: Schools constructed polished organizational identities during citywide high school fairs to attract high-performing families while deterring low-performing or high-need applicants. Administrators highlighted strict uniform codes, heavy Saturday work requirements, and lack of special education resources to signal that the school might not be a "good fit" for certain youth. Some unscreened peer schools went so far as to require multi-page supplemental applications with teacher recommendations and essays to discourage unmotivated applicants.
- **Manipulating District Application Data ("Informed Choice")**: The district's centralized computer admissions system supplied unscreened schools with detailed student files—including math/reading test scores, special education flags, English Language Learner (ELL) status, and historical absence records. Schools were required to code applicants as "1" (made an "informed choice" by attending an open house or contacting the school) or "2" (did not make an informed choice). Code 1 applicants received absolute priority in the lottery. Exploiting the vague definition of "informed choice," administrators at Excel and Renaissance systematically assigned Code 2 to applicants with low attendance (e.g., <90–93%), special education needs, or low grades, effectively removing them from the admissions pool.
- **Forming Alliances with Feeder Middle Schools**: Franchise leaders cultivated direct, informal partnerships with selective junior high schools. Guidance counselors at feeder middle schools provided franchise principals with lists of high-achieving students, guaranteeing these students top lottery placements in exchange for reserving seats for their graduates.
- **Discovering Ranking Preferences**: Because students could rank up to 12 high school choices on district applications, schools faced the risk of squandering admissions offers on candidates who ranked them low. Franchise administrators circumvented district rules by calling applicant families directly during open houses to verify if the school was ranked number one, pressuring parents to adjust their ranking preferences under the threat of losing admission.

### Post-Enrollment Retention, OTC Deflection, and "Counseling Out"

Intake management extended well beyond the formal spring admissions cycle into the active school year:

- **Deflecting "Over-the-Counter" (OTC) Mobile Students**: Students who move mid-year, arrive newly in the district, or lack high school placements are designated as "over-the-counter" (OTC) candidates. Administrators viewed OTC youth as organizational "tornadoes" and "statistical disasters" who threatened attendance and pass-rate targets. Franchise principals fended off OTC placements by artificially keeping transferred students on official attendance rolls to claim maximum building capacity, or by calling in political favors with local city council members to swap unwanted students.
- **"Counseling Out" Problematic Students**: Principals actively removed enrolled students whose excessive absences or disciplinary issues threatened institutional statistics. Administrators held intimidating conferences with parents, exaggerating minor behavioral infractions (e.g., labeling a peer dispute as "inciting gang violence") to convince families to voluntarily transfer their children to other schools.

## Divergent Administrative Responses: A Comparative Analysis

### Franchise Model: Renaissance High School

Marlena, principal of Renaissance High School, operated with a pronounced "logic of agency" focused on "protecting stats". Highly attuned to the demands of corporate philanthropists who funded the franchise, she viewed performance statistics as vital "sound bites" required to secure political clout and financial resources. Embedded in a closed, citywide franchise network that structured regular information-pooling meetings, Marlena activated her network to acquire sophisticated tactics for gaming the choice system. She deployed all four front-end selection strategies, systematically screened out special education and truant applicants, built alliances with high-performing feeder middle schools, deflected mid-year OTC placements, and aggressively "counseled out" low-attending youth who ruined institutional metrics.

### Mom-and-Pop Model: Excel Academy

Anna, principal of Excel Academy, operated under a "logic of constraint". While she recognized that district evaluations depended strictly on bottom-line numbers, she viewed day-to-day metrics as largely beyond her direct control. Isolated from citywide networks and lacking trust in outside colleagues, Anna relied on a small set of dyadic ties with local borough educators. Consequently, her capacity to game the choice system was constrained by limited information. While she utilized district application data to filter out applicants with attendance below 93% and engaged in subtle signaling during school fairs, she lacked the network capital required to forge feeder school alliances, deflect OTC students, or execute systematic counseling-out strategies.

### Professional Organization Model: Horizons High School

Rachel, principal of Horizons High School, operated under an ethos of "educator professionalism" anchored in social justice. Embedded in a dense network of progressive educators, Rachel viewed public schooling as a moral obligation to serve the most vulnerable urban youth, regardless of their incoming academic or behavioral profiles. She explicitly rejected gaming practices, arguing that unadjusted accountability targets forced educators into unethical behavior. Horizons strictly adhered to unscreened lottery rules, accepted all applicants (including those with severe truancy histories), and absorbed massive numbers of mobile OTC students mid-year. As a result, Horizons served a significantly more disadvantaged student population, absorbing the organizational strain and statistical penalties that other schools actively deflected.

## Policy Implications and Structural Contradictions

Jennings identifies fundamental policy flaws at the intersection of market-based school choice and high-stakes accountability systems:

- **Unadjusted Performance Metrics and Perverse Incentives**: Federal and state accountability policies (such as No Child Left Behind) evaluate schools using raw status benchmarks (e.g., percentage of students passing state exams or maintaining 90% attendance) rather than value-added growth measures. Because these targets do not adjust for students' baseline academic or social disadvantages, accountability systems inadvertently punish schools that enroll high-need youth while rewarding schools that successfully cream-skim affluent, high-achieving students.
- **Microstructures of Selection**: District choice mechanics often unwittingly facilitate covert cream-skimming. Providing unscreened schools with disaggregated student performance data during the lottery process creates structural opportunities for administrators to filter out low-performing candidates under the guise of discretionary rules like "informed choice".
- **Erosion of Public Equity**: When schools actively shape their student intakes to ensure organizational survival, school choice policies deepen educational inequality. High-performing, well-resourced students become concentrated in savvy franchise schools that master market navigation, while the most disadvantaged, mobile, and special-needs youth are systematically funneled into non-gaming public schools that bear the full brunt of accountability sanctions.

## Key Theoretical Definitions

- **Sensemaking Theory**: A micro-sociological framework developed by Karl Weick asserting that organizational actors do not merely react to an objective external environment, but actively "enact" it by selecting, filtering, and interpreting ambiguous environmental cues through their personal biographies, worldviews, and social contexts.
- **Enactment**: The cognitive and behavioral process within sensemaking through which individuals diagnose environmental opportunities and constraints, constructing localized understandings of what actions are permissible or risky.
- **Social Closure**: A network structure characterized by dense, highly interconnected, and overlapping ties among actors, which promotes high mutual trust, shared identity, and the rapid transmission of group norms and operational secrets.
- **Instrumental vs. Expressive Network Support**: _Instrumental support_ refers to actionable information, tactical guidance, and operational resources shared within networks to achieve specific goals; _expressive support_ refers to emotional validation, moral solidarity, and shared professional identity.
- **Unscreened Schools**: Public schools of choice designed to be open to all students via a neutral lottery system, explicitly prohibited by district regulations from evaluating applicants based on prior test scores, grades, attendance, or behavioral records.
- **Informed Choice**: A district admissions code originally intended to verify that an applicant had researched a school before applying; unscreened school administrators exploited this subjective designation to filter out low-attending or low-performing students.
- **Over-the-Counter (OTC) Students**: Mobile or late-arriving students who enter the school district mid-year or lack high school placements, frequently viewed by risk-averse administrators as organizational liabilities who threaten attendance and pass-rate statistics.
- **Counseling Out**: The informal practice whereby school administrators pressure low-performing or disruptive students and their parents to voluntarily transfer to another school, avoiding formal expulsion proceedings while protecting institutional performance metrics.

## Summary

Jennifer L. Jennings's "School Choice or Schools' Choice?: Managing in an Era of Accountability" demonstrates that public school choice systems do not operate as pure demand-side markets driven solely by parental preference. In an era of high-stakes, unadjusted accountability, public schools actively function as supply-side decision-makers, employing covert strategies to screen, select, and retain students who enhance organizational performance statistics while deflecting youth who pose statistical risks. By investigating the micro-processes of administrative decision-making, Jennings reveals that principals' responses to educational markets are mediated by their professional biographies, sensemaking frameworks, and interorganizational network embeddedness.

Principals embedded in dense, socially closed networks—such as corporate franchise networks—gain access to vital instrumental capital that teaches them how to safely game unscreened choice lotteries. These administrators strategically deploy marketing signals, exploit district application data under the pretense of "informed choice," forge alliances with selective feeder middle schools, deflect mid-year mobile transfers, and counsel out low-performing youth. Conversely, principals who lack network access or operate under an ethos of educator professionalism adhere strictly to open-enrollment rules, absorbing disproportionate numbers of high-need youth and suffering statistical penalties as a result.

Ultimately, Jennings exposes a fundamental structural contradiction in contemporary education policy: pairing open school choice with unadjusted, high-stakes performance benchmarks incentivizes schools to prioritize organizational survival over equitable access. Without value-added accountability metrics and rigorous oversight of admissions microstructures, school choice policies will continue to allow privileged schools to choose their students, exacerbating educational stratification and leaving the most vulnerable urban youth behind in penalizing institutional environments.
