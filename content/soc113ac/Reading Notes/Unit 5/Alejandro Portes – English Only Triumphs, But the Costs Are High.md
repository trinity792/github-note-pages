# English-Only Triumphs, But the Costs Are High (pp. 728–732)

## Executive Summary

Alejandro Portes addresses the persistent public and political anxieties surrounding the proliferation of foreign languages brought by contemporary immigration to the United States. Conservative advocacy groups, such as U.S. English, and historical figures like Theodore Roosevelt have periodically warned that failure to enforce immediate English monolingualism would lead to cultural disintegration, social hostility, and national fragmentation. Portes demonstrates that these fears are empirically groundless. Comparative sociological research indicates that the United States absorbs foreign language speakers faster than virtually any other industrial nation, with immigrant groups reliably transitioning from native-language dominance in the first generation to complete English monolingualism by the third generation.

Drawing on empirical data from the Children of Immigrants Longitudinal Study (CILS)—which tracked over 5,200 second-generation youth across 77 nationalities in Miami and San Diego—Portes reveals that English acquisition among immigrant children is virtually universal. By age 17, 98 percent of second-generation youth speak English fluently, and 88 percent explicitly prefer English over their parents' native language. Rather than a threat of linguistic fragmentation, the real empirical reality facing the United States is the rapid, irreversible loss of foreign language fluency. Less than one-third of second-generation youth maintain fluent bilingualism into late adolescence, while non-Spanish immigrant languages almost completely disappear within a single generation.

Portes argues that while the "English-Only" movement has effectively triumphed in forcing rapid linguistic shift, this victory carries severe social, developmental, and economic costs. Overturning early twentieth-century psychological myths that falsely labeled bilingualism an intellectual handicap, contemporary research proves that fluent bilingualism enhances cognitive flexibility, standardized test performance, and academic achievement. Furthermore, retaining parental languages fosters strong family cohesion, protects youth against generational conflict, and reduces the risk of downward assimilation into urban poverty. Finally, in an era of global economic integration, forced monolingualism squanders a critical human capital resource, creating acute shortages of skilled multilingual personnel in major American metropolitan centers.

## Historical Context and the Persistence of Nativist Language Fears

### The Myth of Linguistic Disintegration

- **Cyclical Nativist Reactions**: Every period of heightened immigration in American history has generated organized nativist movements warning that the influx of foreign tongues threatens national identity and cultural unity. In the early twentieth century, German, Polish, and Italian immigrants were targeted as existential threats to the republic; contemporary nativist organizations target immigrants from Latin America, Asia, and Eastern Europe under similar pretexts.
- **Ideological Framings of Language**: Groups such as U.S. English portray language diversity in apocalyptic terms, claiming that unchecked multilingualism will inevitably dissolve social cohesion into chaos. This rhetoric mirrors historical calls for forced assimilation, such as President Theodore Roosevelt’s declaration that the nation had room for only one language and must function as a crucible rather than a "polyglot boarding house."

### Empirical Reality: Unprecedented Speed of English Assimilation

- **Comparative Speed of Language Shift**: In a comprehensive comparative study of 35 nations conducted by Stanley Lieberson and colleagues, foreign languages faded more rapidly in the United States than in any other country studied. American institutions exercise an extraordinarily powerful assimilative pull that rapidly erodes non-English languages.
- **The Three-Generation Model of Assimilation**: Sociolinguistic research by Joshua Fishman and Calvin Veltman establishes a clear three-generation trajectory of language shift:
    1. _First Generation_: Immigrants learn as much English as required for economic survival, but continue using their native language as the primary medium of communication at home.
    2. _Second Generation_: Children of immigrants grow up bilingual, speaking the parental language at home while adopting English in public schools, peer groups, and workplaces.
    3. _Third Generation_: Grandchildren of immigrants speak English exclusively in all social domains, completing the transition to total monolingualism.
- **Census Evidence of Monolingual Dominance**: Data from the 1990 U.S. Census confirmed that 92 percent of the total U.S. population spoke only English at home. The remaining 8 percent consisted almost entirely of first-generation adult immigrants actively undergoing the initial phases of language adaptation.

## The Children of Immigrants Longitudinal Study (CILS) Findings

The study surveyed over 5,200 eighth- and ninth-grade students (ages 14 to 17) representing 77 distinct nationalities in Miami/Fort Lauderdale and San Diego, re-interviewing 82 percent of the sample three years later as high school seniors.

### Universal English Fluency and Preference

- **Near-Total English Acquisition**: CILS data revealed that English mastery is virtually universal among the second generation. By age 14, 94 percent of immigrant children spoke English fluently; by age 17, this figure rose to 98 percent.
- **Overwhelming Preference for English**: Linguistic preference shifts rapidly toward English during adolescence. While 72 percent of respondents preferred English over their mother tongue in middle school, 88 percent preferred English by senior year of high school.
- **Assimilation in Ethnic Enclaves**: Even within densely populated ethnic enclaves, English dominance prevails. Among Cuban-American students attending private bilingual schools in the heart of Miami's Cuban enclave, over 95 percent expressed a firm preference for English over Spanish.

### The Rapid Erosion of Parental Mother Tongues

- **Scarcity of True Bilingualism**: By age 17, less than one-third (29 percent) of second-generation youth retained fluent bilingualism, defined as the capacity to speak, understand, read, and write well in both English and their parental tongue.
- **Differential Loss Across Ethnic Groups**: While English-Spanish bilingualism was the most resilient, 65 percent of Latin American immigrant children had lost fluency in Spanish by late adolescence. Among non-Latin nationalities, mother tongue erosion exceeded 90 percent.
- **Extinction of Non-English Languages**: Languages such as Chinese, French, Haitian Creole, Korean, Portuguese, Tagalog, and Vietnamese virtually disappeared as functional languages within this second-generation cohort, leaving the vast majority of youth as English-only monolingual speakers.

## The High Costs of Forced Monolingualism

Portes contends that the rapid, forced destruction of immigrant languages exacts significant cognitive, social, and economic tolls on individuals, families, and the nation.

### Cognitive and Educational Disadvantages

- **Refuting the Early Monolingual Myth**: In the 1930s, researchers such as Madorah Smith erroneously claimed that bilingualism caused speech retardation and mental confusion. Portes notes that these early studies were methodologically flawed because they paired impoverished, non-English-speaking immigrant children against middle-class native English speakers without controlling for social class or levels of language mastery.
- **Cognitive Superiority of Fluent Bilinguals**: Landmark research initiated by Elizabeth Peal and Wallace Lambert in the 1960s demonstrated that fluent bilingual children consistently outperform monolingual peers on standardized intelligence and cognitive flexibility tests. Subsequent studies by Kenji Hakuta, Rafael Diaz, and Jim Cummins confirmed that managing two linguistic systems enhances abstract thinking, metalinguistic awareness, and the capacity to analyze linguistic structures.
- **Higher Academic Achievement**: Analyzing school system data in San Diego and South Florida, Portes, Rumbaut, and Cornelius found that fluent bilingual students achieved significantly higher standardized test scores and grade point averages (GPAs) than both limited bilinguals and English-only monolinguals, even after controlling for parental socioeconomic status.

### Family Cohesion, Parent-Child Communication, and Downward Assimilation

- **Protecting Parental Authority**: When immigrant children rapidly lose their native tongue while their parents remain monolingual in the primary language, fundamental lines of communication break down. Fluent bilingualism preserves intergenerational communication, allowing parents to effectively transmit moral values, provide guidance, and maintain authority.
- **Mitigating Family Conflict**: Statistical analyses by Portes and Lingxin Hao demonstrate that fluent bilingual youth experience significantly lower levels of parent-child conflict, higher family solidarity, higher self-esteem, and higher educational ambitions compared to monolingual English speakers of similar socioeconomic backgrounds.
- **Buffering Against Downward Assimilation**: Because immigrant families frequently settle in economically depressed urban centers, youth are exposed to adversarial street cultures. Strong family cohesion and ethnic community solidarity—anchored by shared language—provide critical buffers that prevent immigrant youth from assimilating downward into permanent poverty and gang involvement.

### Economic Losses in a Globalized Economy

- **The Global City Requirement**: As sociologist Saskia Sassen emphasizes, major urban centers like New York, Los Angeles, and Miami function as "global cities" that coordinate international finance, trade, and information flows. These economies require a highly skilled, multilingual workforce.
- **Shortages in the Workplace**: Forced language shift creates an acute economic paradox: metropolitan areas receiving massive immigration streams simultaneously suffer from severe shortages of bilingual professionals capable of conducting high-level corporate, legal, and administrative correspondence in foreign languages.

## The Bilingual Education Debate and Policy Imperatives

### Misconceptions Surrounding Proposition 227 and Remedial Language Programs

- **The Flaws of "English for the Children"**: In 1998, California voters approved Proposition 227, an initiative sponsored by Ronald Unz that sought to eliminate bilingual education in favor of one-year English immersion. Unz advocated this policy based on his mother's experience as a Yiddish-speaking immigrant who learned English in public schools, arguing that foreign-language instruction kept children trapped in inferior classes.
- **Conflating Remedial Tracking with True Bilingualism**: Portes clarifies that the public backlash against bilingual education was driven by a fundamental misunderstanding. Public school bureaucracies had converted "bilingual education" into permanent, low-track remedial programs taught in foreign languages that failed to teach English efficiently. Opponents attacked these defensive, low-quality programs rather than genuine bilingual instruction.

### True Bilingual Education and the "English-Plus" Framework

- **Mechanics of Dual-Language Immersion**: Genuine bilingual education does not avoid English; it provides rigorous English instruction alongside structured coursework in the native tongue (e.g., spending 1–2 hours daily on native language arts and subject instruction). Exemplified by private Cuban-American schools in Miami and public magnet dual-language programs, this approach successfully produces fluent bilingualism in both immigrant youth and native English speakers.
- **The "English-Plus" Policy Vision**: Supported by educational leaders such as former U.S. Secretary of Education Richard W. Riley, the "English-Plus" framework views foreign language preservation as a valuable national resource rather than a handicap. While European educational systems routinely graduate multilingual students, American nativism treats English acquisition and foreign language retention as a zero-sum game, needlessly destroying valuable human capital.

## Key Theoretical Definitions

- **Three-Generation Model of Language Assimilation**: The classical sociolinguistic framework detailing the generational transition from native-language dominance in the first generation, to bilingualism in the second generation, to exclusive English monolingualism by the third generation.
- **Fluent Bilingualism**: The capacity to speak, understand, read, and write proficiently in both English and a non-English language, associated with cognitive and academic advantages.
- **Limited Bilingualism**: A condition in which an individual possesses incomplete, deficient mastery of both English and their parental native language, often resulting in academic difficulty.
- **Children of Immigrants Longitudinal Study (CILS)**: A comprehensive research project led by Alejandro Portes and Rubén G. Rumbaut tracking over 5,200 second-generation youth in Florida and California to analyze educational, linguistic, and occupational adaptation.
- **Downward Assimilation**: A pathway within segmented assimilation theory wherein immigrant youth adopt the adversarial subcultures of marginalized urban groups, leading to educational detachment and permanent poverty.
- **English-Plus Framework**: An educational policy approach asserting that all students should master English while simultaneously developing or maintaining fluency in a second language.

## Summary

Alejandro Portes's analysis in "English-Only Triumphs, But the Costs Are High" refutes the widespread nativist myth that contemporary immigrants refuse to learn English and threaten American cultural cohesion. Empirical evidence from the Children of Immigrants Longitudinal Study demonstrates that language shift in the United States occurs with extraordinary speed. By late adolescence, 98 percent of second-generation youth are fluent in English, the vast majority prefer English in their daily lives, and non-English mother tongues rapidly disappear. Far from being a country threatened by linguistic fragmentation, the United States continues to be a graveyard for foreign languages.

However, Portes emphasizes that the complete triumph of English monolingualism represents a tragic loss of human and social capital. Rebuffing obsolete psychological theories that labeled bilingualism an intellectual detriment, modern sociological and psychological research proves that fluent bilingualism confers clear cognitive advantages, boosts academic achievement, and strengthens family cohesion. By preserving native language fluency, immigrant families maintain open channels of communication that protect youth against downward assimilation into urban underclass subcultures.

Ultimately, Portes calls for a shift away from punitive, zero-sum "English-Only" mandates like Proposition 227 toward genuine "English-Plus" dual-language education. In an increasingly interconnected global economy, the systematic destruction of foreign language skills harms immigrant families, deprives businesses of essential multilingual talent, and weakens the nation's international competitiveness. True educational progress requires recognizing that universal English fluency and mother-tongue preservation are complementary goals that enrich both individual students and American society as a whole.
