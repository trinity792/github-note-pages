# E Pluribus … Separation: Deepening Double Segregation for More Students (pp. 181–222)

## Executive Summary

Gary Orfield, John Kucsera, and Genevieve Siegel-Hawley examine the profound demographic transformation and accelerating resegregation of American public schools over recent decades. Writing for the Civil Rights Project / Proyecto Derechos Civiles at UCLA, the authors demonstrate that while the United States is rapidly becoming a multiracial, majority-minority nation, its public educational institutions are moving in the opposite direction—becoming increasingly isolated along both racial and economic lines. While public traditions and educational laws were historically constructed around a white, middle-class majority with a significant Black minority, contemporary student enrollments are multiracial and poorer, with nonwhite students comprising the majority in the nation's two largest regions, the West and the South.

The core thesis of the study is that Black and Latino students are trapped in a destructive pattern of **double segregation**: simultaneous isolation by both race and social class. While white and Asian students overwhelmingly attend middle-class schools with well-funded academic infrastructure, the average Black or Latino student attends a school where nearly two-thirds of classmates are low-income. This double segregation generates systemic educational disadvantages, concentrating under-resourced learning environments, inexperienced teaching staff, high turnover, remedial curricula, and elevated dropout rates in minority-segregated schools. Latino students, in particular, have experienced an uninterrupted 40-year rise in racial and economic isolation, becoming the most segregated minority group in American public education.

The authors trace this deepening isolation to a massive retreat from federal civil rights enforcement and a series of Supreme Court rulings—most notably _Dowell_ (1991), _Freeman_ (1992), _Jenkins_ (1995), and _Parents Involved_ (2007)—that systematically dismantled court-ordered desegregation plans and prohibited voluntary, race-conscious student assignment policies. Furthermore, modern market-based reforms, such as the rapid expansion of unregulated charter schools and the reduction of public transportation for magnet programs, have exacerbated educational disparities. To avert the severe long-term economic, social, and democratic consequences of a divided society, Orfield and his co-authors call for a renewed national commitment to multiracial school integration through modernized voluntary choice plans, Title VI civil rights enforcement, fair housing partnerships, and federal funding support.

## Demographic Transformations and the Emergence of Multiracial Schooling

### The Shrinking White Share and the Latino Enrollment Boom

- **National Demographic Reversal**: The racial composition of American public school enrollments has undergone a dramatic shift since the civil rights era. In 1970, white and Black students together accounted for 94 percent of the total national public school population, framing educational policy almost exclusively as a two-group, Black-white issue. By the 2000s, nonwhite minorities comprised more than 40 percent of total enrollments, driven primarily by a soaring Latino population and rapid growth among Asian American students.
- **Regional Disparities and Majority-Minority Frontlines**: The West and the South represent the vanguard of national demographic change. Both vast regions have crossed the threshold where there is no longer a white student majority in public schools. In states like California and Texas, Latino students constitute the single largest demographic bloc in the public school system, transforming educational governance across suburban and urban districts alike.

### The Collapse of the Post-Brown Integration Peak

- **Progress and Subsequent Backsliding in the South**: Following the passage of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and aggressive enforcement by federal courts and executive agencies, the South transformed from the nation's most legally segregated region into its most integrated. However, since the judicial rollback of desegregation mandates in the 1990s, the South has experienced substantial backsliding, with the proportion of Southern Black students in intensely segregated minority schools rising from 25 percent in 1991 to over 33 percent by 2009.
- **Severe Isolation in the Northeast and Midwest**: While civil rights litigation focused historically on the Jim Crow South, the Northeast and Midwest have persistently reported the highest levels of Black student segregation. Driven by residential housing segregation and the refusal of federal courts to enforce cross-district urban-suburban busing (_Milliken v. Bradley_), fully half of all Black students in the Northeast attend intensely segregated schools (90–100% nonwhite), while nearly one in four Black students in the Midwest attends an "apartheid school" (99–100% nonwhite).

## The Mechanism of Double Segregation: Race and Concentrated Poverty

### Defining Double Segregation

- **Interlocking Structural Disadvantages**: Orfield et al. define "double segregation" as the systemic alignment of racial isolation with concentrated school poverty. Because racial minority status and low family income are tightly correlated in American society, schools that enroll high proportions of Black and Latino students are almost universally schools of concentrated poverty.
- **Quantifying Differential Exposure to Poverty**: The typical Black or Latino student attends a school where 63.8% and 63.5% of classmates are low-income (eligible for free or reduced-price lunch), respectively. In stark contrast, the typical white student attends a school where only 37.0% of peers are low-income. The correlation between a school's combined Black/Latino enrollment percentage and its poor student percentage stands at an extraordinary \(r = +0.85\).

### Resource Deprivation in Concentrated Poverty Settings

- **Curricular and Pedagogical Impoverishment**: Schools characterized by double segregation provide significantly less challenging academic programs than middle-class schools serving white and Asian students. Under the pressure of high-stakes testing accountability regimes (e.g., No Child Left Behind), minority-segregated schools frequently reduce instruction to rote memorization and basic skill drills, while failing to offer Advanced Placement (AP) or honors courses required for competitive college admissions.
- **Institutional Instability and the "Dropout Factory" Pipeline**: High-poverty, high-minority schools face chronic instability, marked by high rates of teacher turnover, less experienced teaching staff, higher student discipline and expulsion rates, and severely depressed graduation rates. Virtually all of the nation's 2,000 designated "dropout factories"—high schools where senior class enrollment drops by more than 40 percent compared to freshman entry—are doubly segregated by race and poverty.

## Legal Evolution and Policy Shifts: From Brown to Resegregation

### The Eras of Federal Enforcement and Retreat

- **The Enforcement Era (1965–1968)**: Following the Civil Rights Act of 1964, the federal executive branch and a unanimous Supreme Court actively pressed local school boards to desegregate, utilizing Title VI funding sanctions and court orders to dismantle dual school systems.
- **Nixon's Judicial Realignment and Suburban Barriers**: The 1968 election marked a decisive shift in federal policy. President Nixon halted administrative desegregation enforcement, and his conservative Supreme Court appointments produced the first divided rulings. In _Milliken v. Bradley_ (1974), a 5–4 Supreme Court majority barred court-ordered desegregation across city-suburban district boundaries, insulating affluent white suburbs from urban integration plans and fueling "white flight."

### Key Supreme Court Rulings Driving Resegregation

- **Board of Education of Oklahoma City v. Dowell (1991)**: The Supreme Court authorized federal courts to terminate desegregation orders once school districts had complied in "good faith" for a reasonable period, permitting districts to return to segregated neighborhood attendance zones even if resegregation immediately resulted.
- **Freeman v. Pitts (1992) and Missouri v. Jenkins (1995)**: The Court progressively narrowed federal judicial oversight, ruling that school districts were not responsible for remedying resegregation caused by private residential housing choices or demographic shifts, and cutting off funding designed to make urban magnet schools competitive.
- **Parents Involved in Community Schools v. Seattle School District No. 1 (2007)**: In a landmark 5–4 decision, the Roberts Supreme Court severely restricted the ability of local school boards to voluntarily use individual student race as a factor in assignment plans to maintain integrated schools, invalidating long-standing choice plans in Seattle and Louisville.

### Market Reforms and Policy Inaction

- **The Resegregative Impact of Unregulated Charter Schools**: The rapid expansion of charter schools—championed by both the Bush and Obama administrations—has deepened educational segregation. Charter schools frequently operate without mandatory transportation or free lunch programs, excluding low-income youth and creating hyper-segregated settings that draw vital funding away from traditional public systems.
- **Atrophied Federal Support for Integration**: Federal funding for integration support—such as the Emergency School Aid Act (ESAA) of the 1970s—was eliminated during the Reagan administration. Modern federal initiatives, including "Race to the Top," have prioritized standardized testing, charter expansion, and competitive grants while offering negligible incentives for communities seeking to preserve multiracial enrollment.

## Quantitative Metrics of Segregation: Concentration, Exposure, and Dissimilarity

Orfield, Kucsera, and Siegel-Hawley deploy three distinct statistical measures to capture national, regional, and state-level patterns of school segregation:

### Concentration Metrics

- **Majority-Minority Schools (50–100% Nonwhite)**: In 2009, 74.1 percent of Black students and 79.5 percent of Latino students nationwide attended majority-minority public schools.
- **Intensely Segregated Minority Schools (90–100% Nonwhite)**: Nationally, 38.1 percent of Black students and 43.1 percent of Latino students attended intensely isolated schools where fewer than 10 percent of their classmates were white.
- **Apartheid Schools (99–100% Nonwhite)**: Over 15 percent of Black students and 14 percent of Latino students nationwide enrolled in absolute "apartheid" educational settings where white students constituted 1 percent or less of the student population.

### Exposure and Isolation Indices

- **Exposure to White Students**: In 1988, at the peak of court-ordered desegregation, the average Black student attended a school that was 36% white. By 2009, white exposure for Black students dropped to 29%. For Latino students, white exposure experienced a continuous, unmitigated decline, dropping from 44% white in 1970 to just 25% white in 2009.
- **Isolation Within Same-Group Peers**: The typical white student attends a school that is 75% white, demonstrating that white students remain the most racially isolated group in American public education despite overall demographic shifts.

### Index of Dissimilarity (\(D\))

- **Measuring Spatial Distribution**: The Index of Dissimilarity (\(D\)) measures how evenly two racial groups are distributed across schools within a given geographical area (\(0.0 = \text{complete integration}\); \(1.0 = \text{complete separation}\); values above \(0.60\) indicate high segregation).
- **Persistent High Segregation**: Nationally, Black-white dissimilarity stood at \(0.67\) in 2009, while Latino-white dissimilarity stood at \(0.69\). In the Northeast and Midwest, Black-white dissimilarity reached extreme levels (\(D = 0.73\)), meaning that 73 percent of Black or white students would have to transfer to a different school to achieve perfect racial balance.

## Societal Consequences of Segregation vs. Empirical Benefits of Integration

### The Severe Costs of Resegregation

- **Squandering National Human Capital**: Resegregation severely undermines national educational attainment at a historical moment when economic competitiveness depends entirely on postsecondary skills. Because Latino and Black youth represent the fastest-growing segments of the future American workforce, locking them into failing, doubly segregated schools threatens the nation's economic productivity.
- **Perpetuating Intergenerational Separation**: Segregated schooling possesses a self-perpetuating character. Individuals who attend racially isolated schools are significantly less likely as adults to live in integrated neighborhoods, work in diverse environments, or enroll their own children in multiracial schools.

### The Proven Benefits of Integrated Education

- **Cognitive and Academic Gains**: Decades of social science research demonstrate that desegregated schooling yields substantial academic benefits for minority students—including higher reading and math achievement test scores, improved high school graduation rates, and higher rates of college completion—without harming the academic performance of white students.
- **Social and Civic Competencies**: Integrated educational environments foster critical thinking, enhance students' ability to understand diverse perspectives, and reduce racial stereotyping and prejudice.
- **Employer Demand for Multiracial "Soft Skills"**: In a globalized economy, major corporations and military leaders strongly advocate for integrated education. The capacity to communicate, collaborate, and lead effectively across lines of racial and cultural difference is recognized as an essential "soft skill" in the modern labor market.
- **Gordon Allport’s Intergroup Contact Theory**: To maximize the social benefits of school integration, institutions must structure intergroup contact according to Gordon Allport’s classic criteria: equal status among groups, cooperative work toward common goals, institutional support from leadership, and active detracking at the classroom level.

- **Re-enact Federal Integration Assistance Grants**: Congress and the Department of Education should re-establish a modernized version of the Emergency School Aid Act (ESAA). This program should provide competitive funding to help diverse suburbs and gentrifying urban districts design legally compliant voluntary assignment plans, create whole-school magnet programs, establish university partnerships, and recruit diverse faculty.
- **Vigorous Title VI Civil Rights Enforcement**: The Department of Justice (DOJ) and the Department of Education’s Office for Civil Rights (OCR) must actively enforce Title VI of the Civil Rights Act. Federal authorities should investigate school districts engaged in racial gerrymandering of attendance zones, monitor districts seeking release from court oversight, and penalize charter schools that fail to serve English Language Learners or low-income students.
- **Establish Mandatory Civil Rights Standards for Charter Schools**: Federal and state authorities must condition public charter funding on strict civil rights compliance. Charter schools should be required to provide free meals and free transportation, adopt fair lottery admissions, and actively recruit diverse student bodies, ensuring they do not operate as white-flight enclaves or low-income vestigial centers.
- **Cross-Sector Planning (Education, Justice, and Housing)**: Federal and local officials must coordinate educational policy with housing policy. Housing agencies must enforce fair housing laws to prevent real estate steering, audit housing market discrimination, and ensure that tax-credit affordable housing projects are sited in areas served by strong, integrated schools rather than isolated, high-poverty zones.
- **Judicial Appointments and Community Mobilization**: Civil rights organizations, local journalists, churches, and community groups must raise public awareness regarding the educational harms of resegregation. Citizens must advocate for the appointment of federal judges who recognize the historical reality of systemic discrimination and respect the constitutional authority of local communities to pursue voluntary school integration.

## Key Theoretical and Policy Definitions

- **Double Segregation**: The structural phenomenon wherein minority students (specifically Black and Latino youth) experience simultaneous, overlapping isolation by both race/ethnicity and concentrated school poverty.
- **Resegregation**: The historical process characterized by the erosion of court-ordered desegregation plans and the subsequent return of high levels of racial and economic isolation in public schools.
- **Apartheid Schools**: Educational institutions in which nonwhite minority students constitute 99 to 100 percent of the total student enrollment, representing extreme, near-absolute racial isolation.
- **Index of Dissimilarity (\(D\))**: A demographic measure of evenness evaluating the proportion of a specific racial group that would have to transfer to a different school to achieve a uniform racial distribution across a district or region.
- **Exposure / Isolation Index**: A metric measuring the average racial composition of the school attended by a typical student of a specific racial or ethnic group.
- **Parents Involved in Community Schools v. Seattle (2007)**: A Supreme Court ruling establishing that public school districts cannot use individual student race as a decisive factor in voluntary student assignment plans to maintain integrated schools.
- **Intergroup Contact Theory**: Gordon Allport’s social-psychological framework establishing that contact between different racial groups reduces prejudice and improves relationships only when conducted under conditions of equal status, common goals, cooperative interaction, and institutional support.

## Summary

Gary Orfield, John Kucsera, and Genevieve Siegel-Hawley’s "E Pluribus … Separation: Deepening Double Segregation for More Students" provides an empirical assessment of the expanding racial and economic isolation in American public schools. Despite profound national demographic shifts toward a multiracial, majority-minority population, public schools are systematically resegregating. Latino students have experienced an unbroken four-decade rise in isolation, while Black students have seen the historic desegregation gains achieved following the Civil Rights Act of 1964 systematically erased over the past twenty years.

The authors demonstrate that modern school segregation is fundamentally a crisis of "double segregation"—the compounding intersection of racial isolation and concentrated poverty. Doubly segregated schools serving Black and Latino youth are systematically deprived of key educational resources, suffering from high teacher turnover, narrow rote-learning curricula, elevated discipline rates, and depressed high school graduation numbers. This resegregation has been driven not by the failure of integration, but by judicial retreat—specifically Supreme Court rulings that terminated court oversight and outlawed voluntary, race-conscious local choice plans—alongside passive federal policy and the rapid growth of unregulated charter schools.

Ultimately, Orfield and his co-authors emphasize that America cannot succeed as a prosperous, democratic, multiracial nation while educating its children in separate and unequal schools. Proving that integrated education yields higher academic achievement, enhanced critical thinking, reduced prejudice, and essential labor-market soft skills for all students, the authors present a clear policy roadmap. Reversing resegregation requires re-establishing federal integration funding, strictly enforcing Title VI civil rights protections against discriminatory charter and district policies, aligning housing and school planning, and recommitment to the democratic promise of integrated public education.
