# Privilege: The Making of an Adolescent Elite at St. Paul's School (pp. 120–135)

## Executive Summary

Shamus Khan’s ethnographic study of St. Paul’s School—a prestigious, highly selective New England boarding school—examines how elite educational institutions cultivate privilege and reproduce social inequality in twenty-first-century America. Drawing on his personal experiences as a student at St. Paul's in the 1990s and his subsequent return a decade later as a faculty member and sociological researcher, Khan investigates the fundamental transformation of the American upper class. He contrasts the "old elite"—an exclusive, WASP-dominated aristocracy defined by family lineage, inherited wealth, preppy culture, and rigid social boundaries ("entitlement")—with the "new elite," a group that appears racially diverse, open, self-reliant, and characterized by an embodied capacity for social fluidity ("privilege").

Central to Khan's analysis is the concept of "democratic inequality," which describes the historical paradox wherein American social institutions have progressively opened their doors to previously excluded racial, ethnic, and gender groups, while national levels of economic inequality have simultaneously expanded to historic highs. Elite colleges and prep schools showcase this phenomenon: while student bodies have become visually diverse, they remain overwhelmingly dominated by the economically affluent. Rather than acknowledging how family wealth purchases institutional access and preparatory advantages, the new elite adopt the American meritocratic narrative. They explain their elevated position not as a birthright or class privilege, but as the natural result of individual talent, hard work, and personal capacity.

Khan demonstrates that privilege is actively produced through three core institutional lessons at St. Paul's: learning that hierarchies are natural, enabling ladders rather than restrictive ceilings; learning that personal experiences and accomplishments matter more than inherited lineage ("what you have done" rather than "who you are"); and developing an embodied "ease" that allows individuals to navigate diverse cultural and social settings with radical egalitarianism and "omnivorous" tastes. Ultimately, Khan reveals the "trick of privilege": by converting costly, resource-intensive educational experiences into naturalized personal dispositions, the new elite mask the persistence of durable class inequality, legitimizing their social dominance in an ostensibly open, meritocratic society.

## Democratic Inequality and the Meritocratic Ideal

### The Paradox of Openness and Escalating Inequality

- **Defining Democratic Inequality**: Khan introduces "democratic inequality" to describe the structural reality of contemporary American society: while the nation embraces democratic principles of openness, formal access, and diversity, economic inequality has reached unprecedented levels. Popular assumptions hold that social openness and economic equality move in tandem, but historical evidence over the last fifty years demonstrates that institutions can become significantly more racially and ethnically diverse while simultaneously becoming richer and more economically concentrated.
- **The Illusion of Democratic Progress**: Elite social institutions—most notably private boarding schools and Ivy League universities—frequently publish press releases celebrating racial diversity and financial aid initiatives. However, adding racial diversity without addressing class stratification obscures the reality that elite postsecondary spaces remain bastions of wealth.

### Class Dominance in Elite Higher Education

- **Economic Concentration at Top Colleges**: Citing research on elite higher education, Khan notes that ninety percent of Harvard undergraduates come from families earning above the national median income ($55,000), with the dean of admissions defining "middle-income" families as those earning between $110,000 and $200,000 annually.
- **Underrepresentation of Low-Income Students**: Across prestigious colleges, the proportion of low-income students receiving federal Pell Grants (awarded to families earning under $40,000) remains under 8 percent. The student population at elite universities is wealthier today than in the mid-1970s, proving that postsecondary expansion has primarily benefited affluent families.
- **Education as the Pipeline to Elite Status**: Because level and prestige of education are among the strongest predictors of adult earnings, elite schooling plays a central role in training the individuals who drive national inequality, granting them substantial wage premiums and access to high-status managerial and corporate positions.

### From Conant's Jeffersonian Ideal to the SAT

- **Engineering a Natural Aristocracy**: The transition toward meritocracy was spearheaded in the mid-twentieth century by institutional leaders such as James Bryant Conant, President of Harvard University. Inspired by Thomas Jefferson's vision of a "natural aristocracy," Conant sought to dismantle the inherited WASP aristocracy by selecting elites based on intellectual talent rather than family lineage.
- **The SAT as a Meritocratic Instrument**: Conant's colleague, Henry Chauncey—a Dean at Harvard and descendant of elite WASPs—created the Scholastic Aptitude Test (SAT) to evaluate students' "innate academic aptitude". Chauncey intended the SAT to level the playing field, allowing talented youth from all social backgrounds to gain access to elite universities.
- **The Failure of the Meritocratic Project**: While the SAT succeeded in altering _who_ attended elite schools, it failed to curb the concentration of wealth. Standardized tests individualize achievement by measuring specific skills while treating them as indicators of inherent talent, ignoring the reality that SAT performance is highly correlated with family wealth, private test preparation, and demographic advantages.

## The Evolution of Elite Identity: From Entitlement to Privilege

### The Old Elite: Entitlement, Lineage, and Social Closure

- **Birthright and Inherited Advantage**: The traditional "old elite" derived its authority from ascribed characteristics: family name, ancestral lineage, inherited wealth, and exclusive WASP social networks. Attending elite boarding schools like St. Paul's was treated as a self-evident birthright for upper-class families such as the Rockefellers and Vanderbilts.
- **Social Closure via Moats and Fences**: Old elites functioned as a cohesive class that maintained social dominance through "moats and fences"—exclusive social practices, preppy cultural codes, and institutional barriers designed to keep outsiders out. Their identity was grounded in entitlement, displaying overt arrogance and explicit class boundaries.

### The New Elite: Diversity, Individualization, and Self-Advancement

- **Rejection of Birthright Rhetoric**: The "new elite" at St. Paul's explicitly repudiate the idea that family background or inherited wealth guarantees social standing. They view themselves as an individualized collection of the most hardworking and talented youth in the world.
- **Embracing Hard Work and Mobility**: Like middle-class Americans and recent immigrants, the new elite believe firmly in the American Dream, asserting that upward mobility is available to anyone willing to work hard. Pointing to their racially diverse classmates, they view their elevated status as the fair reward for personal effort rather than structural privilege.

### Cultural Capital, Embodiment, and Naturalized Merit

- **Privilege as Embodied Disposition**: Drawing on Pierre Bourdieu’s sociological framework, Khan argues that privilege is not merely financial capital or credential ownership, but an _embodied disposition_—a set of physical practices, interactional styles, and ways of moving through the world.
- **Naturalizing Socially Produced Advantages**: At St. Paul's, privilege is inscribed directly onto students' bodies through daily routines, chapel gatherings, and residential life. Because this physical poise and social comfort appear effortless, society perceives elite capacity as an innate personal trait ("who you are") rather than the product of an expensive, resource-intensive institutional environment.

### Lesson 1: Hierarchies are Natural Ladders, Not Ceilings

- **Hierarchies as Enabling Structures**: Students learn that social hierarchies are a permanent, natural feature of human society, but they reinterpret these hierarchies as enabling "ladders" for personal advancement rather than rigid, restrictive "ceilings". In an open meritocracy, hierarchies are viewed as fair because they sort winners and losers based on talent and effort rather than inherited birthright.
- **Navigating Authority Through Controlled Intimacy**: Learning to climb these ladders requires mastering a subtle interactional skill: establishing intimacy and ease with authority figures (faculty, administrators, elite adults) without acting as explicit equals. Students learn to pretend the hierarchy is not there while continuously respecting its structural power, making hierarchies work to their personal advantage.

### Lesson 2: Experiences Matter More Than Inheritance

- **Shift from Lineage to Accomplishment**: The new elite transition from the logic of the old elite—_who you are_ (family heritage)—to the logic of the new elite—_what you have done_ (cultivated experience). Students who act entitled based on family wealth or preppy background are rejected by their peers.
- **Developing Privilege Through Action**: Adjusting to St. Paul's is difficult for all students regardless of origin. Privilege is not viewed as an innate trait one is born with, but as an orientation developed through active participation in challenging institutional experiences—academic seminars, athletic competitions, musical performances, and residential life.

### Lesson 3: Privilege Means Being at Ease, No Matter What the Context

- **Embodied Ease Across All Domains**: The core practice of privilege is "ease"—feeling completely comfortable and poised in virtually any social setting. In the classroom, students comfortably analyze both classical literature (_Beowulf_) and popular cinema (_Jaws_); outside class, they listen to both classical compositions and hip-hop.
- **Cultural Omnivorousness vs. Exclusivity**: Rather than erecting boundaries around "highbrow" culture to mark their distinction, the new elite display radical cultural "omnivorousness," consuming across the entire cultural spectrum. Ironically, cultural exclusivity is reinterpreted as the mark of the "losers" in society: lower-class individuals are viewed as disadvantaged because their narrow tastes and closed-mindedness prevent them from seizing the opportunities of an open world.

## Key Theoretical Definitions

- **Democratic Inequality**: The sociological paradox wherein a society fully embraces democratic principles of formal openness, diversity, and equal access, even as actual levels of socioeconomic inequality expand significantly.
- **Entitlement vs. Privilege**: _Entitlement_ represents the old elite orientation built on inherited birthright, WASP lineage, and exclusive social closure; _privilege_ represents the new elite orientation characterized by embodied ease, adaptability, and a meritocratic self-concept.
- **Embodied Ease**: The physical, interactional capacity to project poise, comfort, and confidence across any social context, converting resource-intensive institutional training into an apparent natural personal trait.
- **Cultural Omnivorousness**: The cultural orientation of the new elite characterized by the omnivorous consumption of both high-status classical culture and popular mainstream culture, treating cultural breadth as a sign of open-mindedness.
- **Durable Inequality**: Large, systematic inequalities where advantages and disadvantages are transferred intergenerationally along categorical boundaries (such as class, race, and gender) rather than individual merit.
- **The Trick of Privilege**: The social process through which elite institutions endow students with valuable cultural and interactional capital, while framing those acquired advantages as innate personal qualities ("who you are"), thereby legitimizing class reproduction.
- **The Democratic Conundrum**: The paradox wherein the diversification and democratization of elite institutions undercut collective resistance to inequality by convincing society that outcomes are fair and based solely on individual merit.

## Summary

Shamus Khan's "Privilege: The Making of an Adolescent Elite at St. Paul's School" provides a critical analysis of how elite education reproduces inequality under the guise of democratic meritocracy. By investigating the daily life of St. Paul's School, Khan illustrates that while elite institutions have opened their doors to racial and geographic diversity, they remain structurally dominated by families of immense wealth. However, unlike the "old elite" who justified their dominance through birthright, WASP lineage, and exclusive entitlement, the "new elite" embrace an ideology of individual merit, attributing their success to personal talent, hard work, and open-mindedness.

Through three core institutional lessons, St. Paul's cultivates "privilege"—an embodied ease that allows students to navigate social hierarchies naturally, treat status as the product of personal experience rather than inheritance, and display cultural omnivorousness across diverse settings. By mastering this ease, students learn to project poise and competence everywhere, making their socially engineered advantages appear as natural personal capacities.

Ultimately, Khan exposes the "trick of privilege" and the "democratic conundrum" in modern American society. By replacing explicit social closure with an illusion of open, individual competition, the new elite obscure the persistent reality of durable class inequality. Because society believes that elite institutions select students based solely on talent, the success of the rich is naturalized as personal merit, while the failure of the disadvantaged is internalized as individual incapacity, reinforcing social stratification in an era of democratic inequality.
