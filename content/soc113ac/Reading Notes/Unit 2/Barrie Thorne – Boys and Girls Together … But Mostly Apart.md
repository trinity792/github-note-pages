# Barrie Thorne – “Boys and Girls Together . . . But Mostly Apart”

## Executive Summary

Barrie Thorne’s seminal ethnographic reading, “Boys and Girls Together . . . But Mostly Apart,” explores the complex social construction of gender in the everyday lives of elementary school children. Drawing on extensive field observations conducted across two working-class public elementary schools in Northern California—Ashton School and Oceanside School—Thorne examines how children negotiate, maintain, and occasionally cross gender boundaries within the institutional framework of formal schooling. Unlike families or small neighborhood peer groups, schools are crowded, bureaucratic, age-graded environments where a small number of adults continually manage and evaluate large numbers of children. Thorne demonstrates that the public, witnessed quality of school life and the constant administrative quest for organizational order heavily shape the spatial choreography of children’s daily gender relations.

Central to Thorne’s theoretical framework is a critique of the conventional, static concept of "sex segregation." Rather than viewing gender separation as an absolute, legally enforced, or socio-biologically determined state, Thorne characterizes it as a fluid, context-dependent process summarized by Erving Goffman’s phrase "with-then-apart." Children periodically separate into single-gender groups, spatial territories, and social rituals, but they also regularly come together to participate in shared classroom activities, academic routines, and integrated playground games. The extent of gender separation fluctuates significantly depending on organizational constraints, adult interventions, spatial ecology, and children's self-directed peer interactions.

The spatial layout of school environments plays a decisive role in structuring gender dynamics. In classrooms, when teachers assign seating based on non-gender criteria such as reading ability, height, sight, or behavioral management, students are frequently placed in gender-integrated arrangements. Conversely, when students are permitted to select their own seats, they consistently create a clear spatial divide between a "boys' side" and a "girls' side." The contrast between indoor structured settings and outdoor unstructured settings is even more pronounced: on the playground, where adult supervision is minimal, boys control roughly nine-tenths of the open physical territory (monopolizing large sports fields and courts), while girls occupy much smaller equipment areas situated directly adjacent to school buildings.

Finally, Thorne evaluates the sociometric and interpersonal dimensions of cross-gender relations, highlighting the phenomenon of children as "familiar strangers." Despite living in physical proximity and interacting continuously within shared organizational routines, boys and girls rarely form lasting cross-gender friendships or integrated cliques. Deep-seated peer norms, fear of romantic teasing, and ritualized boundary work constrain cross-gender interactions, causing emotional intimacy and social solidarity to consolidate primarily within same-gender groups. Through this intricate spatial and social choreography, school settings actively amplify and socialize traditional gender roles.

## Organizational Context and the "With-Then-Apart" Choreography

### Schools as Bureaucratic and Public Spaces

- **Density and Adult Oversight**: Schools operate as crowded, highly public, and bureaucratic environments where a small cadre of teachers and aides must maintain order among hundreds of children. To control noise, manage spatial congestion, and regulate movement, school authorities continually sort students into smaller units—such as reading groups, seating tables, cafeteria shifts, and hallway lines—which inadvertently serve as organizational contexts for children's gender work.
- **The Concept of "With-Then-Apart"**: Thorne rejects the totalizing term "sex segregation" because it implies an immutable or legally enforced divide similar to religious purdah. Instead, she utilizes Erving Goffman's concept of "with-then-apart" to emphasize that gender separation is a dynamic and variable process; children periodically separate into single-gender groups and territories, yet frequently converge to form a shared social world within the same institutional framework.

### The Relevance of Contextual Gender Display

- **Variability of Gender Relevance**: Thorne asserts that while individual actors always carry or "do" gender, the dichotomous distinction between male and female varies in its social relevance across different structural settings. Gender boundaries become highly salient during specific competitive rituals or unstructured play, whereas gender recedes in importance when organizational tasks demand focused, cooperative participation.

## Spatial Arrangements across School Settings

### Classroom Seating and Seating Plans

#### Teacher-Assigned Seating

- **Integration Through Non-Gender Criteria**: In classrooms where teachers actively assign student seating according to physical or academic needs—such as hearing, vision, height, or reading group levels—the resulting desk arrangements cut directly across gender lines, producing extensive cross-gender contact and informal interaction.
- **Behavioral Management and Gender Skew**: When teachers separate close friends to minimize classroom chatter and behavioral disruption, an unintentional gender-mixing effect occurs. Because children’s close friendships are predominantly single-gender, splitting up best friends naturally forces students to sit next to peers of the opposite gender.

#### Student-Chosen Seating

- **Spatial Cleavage and "Sides"**: When teachers allow students to select their own desks—such as Miss Bailey’s combined fourth-fifth grade class at Oceanside School—students consistently organize themselves into a rigid spatial divide, creating a recognized "boys' side" and "girls' side" within the classroom.
- **Institutional Ratification**: Teachers often treat gender-divided seating as an indulgence or privilege granted to students, threatening to dismantle it as a disciplinary measure while simultaneously reinforcing the divide by pitting the "boys' side" against the "girls' side" in academic contests like spelling bees or math drills.

### Life on the Line: Queuing and Boundary Maintenance

- **Historical and Structural Roots**: Lining up is a fundamental organizational routine taught as early as kindergarten to manage delay and hallway traffic. Historical patterns of formal sex segregation—such as separate building entrances engraved with "Girls" and "Boys" or centralized gender-specific bathrooms—established long-standing school traditions of forming separate lines.
- **Peer Policing and Line Sanctuaries**: Children actively construct and defend separate queues through verbal policing, gestures, and teasing (e.g., chanting "John's in the girls' line"). These queues frequently function as gendered sanctuaries where children invoke line rules to block cross-gender intrusion or protect contested items like playground equipment.

### Lunchroom Tables and Informal Seating Claims

- **Territorial Staking and Arrival Patterns**: Lunchroom seating takes shape through an informal scramble where early-arriving students (often cold-lunch bringers) stake out spatial claims at table ends. Tables fill through invitations, squeezing in, or filling leftover spots, resulting in same-gender clusters even when boys and girls share a single long cafeteria table.
- **Enforcing Table Taboos**: High-status students can convert mixed tables into single-gender zones through dramatic verbal declarations (e.g., announcing "Oooo, too many girls"), prompting peers to vacate the table and establishing an informal taboo against cross-gender seating for the remainder of the lunch period.

## Playground Geography and Activity Sorting

### Spatial Control and Turf Allocations

- **Boys' Monopolization of Open Fields**: On the school playground—an arena characterized by minimal adult intervention and maximum student autonomy—boys control approximately nine-tenths of the available physical space, specifically large, fixed territories designated for team sports like baseball fields, soccer grids, and basketball courts.
- **Girls' Predominance in Building-Adjacent Spaces**: The playground areas where girls predominate are significantly smaller and situated close to school buildings. These spaces consist of fixed equipment like jungle gyms, monkey bars, and painted cement areas used for turn-taking games like foursquare, jump rope, and hopscotch.

### Integrated Activities and Adult Intervention

- **Sites of Cross-Gender Play**: Although chasing games, pretend play, and organized sports frequently segregate by gender, specific playground activities serve as common sites of gender integration, including kickball, dodgeball, handball, foursquare, and organized tag.
- **Adult Policing of Turf**: Playground aides routinely take gendered spatial divisions for granted, actively enforcing boundaries by shooing children away from "inappropriate" territory—particularly when boys enter girls' equipment areas with teasing intent.

## Sociometric Relations and the "Familiar Strangers" Phenomenon

### Friendship Perceptions and Single-Gender Cliques

- **Self-Reported Best Friendships**: Sociometric research across elementary schools demonstrates that at least 75 percent of children name only same-gender peers as best friends. Sociometric mapping confirms that public classroom cliques are almost exclusively single-gender.
- **Superficiality of Cross-Gender Contacts**: Although boys and girls interact daily in classrooms, lunchrooms, and queues, these contacts rarely deepen into lasting friendships or acknowledged social alliances.

### The Concept of "Familiar Strangers"

- **Proximity Without Intimacy**: Drawing on Janet Schofield's concept, Thorne describes boys and girls in coeducational schools as "familiar strangers"—peers who exist in continuous physical proximity and recognize one another, yet possess little genuine understanding or intimate knowledge of each other's social worlds.
- **Teasing and Social Distance**: Deeply ingrained peer norms and the pervasive threat of romantic teasing (such as being accused of having a "boyfriend" or "girlfriend") lead children to view the gender gulf as unbridgeable, prompting them to channel emotional intimacy exclusively into same-gender peer groups.
