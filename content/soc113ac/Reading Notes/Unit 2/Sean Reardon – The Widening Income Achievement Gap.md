# Sean F. Reardon – “The Widening Income Achievement Gap”

## Executive Summary

Sean F. Reardon’s landmark study, “The Widening Income Achievement Gap,” provides a comprehensive empirical analysis of how the relationship between family income and academic achievement has transformed in the United States over the past fifty years. Drawing on data from twelve nationally representative studies spanning birth cohorts from the 1940s through the early 2000s, Reardon standardizes standardized reading and math test scores to track the gap between students at the 90th percentile and 10th percentile of the family income distribution. His findings reveal a profound structural shift: the income achievement gap expanded by approximately 40 percent over three decades, growing from 0.9 standard deviations among children born in the 1950s and 1960s to 1.25 standard deviations for those born around 2000. Strikingly, while the Black-White achievement gap was twice as large as the income gap fifty years ago, substantial civil rights progress alongside surging economic inequality has caused the income achievement gap to surpass the racial achievement gap in magnitude.

To evaluate whether this widening disparity is driven by declining or unequal K–12 public school quality, Reardon examines longitudinal data from the Early Childhood Longitudinal Study-Kindergarten Cohort (ECLS-K). The data show that the income achievement gap is already large (1.15 standard deviations) when children enter kindergarten and remains virtually unchanged (1.25 standard deviations) by the end of eighth grade. Furthermore, achievement gaps narrow slightly while school is in session during the kindergarten and first-grade school years and widen during the summer months when children are out of school. This "summer setback" pattern demonstrates that K–12 schools do not actively generate or expand the income achievement gap; rather, schools exert a modest equalizing effect that partially offsets the severe developmental inequalities established in early childhood and home environments.

Reardon attributes the expansion of the income achievement gap to five interconnected macro-social and economic trends: rising national income inequality (which expanded the 90/10 income ratio from 5:1 in 1970 to 11:1 today); a near sevenfold differential in financial enrichment investments made by high-income versus low-income parents; economic bifurcation that eliminates middle-wage jobs and increases career insecurity; the growing dominance of high-stakes testing in educational policy and college admissions; and demographic shifts toward assortative mating that concentrate educational and financial capital in two-parent affluent households. Reardon concludes that addressing educational inequality requires a comprehensive strategy combining macro-economic income redistribution and family support policies with targeted educational investments, such as expanding high-quality early childhood education, extending instructional time, and implementing socioeconomic school desegregation.

## Core Empirical Findings on the Income Achievement Gap

### The Widening Gap Across Birth Cohorts

- **Substantial Growth Over Three Decades**: Reardon analyzes twelve nationally representative datasets covering birth cohorts from 1943 to 2001 to measure the academic achievement gap between students at the 90th percentile and 10th percentile of family income. For children born in the 1950s, 1960s, and early 1970s, the reading achievement gap remained stable at roughly 0.90 standard deviations. Beginning with cohorts born in the mid-1970s, the gap widened rapidly, reaching approximately 1.25 standard deviations for cohorts born in the late 1990s and early 2000s—a 40 percent increase in academic disparity.
- **Driven by Gains at the Top**: Reardon emphasizes that the widening income achievement gap is not caused by declining performance among low-income students. In absolute terms, average reading and math test scores for low-income and middle-income students have increased modestly over recent decades; however, test scores among high-income students have risen at a dramatically faster rate, pulling the top of the distribution away from the rest.

### Convergence and Divergence: Income versus Racial Achievement Gaps

- **Reversal of Historical Disparities**: For birth cohorts in the 1950s and 1960s, the Black-White achievement gap (~1.20 to 1.25 standard deviations) was significantly larger than the income achievement gap (~0.90 standard deviations). Over the subsequent fifty years, substantial progress in dismantling legal racial segregation and improving economic and educational opportunities for African Americans significantly narrowed the Black-White achievement gap.
- **Income Inequality Replaces Racial Disparities as the Primary Gap**: Concurrently, rapid increases in economic inequality expanded class-based educational disparities. By the early 21st century, the income achievement gap (1.25 standard deviations) grew to be nearly twice as large as the Black-White achievement gap (~0.65 to 0.70 standard deviations). While racial inequality remains a persistent problem in American education, socio-economic status has become an even stronger predictor of standardized academic achievement than race.

### Disparities in Higher Education and Non-Cognitive Outcomes

- **Diverging College Completion Rates**: Class-based polarization extends far beyond standardized K–12 test scores. Citing research by Bailey and Dynarski, Reardon highlights that college completion rates among children from high-income families grew sharply over the last several decades, whereas completion rates for low-income students remained flat.
- **Stratification at Selective Higher Education Institutions**: High-income students occupy an increasing share of undergraduate enrollments at the nation's most selective colleges and universities. This overrepresentation persists even when comparing high-income and low-income students who possess identical standardized test scores and academic high school records.
- **The Class Gap in Civic Engagement and Soft Skills**: Citing Robert Putnam and colleagues, Reardon notes growing class gaps in adolescent "soft skills" and civic behaviors, including participation in extracurricular academic clubs, varsity sports, community volunteering, and self-reported levels of generalized social trust.

## Educational Trajectories and the Role of K–12 Schooling

### Early Emergence and K–12 Stability

- **Pre-School Origin of the Gap**: Utilizing longitudinal panel data from the Early Childhood Longitudinal Study-Kindergarten Cohort (ECLS-K), which tracked 25,000 students from 1998 to 2007, Reardon evaluates how the income achievement gap evolves as children progress through school. The reading gap is already exceptionally large at kindergarten entry (1.15 standard deviations) when children are five years old.
- **Parallel Growth Dynamics During Schooling**: As the ECLS-K cohort advanced from kindergarten through eighth grade, the income achievement gap expanded by only 0.10 standard deviations (reaching 1.25 standard deviations in eighth grade). Other longitudinal studies tracking students through middle and high school confirm that the income achievement gap remains largely stable across the K–12 trajectory, proving that unequal K–12 school quality is not the primary origin of the income gap.

### The "Summer Setback" and School Equalizing Capacity

- **Schools as Equalizing Institutions**: Detailed seasonal analyses within the ECLS-K dataset reveal that the income achievement gap actually narrows slightly between the fall and spring of the kindergarten and first-grade school years when children are actively attending school.
- **Summer Setback Mechanics**: ==The income gap widens primarily during the summer months between kindergarten and first grade when children are out of school and entirely dependent on home environments. This pattern aligns with classic seasonal learning research (e.g., Entwisle, Alexander, and Olson), demonstrating that formal schooling serves a compensatory, equalizing function that mitigates home-based socio-economic advantages.==

## Social and Economic Drivers of Gap Expansion

### Rising Income Inequality and Financial Investment in Children

- **Expansion of the 90/10 Income Ratio**: Between 1970 and the late 2000s, overall family income inequality grew dramatically. In 1970, a family with school-age children at the 90th percentile of the income distribution earned 5 times as much as a family at the 10th percentile; by 2010, the 90th percentile family earned 11 times as much as the 10th percentile family.
- **Surging Parental Enrichment Spending**: Rising top incomes provided affluent parents with ==unprecedented financial capital to invest in their children's cognitive development==. Citing Kornrich and Furstenberg, Reardon notes that high-income families in 1972 spent 4 times as much on child enrichment activities (e.g., high-quality child care, private tutoring, summer camps, books, computers, and SAT prep) as low-income families; by 2007, high-income families spent nearly 7 times as much.

### Economic Bifurcation and Career Insecurity

- **Loss of Middle-Skill Employment**: The American economy has bifurcated into a low-skill/low-wage service sector and a high-skill/high-wage technical and financial information sector. The contraction of manufacturing and unionized blue-collar jobs means that a high school diploma no longer secures a middle-class wage.
- **Heightened Parental Anxiety and Competition**: Because cognitive skills and formal postsecondary credentials have become strict prerequisites for economic security, parents experience intense anxiety regarding their children's upward mobility. Affluent parents channel this anxiety into competitive, resource-intensive child-rearing strategies designed to maximize test scores and secure college admissions.

### Family Structure, Assortative Mating, and Accountability Pressures

- **Diverging Family Demographics**: American family structures have experienced significant class polarization over the last four decades. High-income children are increasingly raised in stable, two-parent households where both parents hold college degrees, whereas low-income children are increasingly raised in single-mother households with lower educational attainment.
- **Compounding Family Resources**: Due to educational assortative mating, family income has become highly correlated with other crucial developmental assets, such as parental formal education, maternal time availability, and household stability.
- **Centrality of High-Stakes Standardized Testing**: Over the past two decades, standardized test scores have become the primary benchmark for measuring school quality and student merit under state and federal accountability policies, as well as in competitive college admissions. This institutional shift incentivizes affluent parents to utilize their superior financial resources specifically to boost test performance.

## Policy Implications and Educational Interventions

### Macro-Socioeconomic and Family Support Strategies

- **Limits of School-Sole Solutions**: Reardon stresses that because the income achievement gap originates in early childhood and reflects broader structural economic inequality, school-based interventions alone cannot eliminate educational disparities.
- **Comprehensive Policy Framework**: Reardon advocates for a multi-sector policy strategy that includes:
    1. _Economic Policies_: Tax policies, wage support, and wealth redistribution mechanisms designed to reduce raw family income inequality.
    2. _Family Support Policies_: Expanded safety nets, paid family leave, affordable health care, and housing assistance to ensure low-income children grow up in stable, secure home and neighborhood environments.
    3. _Early Childhood Investments_: Universal high-quality preschool and early center-based care to foster cognitive and socio-emotional development before class gaps solidify at kindergarten entry.

### Targeted School-Level Interventions

- **Prioritizing Early Elementary Grades**: States and school districts should concentrate educational funding and instructional interventions in preschool, kindergarten, and the early primary grades. Because achievement gaps are self-perpetuating over time, early interventions yield the highest long-term returns.
- **Expanding High-Quality Learning Time**: Extending instructional time through longer school days, expanded school years, high-quality after-school enrichment, and mandatory summer learning programs directly counteracts the "summer setback" that drives class disparities.
- **Socioeconomic School Integration**: Income-based residential segregation has concentrated low-income students in high-poverty schools. School districts should implement student assignment policies—such as controlled choice plans, magnet programs, and inter-district transfers—that actively promote socioeconomic diversity across classrooms and ensure equitable access to highly qualified teachers, rigorous curricula, and modern educational resources.
