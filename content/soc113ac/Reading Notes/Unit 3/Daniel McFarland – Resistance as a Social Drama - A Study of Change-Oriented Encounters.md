# Resistance as a Social Drama: A Study of Change-Oriented Encounters (pp. 567–581)

## Executive Summary

Daniel A. McFarland investigates the dynamic micro-sociological processes through which high school students resist academic learning and challenge classroom authority. Rather than viewing student misbehavior as random, chaotic, or purely irrational, McFarland demonstrates that acts of resistance unfold as patterned, change-oriented interactional sequences. Drawing on dramaturgical theory, micro-interactionism, and social movement framing literature—specifically adapting Victor Turner’s concept of "social dramas" and Erving Goffman’s frame analysis—McFarland models classroom resistance as a multi-stage social drama. When student resistance breaches established classroom routines, it initiates a process of ceremonial deconstruction and reconstruction that has the potential to reproduce, modify, or revolutionize the existing classroom social order.

Central to McFarland’s analytical framework is the distinction between passive and active resistance. Passive resistance represents a tacit, indirect subversion of normative schooling codes (e.g., side conversations, sleeping, non-compliance), serving primarily as a diagnostic critique of classroom affairs without offering a direct alternative. In contrast, active resistance involves an open, overt effort to undermine teacher instruction, contest institutional rules, and instantiate an alternative socio-cultural order. Active resistance functions as "frame contestation," wherein resisters adopt an alternative interpretive standpoint—such as a personal, peer-social, or racial-equality frame—to demobilize academic tasks and recruit classmates to their cause.

McFarland maps the social drama of resistance across four sequential phases: breach, crisis, redress, and reintegration (or schism). The drama begins when a student’s public defiance creates a breach that disrupts academic instruction. If the breach resonates with peers, it escalates into a public crisis characterized by liminality, wherein conventional academic roles fail and the true power relations of the classroom are exposed. To contain the crisis, teachers and student leaders deploy redressive framing strategies—such as amplifying sanctions, bridging interests, extending tasks, or transforming rules. The drama culminates in one of four distinct social outcomes: reproduction (teacher wins, student complies), negotiation (compromise and concessions), social revolution (student resistance transforms classroom rules), or schism (irreparable conflict resulting in student expulsion or teacher resignation). Through detailed field observations across diverse high school classrooms, McFarland demonstrates that these micro-level interactional dramas leave lasting ad hoc narratives that structure future classroom encounters and dictate the long-term stability of educational authority.

## Passive vs. Active Resistance and Frame Contestation

### Defining Student Resistance in the Classroom

- **Symbolic Inversion of Normative Codes**: McFarland defines student resistance as an oppositional form of nonconformity in which students symbolically invert, distort, or undermine the pre-established cultural codes and rules of conduct within the school and classroom. Resistance is fundamentally a change-oriented encounter that commences drawn-out episodes of conflict spanning a succession of interactions.
- **Disrupting the Flow of Resources**: Rather than being isolated outbursts, resistant acts redirect classroom attention, shift the flow of instructional time, and demobilize student participation away from the teacher's intended academic tasks toward alternative social or personal agendas.

### Passive Resistance vs. Active Resistance

- **Passive Resistance (Diagnostic Framing)**: Passive resistance entails a tacit, indirect subversion of academic routines—such as quiet off-task socializing, doodling, daydreaming, or sluggish compliance. It functions as an expression of malcontent and critique (diagnostic framing), highlighting flaws in the current situation without openly challenging the teacher's authority or proposing an alternative structure.
- **Active Resistance (Prognostic Framing)**: Active resistance consists of an open, overt attempt to subvert teacher instruction, defy institutional rules, and alter the classroom situation. Resisters deploy prognostic framing by proposing an explicit alternative definition of the situation, attempting to rally the classroom audience and force a structural transformation of classroom affairs.

### Frame Contestation and Standpoint Mobilization

- **Attacking the Academic Frame**: Active resisters breach classroom order by adopting a non-academic interpretive standpoint (e.g., personal rights, peer solidarity, or racial fairness) and using that perspective to attack the teacher's academic framework.
- **Recruiting the Classroom Audience**: For an act of resistance to succeed in altering classroom affairs, the resister must work the classroom audience, seeking alignment and consensus among peers. If classmates validate the resister's ideological standpoint, the breach expands into a collective challenge; if peers reject or ignore the breach, the resistance implodes.

## The Four Stages of the Social Drama Model

McFarland conceptualizes serious classroom resistance as an extended social drama divided into two overarching phases—ceremonial deconstruction and ceremonial reconstruction—which unfold across four distinct stages.

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                   THE FOUR STAGES OF SOCIAL DRAMA                       │
│                                                                         │
│   [STAGE 1: BREACH]  ──►  [STAGE 2: CRISIS]  ──►  [STAGE 3: REDRESS]      │
│  (Student Contestation)   (Mounting Discontent)   (Teacher Countermoves)│
│                                                            │            │
│                                                            ▼            │
│                                                  [STAGE 4: REINTEGRATION]│
│                                                  (Reproduction/Negotiation/│
│                                                   Revolution/Schism)    │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Stage 1: The Breach (Ceremonial Deconstruction)

- **Initiating the Drama**: The breach occurs when a student or group of students engages in an overt act of nonconformity that ruptures the axiomatic rules of the classroom and demobilizes academic work.
- **Positing an Alternative Standpoint**: Resisters adopt aggressive definitional maneuvers, questioning teacher roles, challenging task relevance, and attempting to instantiate a new socio-cultural order grounded in personal, social, or political frames.

### Stage 2: The Crisis (Mounting Discontent & Liminality)

- **Escalation and Audience Alignment**: If the initial breach strikes a chord with the classroom audience, the conflict escalates into a public crisis. Bystanders take sides, latent grievances surface, and old interpersonal wounds are reopened.
- **The Liminal State**: In a full-blown crisis, the situation acquires liminal characteristics: participants drop their routine academic masks, the illusion that normal "schooling" is occurring breaks down, and regular instructional interaction fails completely.

### Stage 3: Redress (Contestation, Alignment, & Countermoves)

- **Containment and Counterattacks**: Representatives of authority—primarily teachers, but sometimes popular student leaders—perform redressive actions to contain the breach and dispel the crisis.
- **Opposing Framing Strategies**: Both parties engage in aggressive facework and framing maneuvers to win the audience:
    - _Amplify vs. Dampen_: Teachers amplify the importance of grades or task relevance, while resisters dampen their significance.
    - _Bridge vs. Detach_: Teachers bridge tasks to student interests or future goals, while resisters detach from the academic frame.
    - _Extend vs. Limit_: Teachers extend the scope of options to co-opt resisters, while resisters limit the teacher's authority.
    - _Transform vs. Reverse_: Teachers attempt to transform student attitudes, while resisters reverse arguments to accuse the teacher of unfairness or incompetence.

### Stage 4: Reintegration vs. Schism (Reconstructing Order)

- **Reaching a Final Resolution**: The drama concludes when participants establish a working agreement on how the classroom situation will proceed. Reintegration occurs through micro-rituals of acceptance, resulting in one of four distinct social outcomes:
    1. _Reproduction_: The teacher successfully re-establishes authority, the academic frame is restored, and the resister complies (often saving face through private grumbling or humor).
    2. _Negotiation_: The teacher and students trade concessions, modifying assignment rules, task pacing, or evaluation criteria to achieve a mutually acceptable compromise.
    3. _Social Revolution_: Student resistance completely overwhelms teacher authority, forcing the teacher to abandon the academic plan and adopt the social or behavioral norms dictated by the students.
    4. _Schism_: The drama ends in an irreparable breakdown rather than reintegration. Parties agree to exist in a permanent state of animosity, frequently accompanied by the forced role-exit (expulsion or transfer) of the student or the eventual resignation of the teacher.

## Empirical Exemplars of Classroom Social Dramas

McFarland illustrates the mechanics of the social drama framework through detailed ethnographic observations collected across multiple high school environments.

### Failed Crises and Imploded Breaches (Examples 1 & 2)

- **Example 1 (Imploded Breach)**: In a 10th-grade English class, a female student (Heather) loudly confronts the teacher over journal writing assignments ("What's the point?!"). Nearby male peers immediately tell her to "shut up," prompting Heather to strike one of them and drop her challenge to the teacher. Because Heather lacked peer support and did not represent the collective sentiment of the class, her breach failed to generate a crisis and imploded.
- **Example 2 (Crisis Management & Counter-Mobilization)**: In an accelerated trigonometry class, a popular student (Jocelyn) repeatedly interrupts a rigid teacher (Mr. M) to demand that he move past basic problems. Mr. M contains the public breach by threatening detention. Deprived of a public platform, Jocelyn conducts covert counter-mobilization, whispering to neighbors, mocking the teacher's mannerisms, and aligning grievances with peers to build support for future resistance.

### Collective Mobilization and Task Demise (Example 3)

- **Example 3 (Task Failure via Personal Frame)**: In a basic 10th-grade English class, a physically handicapped student (Chris) refuses to give a speech, stating, "I don't like talking in front of people." When the teacher attempts to coax him, Chris's classmates rally to his defense, asserting, "He shouldn't have to talk if he doesn't want to!" The collective adoption of the "personal rights" frame overwhelms the novice teacher's redressive capacity, forcing the complete demise of the lesson.

### Political Rebellion to Begrudging Compliance (Example 6)

- **Example 6 (Racial Framing and Hallway Debate)**: In a 12th-grade English class, an African American student (Tanya) slaps her book on her desk and refuses to read an assignment, pointing out that the author capitalizes "Puerto Rican" but leaves "black" in lowercase, accusing the author and task of racism. Her friend (Clarissa) joins the defiance. The African American female teacher (Ms. Washington) redresses the crisis by removing both students to the hallway. Ms. Washington invokes her own status as a Black woman to challenge their interpretation, co-opting Clarissa and isolating Tanya. Upon returning to the room, the girls begrudgingly comply with the task while maintaining private resentment, illustrating a reproduction outcome mediated by hallway redress.

### Escalation, Expulsion, and Irreparable Schism (Example 10)

- **Example 10 (The Case of Jefe and Mrs. M)**: In a low-track English class, a popular student (Jefe) repeatedly interrupts a substitute teacher (Mrs. M), calling the class "boring" and asserting he does not need college because he can work at his brother's nightclub. Classmates cheer Jefe on as he crumples his work and shoots trash baskets across the room. When Mrs. M demands he stay in his seat, Jefe defies her, stating, "I can get up or out whenever I want." Mrs. M issues a pink slip and expels Jefe from the room. Rather than restoring order, Jefe's expulsion turns him into a martyr; students taunt Mrs. M for being "mean" and "harsh," establishing an irreparable schism that eventually contributes to Mrs. M's resignation mid-semester.

## Key Theoretical Definitions

- **Social Drama of Resistance**: A change-oriented interactional process through which acts of student nonconformity disrupt classroom routines, initiating sequential phases of ceremonial deconstruction (breach and crisis) and ceremonial reconstruction (redress and reintegration).
- **Passive vs. Active Resistance**: _Passive resistance_ consists of tacit, indirect subversions of school rules that express diagnostic critique without disrupting academic affairs; _active resistance_ consists of open, overt challenges that deploy prognostic framing to demobilize tasks and alter classroom rules.
- **Frame Contestation**: The interactional strategy wherein resisters reject the dominant academic interpretation of a situation and promote an alternative interpretive standpoint (e.g., personal rights, peer social norms, or racial equality).
- **Breach**: The initial overt act of defiance or nonconformity that breaks established classroom rules and threatens to demobilize student participation in academic tasks.
- **Crisis**: The second stage of a social drama occurring when a breach resonates with the classroom audience, causing conflict to spread, latent grievances to surface, and normal instructional interaction to fail.
- **Liminality**: A transitional state during a classroom crisis wherein conventional academic roles, rules, and masks break down, exposing raw power dynamics and forcing key actors to reconstruct order.
- **Redress**: The third stage of a social drama in which teachers or student leaders perform countermoves and framing strategies (amplifying, bridging, extending, transforming) to contain a crisis and restore order.
- **Reintegration**: The final stage of a social drama in which teachers and students reach an explicit or tacit agreement on how classroom interaction will proceed, resulting in reproduction, negotiation, or social revolution.
- **Schism**: A non-reintegrated outcome of a social drama characterized by an unresolvable breakdown of authority, permanent mutual animosity, and frequently the forced role-exit of a student or teacher.

## Summary

Daniel A. McFarland's "Resistance as a Social Drama: A Study of Change-Oriented Encounters" provides a micro-sociological framework for understanding how student defiance shapes classroom order and educational authority. Moving beyond static models that treat student rebellion as random pathology or fixed structural determinism, McFarland demonstrates that classroom resistance unfolds as a dynamic, four-stage social drama: breach, crisis, redress, and reintegration (or schism). By analyzing how students move from diagnostic complaints to prognostic active resistance, McFarland shows how micro-interactional encounters can either reinforce or revolutionize the social structure of schooling.

Central to McFarland's analysis is the role of the classroom audience and framing strategies. For a student breach to escalate into a full-blown crisis, it must find resonance among peers, converting individual discontent into a collective contestation of the academic frame. In response, teachers deploy redressive countermoves—such as bridging tasks to student interests, extending options, or isolating ringleaders—to restore instructional momentum. When redressive efforts succeed, the classroom social order is reproduced or negotiated; when redressive efforts fail, the encounter results in student-led social revolutions or permanent, irreparable schisms.

Ultimately, McFarland highlights the enduring significance of these micro-level interactional dramas. Every classroom social drama leaves behind an ad hoc narrative that participants recall and invoke during future disputes. Whether resolved through begrudging compliance, negotiated compromises, or tragic expulsions, these change-oriented encounters establish agreed-upon pathways for conflict resolution, proving that classroom authority is continuously constructed, contested, and remade through the daily drama of student resistance.
