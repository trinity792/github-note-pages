# The Adolescent Culture (pp. 523–535)

## Executive Summary

James S. Coleman investigates the fundamental structural transformation of youth in post-World War II American society, examining how high schools concentrate adolescents into age-segregated environments removed from general adult society. Historically, parents and teachers exercised direct control over the "levers" of youth motivation because they directly supplied the primary social rewards of approval, status, and prestige. However, macro-sociological shifts—including extended compulsory schooling, changing family dynamics, altered labor market requirements, and the rise of youth-targeted mass media—have created an autonomous adolescent subculture. In contemporary society, teenagers look to one another rather than to adults for social validation, making traditional adult levers of motivation far less effective and giving rise to peer value systems that frequently operate in opposition to the academic goals of the educational system.

Drawing on empirical data from a multi-school survey of 6,289 students across nine public high schools (and a university laboratory school) in the late 1950s, Coleman analyzes student leisure patterns, gender-differentiated interests, and the specific criteria required to gain admission to the "leading crowd" or "inner circle" of high school social life. The findings reveal a stark divergence between the status hierarchies of boys and girls. For girls, social success dominates the peer status system: "having a good personality" (friendliness) is the single most cited criterion for joining the leading crowd, followed by physical attractiveness, neat/expensive clothes, and maintaining a good reputation. Scholastic achievement is cited less than 12 percent of the time (ranking eighth), while leadership in school activities accounts for only 6.5 percent. For boys, athletic prowess represents the single clearest, most direct pathway to the leading crowd, followed by personality, owning or having access to a car, and good looks, with academic success ranking fifth.

Coleman highlights a profound structural asymmetry in adolescent status criteria: girls are evaluated primarily on what they _are_—tangible and intangible personal attributes (personality, looks, family background) that are ascribed, external, and easily manipulated by the in-group—whereas boys are evaluated largely on what they _do_ (athletic performance and achievement). Furthermore, Coleman critiques the unexamined educational assumption that coeducation automatically promotes healthy social development. He demonstrates that coeducational high school environments intensify the "rating and dating" system, elevating the salience of physical beauty, cars, and clothes while systematically depressing the value of intellectual excellence. To reverse this institutional drift, Coleman argues that educators cannot break down adolescent society; instead, they must learn to understand and control the adolescent community _as a community_, restructuring peer reward systems so that social approval aligns with academic achievement.

## Structural Origins of the Autonomous Adolescent Subculture

### The Erosion of Traditional Adult Levers of Motivation

- **Historical Shift in Social Rewards**: In earlier historical periods, the socialization of youth occurred primarily within the family and local community, where parents and teachers directly controlled the rewards that motivated children. Because adults directly distributed social approval, educational theory did not need to explicitly account for peer dynamics.
- **Structural Segregation of Youth**: Modern industrial development, extended secondary schooling, changing family structures, and targeted mass media have cut adolescents off from adult society. High schools concentrate youth into crowded, age-segregated environments for prolonged periods, creating a distinct "small society" with its own internal norms, language, and status hierarchies.
- **Diminishing Adult Influence**: Because teenagers seek approval, admiration, and respect almost exclusively from their peers in everyday activities, traditional adult incentives—such as parental praise or teacher grades—have lost their efficacy. As a result, adult efforts to motivate students as isolated individuals routinely fail when peer norms discount academic success.

### Controlling the Adolescent Community as a System

- **Futility of Re-establishing Old Controls**: Coleman argues that attempting to break down adolescent subcultures and reinstate direct adult authority is practically impossible because it flies in the face of irreversible, large-scale socio-economic changes in work and community structure.
- **Systemic Educational Strategy**: The only viable solution for educational policy is to learn how to manipulate and channel the adolescent community _as a collective system_. Rather than viewing the student as an isolated "gifted" or "backward" child, educators must understand the structural mechanics of peer approval and align institutional rewards so that high academic performance earns status within the youth community.

## Leisure Activities and Gender Disparities in Youth Interests

### Divergent Time Allocations Between Boys and Girls

- **Boys' Active and Mechanical Pursuits**: Survey findings across nine public high schools reveal that boys prefer active outdoor pursuits, organized sports (22.0%), unorganized outdoor activities like hunting and fishing (14.7%), working on hobbies such as cars or bicycles (22.5%), watching television (19.4%), and hanging out with male peers (17.2%).
- **Girls' Person-Oriented and Expressive Pursuits**: Girls' leisure time centers on expressive, person-oriented, and home-based activities: reading (35.5%), "being with the group" or riding around (32.5%), listening to records or the radio (31.7%), watching television (23.6%), dancing among themselves (12.0%), and dating (11.6%).
- **Structural Availability of Activities**: Coleman notes that American society provides a rich, varied set of structured, active pursuits for boys (sports, mechanics, outdoors), whereas structured activities for girls are scarce. Consequently, when girls gather, they spend more time simply "being with the group" or "going up town" to window shop, making peer acceptance and clique membership far more intense and central to girls' daily lives.

## The "Leading Crowd" and Status Criteria in High Schools

### Determinants of Social Inclusion for Girls

- **The Dominance of Personality**: Across all nine public high schools, most students recognize the existence of a "leading crowd" or "top clique" that sets social norms and "runs everything." For girls, "having a good personality" or "being friendly and nice" is the single most cited criterion for entering the leading crowd, mentioned most frequently in seven of the nine schools.
- **External Beauty, Clothes, and Reputation**: Physical attractiveness ("good looks") ranks second, followed closely by wearing nice or expensive clothes. Maintaining a "good reputation" is the fourth most frequent requirement, trapping girls in a delicate dilemma between attracting male attention and avoiding social stigmatization for immoral behavior.
- **Family Background as Ascribed Capital**: Family background—expressed as "parents having money," "coming from the right neighborhood," or "wearing expensive clothes"—functions as an ascribed, non-negotiable criterion for girls. Girls from the "wrong side of the tracks" face severe structural barriers to breaking into the leading crowd.
- **Marginality of Academic Achievement**: Scholastic success ("being smart" or "getting good grades") is mentioned less than 12 percent of the time by girls (ranking eighth), far behind personality, looks, and clothes. Active participation in school clubs or cheerleading accounts for only 6.5 percent of responses.

### Determinants of Social Inclusion for Boys

- **Athletics as the Primary Pathway**: For boys, athletic achievement represents the clearest, most direct, and undisputed pathway to membership in the leading crowd. Football touchdowns and basketball prowess bestow unambiguous, objective status that cannot be denied by the in-group.
- **Cars and Independence**: Owning or having regular access to a car is a major status symbol for boys. In four out of five small-town high schools, having a car was cited more frequently than academic achievement as a requirement for the leading crowd.
- **Subordinate Status of Academics**: Academic achievement ranks fifth for boys—slightly higher than for girls, but still far below athletics and personality. In some male peer subcultures, being "too intelligent" or overly academic is viewed as a liability that actively pulls a boy away from the leading crowd.

## Coeducation, Boy-Girl Relations, and Psychological Consequences

### The Distortion of Institutional Academic Goals

- **Rating and Dating System**: Coleman challenges the popular assumption that coeducational high schools promote healthy social development. In coeducational settings, relations between boys and girls elevate the importance of physical attractiveness, cars, and clothes, while actively depressing the value of scholastic achievement.
- **Asymmetric Judging Standards**: A boy's popularity with girls depends heavily on having a car, good looks, and athletic prestige rather than academic excellence. Similarly, a girl's popularity with boys depends on physical beauty and an enticing manner rather than intelligence. Coeducation inadvertently reinforces superficial, hedonistic values over academic learning.

### Impact on Girls' Self-Evaluation and Mental Health

- **Cultivating Superficial Roles**: Because high school culture defines girls as objects of male attention, girls are encouraged to cultivate attributes suited for "models, chorus girls, or actresses" rather than citizens, mothers, or professionals.
- **Distorted Self-Concepts**: Sentence-completion tasks reveal that girls experience intense anxiety regarding physical appearance, figure, skin, and popularity. Girls who are not recognized by peers as "best dressed" are twice as likely to express a desire to "be someone different," demonstrating the psychological toll of superficial status criteria.

### Institutional Variation: The Laboratory School Exception

- **Overriding Superficial Values**: In School 10 (a university laboratory school serving upper-middle-class students within an intensely academic atmosphere), the proportion of girls citing "good looks" as crucial for the leading crowd dropped precipitously from freshman to senior year.
- **Climate vs. Maturation**: In contrast, across the nine typical public high schools, the emphasis on good looks remained high or increased through the sophomore and junior years as dating intensified. This contrast proves that local institutional climate—not biological maturation—shapes whether adolescent cultures prioritize superficial attractiveness or academic achievement.

## Key Theoretical Definitions

- **Adolescent Subculture**: An autonomous social world developed by teenagers in age-segregated settings, characterized by distinctive norms, values, status criteria, and language that often operate independently of or in opposition to adult authority.
- **Leading Crowd**: The dominant "inner circle" or elite clique within a high school that holds high social status, sets peer norms, controls access to social events, and determines what attributes are valued by the student body.
- **Ascribed vs. Achieved Peer Status**: _Ascribed status_ in peer cultures rests on attributes an individual cannot easily change (family wealth, neighborhood, ascribed reputation); _achieved status_ rests on direct personal accomplishments (football touchdowns, academic honors).
- **Rating and Dating System**: A competitive social hierarchy within coeducational high schools where peer prestige is gained through dating high-status partners, displaying physical attractiveness, and owning consumer goods like cars and fashionable clothing.
- **Intergenerational Closure**: The structural condition in which adults (parents and teachers) maintain interconnected relationships and shared norms, enabling them to effectively monitor, guide, and supply motivational "levers" to youth.

## Summary

James S. Coleman's "The Adolescent Culture" demonstrates that contemporary American high schools host an autonomous youth subculture that has largely detached itself from adult social control. Because structural transformations in post-WWII society concentrated adolescents into age-segregated educational institutions, teenagers look to peer approval rather than adult praise for social rewards. As a result, the traditional "levers" used by parents and teachers to motivate students have lost their force, giving way to peer-driven status systems that prioritize social and athletic success over academic performance.

Coleman's empirical findings across ten high schools reveal that adolescent status hierarchies are heavily gendered and structurally asymmetric. For girls, admission to the "leading crowd" depends on ascribed and personal attributes—personality, physical beauty, expensive clothes, and family background—while academic achievement is relegated to a marginal position (<12% mention). For boys, status is tied primarily to athletic achievement and cars, with academic success playing a secondary role. Coleman shows that coeducational high schools exacerbate these patterns by fostering a "rating and dating" culture that forces girls into superficial roles focused on physical attractiveness.

Ultimately, Coleman emphasizes that educators cannot dismantle adolescent subcultures or force a return to historical patterns of family control. Instead, educational policy must adopt a structural approach that seeks to manage and channel the adolescent community _as a system_. By understanding how peer status is awarded, school leaders can restructure institutional activities and competition so that social approval within the youth culture is tied directly to academic excellence, turning the power of peer influence toward educational ends.
