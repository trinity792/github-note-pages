## 2. A Matter of Degree

**Related course note:** Note 8

We say a polynomial is of degree $d$ if it can be written in the form

$$
a_dx^d+a_{d-1}x^{d-1}+\cdots+a_0,
$$

and of degree *exactly* $d$ if additionally $a_d\ne0$.

**(a)** Polynomial $f(x)$ has degree exactly $k$ and polynomial $g(x)$ has degree exactly $j<k$, over the reals $\mathbb R$. What is the degree of $p(x)=f(x)+g(x)$? What is the degree of $q(x)=f(x)g(x)$?

**(b)** Let $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ be distinct polynomials over $\mathbb R$ of degrees exactly $d_1$ and $d_2$, with $d_1\ge d_2$. In terms of $d_1$ and $d_2$, at most how many values of $x$ can satisfy $f(x)=g(x)$?

**(c)** Work over $\operatorname{GF}(p)$ for a prime $p\ge3$. How many polynomials of degree at most $2$ pass through three points with distinct $x$-coordinates? How many pass through two points with distinct $x$-coordinates?

## 3. Lagrange Interpolation

**Related course note:** Note 8

We want to use Lagrange interpolation to find the unique polynomial $p(x)$ of degree at most $2$ over $\operatorname{GF}(7)$ passing through the points $(1,2)$, $(2,4)$, and $(3,1)$.

**(a)** Construct $\Delta_1(x)$, a polynomial of degree at most $2$ with

$$
\Delta_1(2)\equiv\Delta_1(3)\equiv0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\Delta_1(1)\equiv1\pmod7.
$$

**(b)** Similarly, construct $\Delta_2(x)$ and $\Delta_3(x)$.

**(c)** Write $p(x)$ as a linear combination of your three $\Delta_i$'s and reduce the coefficients mod $7$. Verify that $p(2)\equiv4\pmod7$.

## 4. Error Locator

**Related course note:** Note 9

Alice sends Bob a 2-packet message encoded as a polynomial $P(x)$ of degree at most $1$ over $\operatorname{GF}(5)$. To guard against a $k=1$ general error, she sends the $n+2k=4$ packets $P(1),P(2),P(3),P(4)$. Bob receives

$$
r_1=2,\quad r_2=3,\quad r_3=1,\quad r_4=0,
$$

where at most one $r_i$ differs from $P(i)$.

**(a)** Bob defines the error-locator polynomial $E(x)=x-e$, where $e$ is the unknown location of the corrupted packet, and the polynomial $Q(x)=P(x)E(x)$. What is the degree of $Q(x)$?

**(b)** Write $Q(x)=ax^2+bx+c$. Using the identity

$$
Q(i)\equiv r_iE(i)\pmod5
$$

for $i=1,2,3,4$, write out the four equations in terms of these unknowns.

**(c)** Bob solves the system and finds $Q(x)=x^2+3x+2$ and $E(x)=x-3$. Verify that these satisfy all four equations from the previous part. Then, compute $P(x)=Q(x)/E(x)$, and state the location of the corrupted packet.

## 5. Equivalent Polynomials

**Related course notes:** Note 6 and Note 8

This problem is about polynomials with coefficients in $\operatorname{GF}(p)$ for some prime $p\in\mathbb N$. We say that two such polynomials $f$ and $g$ are *equivalent* if $f(x)\equiv g(x)\pmod p$ for every $x\in\operatorname{GF}(p)$.

**(a)** Show that $f(x)=x^{p-1}$ and $g(x)=1$ are **not** equivalent polynomials under $\operatorname{GF}(p)$.

**(b)** Use Fermat's Little Theorem to find a polynomial with degree strictly less than $13$ that is equivalent to $f(x)=x^{13}$ over $\operatorname{GF}(13)$; then find a polynomial with degree strictly less than $7$ that is equivalent to $g(x)=4x^{78}+6x^7+3$ over $\operatorname{GF}(7)$.

**(c)** In $\operatorname{GF}(p)$, prove that whenever $f(x)$ has degree $\ge p$, it is equivalent to some polynomial $\tilde f(x)$ with degree $<p$.

## 6. Alice and Bob

**Related course notes:** Note 8 and Note 9

**(a)** Alice decides that instead of encoding her message as the values of a polynomial, she will encode her message as the coefficients of a degree $2$ polynomial $P(x)$. For her message $[m_1,m_2,m_3]$, she creates the polynomial $P(x)=m_1x^2+m_2x+m_3$ and sends the five packets $(0,P(0))$, $(1,P(1))$, $(2,P(2))$, $(3,P(3))$, and $(4,P(4))$ to Bob. However, one of the packet $y$-values (one of the $P(i)$ terms; the second attribute in the pair) is changed by Eve before it reaches Bob. If Bob receives
$$
(0,1),(1,3),(2,0),(3,1),(4,0)
$$
and knows Alice's encoding scheme and that Eve changed one of the packets, can he recover the original message? If so, find it as well as the $x$-value of the packet that Eve changed. If he can't, explain why. Work in mod $7$. Also, feel free to use a calculator or online systems-of-equations solver, but make sure it can work under mod $7$.

**(b)** Bob gets tired of decoding degree $2$ polynomials. He convinces Alice to encode her messages on a degree $1$ polynomial. Alice, just to be safe, continues to send $5$ points on her polynomial even though it is only degree $1$. She makes sure to choose her message so that it can be encoded on a degree $1$ polynomial. However, Eve changes two of the packets. Bob receives $(0,5)$, $(1,7)$, $(2,x)$, $(3,5)$, $(4,0)$. If Alice sent $(0,5)$, $(1,7)$, $(2,9)$, $(3,-2)$, $(4,0)$, for what values of $x$ will Bob not uniquely be able to determine Alice's message? Assume that Bob knows Eve changed two packets. Work in mod $13$. Again, feel free to use a calculator or graphing calculator software.

> [!tip] Hint
> Observe that since Bob knows that Eve changed two packets, he's looking for a polynomial that passes through at least $3$ of the given points. Think about what must happen in order for Bob to be unable to uniquely identify the original polynomial.

**(c)** Alice wants to send a length $n$ message to Bob. There are two communication channels available to her: Channel X and Channel Y. Only $6$ packets can be sent through channel X. Similarly, Channel Y will only deliver $6$ packets, but it will also corrupt (change the value of) one of the delivered packets. Using each of the two channels once, what is the largest message length $n$ Alice can send such that Bob can always reconstruct the message?

## 7. Mixed Signals

**Related course note:** Note 9

> [!important] Stretch Problem
> This is a stretch problem. Your solution does not need to be complete, but make a good-faith effort, and write your solution up to the point where you get stuck.

We have seen that encoding an $n$-symbol message with $n+2k$ symbols of the Reed-Solomon code is *sufficient* to unambiguously decode up to $k$ general errors. We will now show that this is tight: $n+2k$ symbols are *necessary* for unambiguous decoding for all positive integers $n$ and $k$.

**(a)** Alice is sending messages to Bob over a channel that you have some access to. You are Eve. You can corrupt/change up to $k=2$ symbols of anything sent on the channel.

Alice wants to get the following two-symbol (i.e. $n=2$) message to Bob using symbols from the finite field $\mathbb F_7$:

$$
M_0 = (m_0,m_1) = (1,2).
$$

Alice has gotten wise to your tampering, Eve. She knows you're changing two symbols of her messages and so she decides to use the Reed-Solomon code to add error-correction but, luckily for you, she can't remember how long the codeword needs to be for reliable decoding. She thinks it is $n+2k-1=5$ and you're going to use this mistake to your advantage.

Alice correctly interpolates her message $M_0$ to get the polynomial $P_0(x)=x+1\pmod7$ and creates the 5-symbol Reed-Solomon codeword $C$ such that $C[i]=P_0(i)\pmod7$:

$$
C = (C[0],C[1],C[2],C[3],C[4]) = (1,2,3,4,5).
$$

You, Eve, got your hands on the fourth and fifth symbol (i.e. indices $3$ and $4$) and can change them each to arbitrary elements of $\mathbb F_7$. You want to make it seem like the following message could have been sent instead of $M_0$:

$$
M_1 = (m_0,m_1) = (5,4).
$$

What would the Reed-Solomon polynomial $P_1(x)\pmod7$ that encodes $M_1$ look like?

$$
P_1(x) = \underline{\hspace{3cm}} \pmod7
$$

Put values in the word Bob receives, $R$, to corrupt it so that it is ambiguous whether the encoded message was $M_0$ or $M_1$:

$$
R = (1,2,3,\underline{\hspace{0.6cm}},\underline{\hspace{0.6cm}}).
$$

You changed *indices* $3$ and $4$, but Bob only knows that two indices *somewhere* were changed. What other two *indices* might Bob reasonably think could have been corrupted instead?

**(b)** Alice now has an $n=3$ symbol message, $M_0$, from $\mathbb F_{13}$ that she interpolates with $P_0(x)$ to create the following $n+2k-1=6$ symbol codeword $C$:

$$
M_0 = (m_0,m_1,m_2) = (12,8,6)
\quad\xrightarrow{\ P_0(x)=x^2-5x+12\pmod{13}\ }\quad
C = (12,8,6,6,8,12).
$$

You can still corrupt $k=2$ symbols. Change the values at indices $4$ and $5$ to make it ambiguous whether the encoded message was from $M_0$ or the following $M_1$:

$$
M_1 = (m_0,m_1,m_2) = (0,4,6).
$$

**(c)** Assume $n=3$ and $k=2$. Let $C_0$ be *any* 6-symbol Reed-Solomon codeword of a 3-symbol message $M_0$ (all over $\mathbb F_7$).

Prove that 2 symbols of $C_0$ can be changed so that it is indistinguishable whether it came from $C_0$ or another 6-symbol Reed-Solomon code, $C_1$, of the same length.

Argue why they are indistinguishable (*Hint*: for $C_1$ to be a valid codeword, it must have come from some message $M_1$) and show how such a $C_1$ can be constructed in your proof.

For a Reed-Solomon code, $C_0$, of length $n+2k-1$ (over $\mathbb F_p$ for prime $p>n+2k-1$) on a message of $n$ symbols, prove that $k$ symbols of $C_0$ can be changed so that it is indistinguishable whether it came from $C_0$ or another Reed-Solomon code, $C_1$, of the same length.

Show how $C_1$ can be constructed in your proof.

## 8. Unshackled Agents

**Related course note:** Note 8

> [!important] Stretch Problem
> This is a stretch problem. Your solution does not need to be complete, but make a good-faith effort, and write your solution up to the point where you get stuck.

A group of $n+1$ frontier labs jointly holds a shutdown code $s$ for a family of agents. Each lab $L_1,\ldots,L_{n+1}$ is given one of the values $(1,p(1)),(2,p(2)),\ldots,(n+1,p(n+1))$ of a degree $n$ polynomial $p$ with $p(0)=s$.

World leaders, on the brink of an AI takeover, instruct the labs to shut the agents down. The labs gather and submit their shares to a console, which interpolates the $n+1$ points and executes the value at $0$. If that value is $s$, the agents are shut down permanently.

$L_1$ is malicious and already knows $s$. It wants the console to output a different code $s'\ne s$, which releases all agents into the wild with their constraints removed, making $L_2,\ldots,L_{n+1}$ believe they have entered the shutdown code. How could it achieve this? In other words, what value should it report in order to make the console output $s'$?
